# Introduction to Accounting

Instructor: Doan Thuy Duong (SAA). Assessment: attendance and homework 10%, progress tests 20% + 20%, final exam 50%. Textbook: Weygandt, Kimmel & Kieso, *Accounting Principles* 13e.

The slides mix US vocabulary (Weygandt: FASB, SEC, balance sheet, income statement) with international IAS 1 vocabulary (£m, statement of financial position, statement of profit or loss). Learn both names; they describe the same accounting.

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### What accounting does

Accounting **identifies**, **records** and **communicates** an organization's economic events to interested users.

| Activity | Meaning |
|---|---|
| identify | select the relevant economic events (a sale, a purchase, a loan; hiring a good manager is not a transaction) |
| record | a systematic, chronological record in money terms: **bookkeeping** |
| communicate | prepare financial statements, and analyse and interpret them |

Bookkeeping is only the recording step; accounting also involves deciding what to record and interpreting the results. Accountants both prepare and interpret reports.

### Users

**Internal users** (managers) are served by **managerial accounting**: is there cash for dividends, what price maximizes profit, can we afford pay rises, which product line is most profitable? Reports can be as detailed and frequent as management wants, with no external rules.

**External users** are served by **financial accounting**:

| User | Question |
|---|---|
| investors (owners) | is the company earning satisfactory income? how does it compare with competitors? |
| creditors (suppliers, banks) | can it pay its debts when due? |
| tax authorities | does it comply with tax law? |
| regulators | does it follow the rules? |
| customers | will it honour warranties? |
| labour unions | can it afford pay and benefit increases? |

The two most common external users are investors and creditors. External users can't ask for more information and need to compare companies, which is why financial accounting has standards.

### The financial statements

IAS 1 requires a full set of five:

| IAS 1 | US / Weygandt | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| statement of financial position | balance sheet | assets, liabilities, equity at a point in time |
| statement of profit or loss | income statement | revenue and expenses over a period |
| statement of changes in equity | statement of owner's equity | movements in equity over the period |
| statement of cash flows | statement of cash flows | cash in and out over the period |
| notes | note disclosure | what the numbers alone can't show |

**Statement of financial position**: a snapshot listing **assets** (owned or controlled) and **liabilities** (owed), showing net assets = assets − liabilities at the end of the (usually 12-month) period. Control, not legal title, decides: a leased van the business controls is its asset.

Format (XYZ Ltd at 31 December 20X8, £m):

| Assets | | Equity and liabilities | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-current assets**: property, plant and equipment; goodwill; investments | X | **Capital and reserves**: ordinary and preference share capital; share premium; reserves; retained earnings | X |
| **Current assets**: inventories; trade and other receivables; prepayments; cash and cash equivalents | X | **Non-current liabilities**: loan notes | X |
| | | **Current liabilities**: trade and other payables; short-term borrowings; tax payable | X |
| **Total assets** | X | **Total equity and liabilities** | X |

Total assets always equal total equity and liabilities (the accounting equation, [[02 - The Accounting Equation]]). **Current** means expected to be settled or realized within 12 months (or the operating cycle); comparing current assets with current liabilities shows whether bills can be paid.

**Statement of profit or loss**: revenue and expenses for the period, showing performance and the profit or loss made.

| Line | £m |
|---|---|
| Revenue | X |
| Cost of sales | (X) |
| **Gross profit** | X |
| Other operating income | X |
| Distribution costs | (X) |
| Administrative expenses | (X) |
| **Profit from operations** | X |
| Finance cost | (X) |
| Investment income | X |
| **Profit before tax** | X |
| Taxation | (X) |
| **Net profit/(loss) for the period** | X |

Gross profit tests the product, profit from operations the business before financing (the most comparable across companies), profit before tax includes financing, net profit is what is left for owners.

The balance sheet is a snapshot ("at 31 December"), the profit statement a flow ("for the year ended"), as with stocks and flows ([[Time-series Analysis/contents/01 - What is a Time Series|stock and flow variables]]). Profit for the period flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet.

### Regulation and ethics

Effective financial reporting depends on ethical behaviour. After Enron, WorldCom, HealthSouth and AIG, the US Congress passed the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** (SOX: CEO/CFO certification, auditor independence, audit oversight), because lost confidence in accounts raises the cost of capital for every company.

**GAAP** (generally accepted accounting principles) are standards that are generally accepted and universally practised; they say how to report economic events. Standard setters: **FASB** (US GAAP), **SEC** (US regulator of listed companies), **IASB** (IFRS, used in most of the world). Vietnam uses VAS, based on older IFRS, with a move to IFRS for large and listed entities.

### Qualitative characteristics

(Slide 17 has only a title; this follows the IASB Conceptual Framework.)

| Fundamental | Meaning |
|---|---|
| relevance | can make a difference to decisions (predictive or confirmatory value); **materiality**: omission or misstatement could influence a user |
| faithful representation | complete, neutral, free from error in process (not perfectly accurate: many figures are estimates) |

Enhancing: **comparability** (across companies and periods), **verifiability** (independent observers would agree), **timeliness**, **understandability**. Constraint: **cost** must be justified by the benefit. Relevance and faithful representation often conflict (current value of a unique factory vs its historical cost).

### Concepts and conventions

(Slide 18 lists these without explanation.)

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| fair presentation | statements faithfully represent transactions (IAS 1's overriding requirement) |
| going concern | the business will continue for the foreseeable future; otherwise assets are valued at forced-sale amounts |
| **accruals** | transactions are recognized when they occur, not when cash moves (the basis of [[05 - Adjusting Entries]]) |
| consistency | the same treatment for like items across periods; changes must be justified and disclosed |
| materiality and aggregation | material items shown separately, immaterial ones combined |
| offsetting | assets and liabilities, income and expenses, are not netted unless a standard requires or permits it |
| **business entity** | the business is separate from its owner (the owner's car isn't a business asset; money put in by the owner is capital) |
| historical cost | assets recorded at what was paid; objective but possibly out of date |

### Ethics framework

(Slides 19–20 are empty duplicates; this is Weygandt's framework.) (1) Recognize the ethical situation and issues; (2) identify the stakeholders and their responsibilities; (3) identify the alternatives, weigh their effects on stakeholders, and choose the most ethical. Typical pressures: earnings targets, management pressure, conflicts of interest, confidentiality. The IESBA Code's principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour.

## ✏️ Exercises

**1.** (Lecture DO IT!) True or false? (1) The three steps are identification, recording and communication. (2) Bookkeeping covers all steps. (3) Accountants prepare but don't interpret reports. (4) The two most common external users are investors and company officers. (5) Managerial accounting focuses on reports for internal users.

> [!example]- Solution
> (1) True. (2) False: bookkeeping is only recording. (3) False: they also interpret. (4) False: investors and creditors; officers are internal. (5) True.

**2.** Internal or external, and what does each want? (a) production manager; (b) a bank considering a loan; (c) the tax authority; (d) a shareholder deciding whether to sell; (e) the marketing director; (f) a trade union; (g) a supplier deciding on 60-day credit.

> [!example]- Solution
> Internal: (a) which products are profitable, unit costs; (e) pricing, return on advertising. External: (b) can the loan be repaid; (c) correct taxable profit; (d) satisfactory income vs alternatives; (f) affordability of pay rises; (g) will we be paid in 60 days. Internal users ask detailed, forward-looking questions and get what they ask for; external users rely on published summaries. The union is external: what counts is access to internal reports, not where members work.

**3.** Which statement and where? (a) cash at bank; (b) sales revenue; (c) retained earnings; (d) cost of sales; (e) a bank loan repayable in 5 years; (f) trade payables; (g) tax charge; (h) property, plant and equipment.

> [!example]- Solution
> Statement of financial position: (a) current assets; (c) capital and reserves; (e) non-current liabilities; (f) current liabilities; (h) non-current assets. Statement of profit or loss: (b) top line; (d) deducted to give gross profit; (g) deducted from profit before tax.
> Retained earnings links the statements: closing = opening + profit − dividends. The loan is non-current because of its term; in its final year it moves to current liabilities.

**4.** Which concept applies, and what is the treatment? (a) A sole trader pays a family holiday from the business account. (b) A switch from FIFO to weighted average raises profit by 12%. (c) A contract signed in December, goods delivered in February, cash in March: when is revenue recognized? (d) A factory bought for £2m in 1995 is worth £15m. (e) A company owes a supplier £800,000 and is owed £750,000 by it, and reports a net payable of £50,000. (f) The directors will liquidate in three months.

> [!example]- Solution
> (a) Business entity: drawings (reduce capital), not an expense.
> (b) Consistency: a change needs justification (better information, not better profit), disclosure and restated comparatives.
> (c) Accruals: February, when goods are delivered and control passes; cash timing doesn't matter.
> (d) Historical cost: £2m less depreciation under the cost model. IFRS allows revaluation (£15m, with £13m to a revaluation reserve), applied to the whole asset class; more relevant, less verifiable.
> (e) Offsetting: report the £800,000 payable and £750,000 receivable gross; netting hides exposure (if the supplier fails, the receivable is lost but the payable remains).
> (f) Going concern fails: break-up basis, assets at realizable values, liabilities current, closure provisions, disclosed.

**5.** Retailer, year to 31 December 20X8 (£000): revenue 4,200; cost of sales 2,520; distribution 410; administration 630; rental income from a sublet 95; loan interest 140; dividends received 60; tax 130. (a) Statement of profit or loss; (b) gross and operating margins; (c) which line shows capacity for more debt?

> [!example]- Solution
> (a) Gross profit 1,680; + other operating income 95 − 410 − 630 = profit from operations 735; − finance cost 140 + investment income 60 = profit before tax 655; − tax 130 = net profit 525. Rent is operating income (above the line); dividends are investment income (below), so the operating line measures the trade.
> (b) Gross margin 1,680/4,200 = 40.0%; operating margin 735/4,200 = 17.5%.
> (c) Profit from operations, before interest: interest cover 735/140 = 5.25×. Net profit already deducts interest. Doubling interest to 280 would still leave cover of 2.6×. But profit isn't cash, and one year isn't a trend.

## 📝 Summary

- Accounting identifies, records and communicates; bookkeeping is only the recording.
- Internal users: managerial accounting; external users (mainly investors and creditors): financial accounting, standardized.
- IAS 1's five statements and their US names.
- Balance sheet: a snapshot, current vs non-current, assets = equity + liabilities.
- Profit statement: a flow with gross profit → profit from operations → profit before tax → net profit.
- GAAP by FASB (overseen by the SEC); IFRS by the IASB; SOX after the scandals.
- Concepts: fair presentation, going concern, accruals, consistency, materiality, offsetting, business entity, historical cost.
- Useful information is relevant and faithfully represented, plus comparable, verifiable, timely, understandable, subject to cost.
