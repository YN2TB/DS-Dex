## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. What accounting is for

**Accounting consists of three basic activities.** It

1. **identifies**,
2. **records**, and
3. **communicates**

the economic events of an organisation to interested users.

| Activity | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **Identify** | Select the economic events relevant to the business — a sale, a purchase, a loan. Not every event qualifies: hiring a well-regarded manager may matter commercially, but it is not an accounting transaction. |
| **Record** | Keep a systematic, chronological diary of those events, measured in money. This is the **bookkeeping** function. |
| **Communicate** | Aggregate the records into **financial statements** and *analyse and interpret* them for users. |

> [!important] Accounting ≠ bookkeeping
> **The accounting process *includes* the bookkeeping function, but bookkeeping is only the recording step.** Bookkeeping is mechanical: write down what happened. Accounting adds the judgement at both ends — deciding *what* to record (identification) and explaining *what it means* (communication and interpretation).
>
> This distinction is tested directly in the chapter's DO IT! exercise, and it is the single most common early misconception.

**Accountants both prepare *and* interpret financial reports.** Interpretation is not somebody else's job — it is the third activity.

### 2. Who uses accounting information

The fundamental split is by **whether the user is inside the organisation**, and it drives everything about how the information is prepared.

#### Internal users — managerial accounting

Managers inside the business, asking operational questions:

- *Is cash sufficient to pay dividends to shareholders?*
- *What price should we charge for our product to maximise profit?*
- *Can we afford to give employees pay raises this year?*
- *Which product line is the most profitable?*

**Managerial accounting activities focus on reports for internal users.** These reports can be as detailed, as frequent, and as forward-looking as management wants — there is no external rulebook.

#### External users — financial accounting

People outside the business, with no access to its internal records:

| User | Typical question |
|---|---|
| **Investors** (owners) | Is the company earning satisfactory income? How does it compare with competitors? |
| **Creditors** (suppliers, banks) | Will the company be able to pay its debts as they come due? |
| Taxing authorities | Does the company comply with tax laws? |
| Regulatory agencies | Is the company operating within prescribed rules? |
| Customers | Will the company continue to honour product warranties? |
| Labour unions | Can the company afford pay and benefit increases? |

**The two most common types of external users are investors and creditors** — not "investors and company officers", since company officers are *internal*.

> [!important] Why the internal/external split matters so much
> External users **cannot demand more information**. They see only what the company publishes, and they must be able to compare that against other companies. That is precisely why **standards exist** — the subject of §4. Internal reporting needs no standards because the user can simply ask for whatever they want.
>
> This one idea explains why financial accounting is so heavily regulated and managerial accounting is not.

### 3. The financial statements

#### The full set under IAS 1

According to **IAS 1 *Presentation of Financial Statements***, a full set of financial statements comprises:

1. **Statement of financial position**
2. **Statement of profit or loss**
3. **Statement of changes in equity**
4. **Statement of cash flows**
5. **Notes to the financial statements**

> [!note] The naming glossary — learn both columns
> | International (IAS 1) | US / Weygandt | What it shows |
> |---|---|---|
> | Statement of financial position | **Balance Sheet** | Assets, liabilities and equity **at a point in time** |
> | Statement of profit or loss | **Income Statement** | Revenue and expenses **over a period** |
> | Statement of changes in equity | **Statement of Owner's Equity** | How equity moved over the period |
> | Statement of cash flows | Statement of Cash Flows | Cash in and out **over a period** |
> | Notes | Note Disclosure | Everything the numbers alone cannot convey |
>
> The slides use both sets of names, sometimes on adjacent slides.

#### Statement of financial position (balance sheet)

**A list of the assets and liabilities of the business — a snapshot at a particular point in time.**

- **Assets** are items the business **owns (or controls)**.
- **Liabilities** are items the business **owes**.

Its purpose is to show the **net assets** of the business at the end of each accounting period:

$$
\text{Net assets} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}
$$

normally at the end of a 12-month period of account set by the business.

> [!tip] "Owns **or controls**" is doing real work
> A leased delivery van may be legally owned by the leasing company, yet the business controls it, bears its risks, and enjoys its benefits — so it appears as an asset. **Control, not legal title, is the modern test.** This is why leases now appear on the balance sheet under IFRS 16, and it is a good early illustration that accounting follows economic substance rather than legal form.

**Standard format** (XYZ Ltd at 31 December 20X8, £m — `X` stands for a number):

|---|---|---|
| **Non-current assets** | | |
| Property, plant and equipment | X | |
| Goodwill | X | |
| Investments | X | |
| | | **X** |
| **Current assets** | | |
| Inventories | X | |
| Trade and other receivables | X | |
| Prepayments | X | |
| Cash and cash equivalents | X | |
| | | **X** |
| **Total assets** | | **X** |

| Equity and liabilities | £m | £m |
|---|---|---|
| **Capital and reserves** | | |
| Ordinary share capital | X | |
| Preference share capital | X | |
| Share premium account | X | |
| Reserves | X | |
| Retained earnings | X | |
| | | **X** |

> [!important] Two structural features to notice immediately
> **1. Total assets = Total equity and liabilities.** Always, exactly. This is the **accounting equation**, and it is why the statement is called a *balance* sheet. [[02 - The Accounting Equation]] is entirely about why this identity can never fail.
>
> **2. Everything is split current vs non-current.** **Current** means expected to be settled or realised within **12 months** (or within the normal operating cycle); **non-current** is everything else. The split exists because a user comparing current assets against current liabilities can judge whether the business can pay its bills — the single most important question a creditor asks.

#### Statement of profit or loss (income statement)

**Shows the revenue and the expenses of a business for the accounting period** (normally 12 months). It shows **business performance over that period of time**, and its purpose is to show **the amount of profit or loss made during the last accounting period.**

**Standard format** (XYZ Ltd, year ended 31 December 20X8, £m — brackets mean a deduction):

| | £m |
|---|---|
| Revenue | X |
| Cost of sales | (X) |
| **Gross profit** | **X** |
| Other operating income | X |
| Distribution costs | (X) |
| Administrative expenses | (X) |
| **Profit from operations** | **X** |
| Finance cost | (X) |
| Investment income | X |
| **Profit before tax** | **X** |
| Taxation | (X) |
| **Net profit/(loss) for the period** | **X/(X)** |

> [!tip] Read it as four nested profit measures
> Each subtotal answers a different question, and analysts use them for different purposes:
>
> | Measure | Excludes | Answers |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Gross profit** | only the direct cost of goods sold | Is the product itself profitable? |
> | **Profit from operations** | + overheads and distribution | Is the *business* profitable, ignoring how it is financed? |
> | **Profit before tax** | + interest and investment income | Profitable after financing costs? |
> | **Net profit** | + tax | What is actually left for the owners? |
>
> Profit from operations is the most comparable across companies, because it is unaffected by how much debt a company happens to carry or what tax regime it sits in.

#### Point in time vs period of time

> [!important] The single most important structural distinction in accounting
> - The **statement of financial position** is a **snapshot** — "at 31 December 20X8". Take a photograph.
> - The **statement of profit or loss** is a **flow** — "for the year ended 31 December 20X8". Film a movie.
>
> The same distinction as **stock vs flow** in economics and time-series analysis (see [[Time-series Analysis/contents/01 - What is a Time Series|stock and flow variables]]). It is why you can meaningfully ask "how much cash *do* we have?" but must ask "how much profit did we *make over* the year?"
>
> The two statements are linked: **profit for the period flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet.** That link is the subject of [[02 - The Accounting Equation]].

### 4. Regulation, standards and ethics

#### Ethics in financial reporting

**Effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behaviour.**

Recent financial scandals include **Enron, WorldCom, HealthSouth, AIG** and other companies. Regulators and lawmakers were concerned that the economy would suffer if investors lost confidence in corporate accounting. In response, **Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (SOX)**.

> [!note] Why accounting scandals matter more than the fraud itself
> The direct losses in Enron were large, but the systemic worry was different: if investors cannot trust *any* published accounts, they demand a higher return from *every* company, and the cost of capital rises across the whole economy. **Accounting standards are infrastructure** — their value lies in being trusted, and a few high-profile failures damage everyone.
>
> SOX responded by requiring CEOs and CFOs to personally certify the accounts, strengthening auditor independence, and creating oversight of the audit profession.

#### Generally Accepted Accounting Principles

Various users need financial information, and the accounting profession has developed **standards that are generally accepted and universally practised**.

**GAAP — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles:** standards that are generally accepted and universally practised. **These standards indicate how to report economic events.**

**Standard-setting bodies:**

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **FASB** — Financial Accounting Standards Board | Sets US GAAP |
| **SEC** — Securities and Exchange Commission | US regulator; oversees listed companies and can enforce standards |
| **IASB** — International Accounting Standards Board | Sets IFRS, used across the EU, UK, Vietnam-adjacent jurisdictions and most of the world |

> [!tip] Which framework does Vietnam use?
> Vietnam uses **VAS** (Vietnamese Accounting Standards), historically based on older IFRS, with a transition toward full IFRS underway for large and listed entities. **This course teaches the international/US concepts** that VAS is built on. If an exam question specifies a jurisdiction, follow it; otherwise the IAS 1 formats above are the safe default given the slides' £m presentation.

### 5. Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information

> [!warning] This slide is **title-only** in the source
> Slide 17 gives the heading "4. Qualitative characteristics of useful financial information" with **no content** — the material was presumably an image or delivered verbally. The following is reconstructed from the **IASB Conceptual Framework**, which is the standard source and matches the course's IAS 1 orientation. **Verify against the lecturer's own material before the exam.**

The Conceptual Framework distinguishes two tiers.

**Fundamental characteristics** — without these, information is not useful at all:

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Relevance** | Capable of making a difference to a decision — it has *predictive value*, *confirmatory value*, or both. **Materiality** is entity-specific relevance: information is material if omitting or misstating it could influence a user's decision. |
| **Faithful representation** | Depicts the economic substance completely, neutrally, and free from error. (Note: "free from error" means no errors in the process and description — **not** perfect accuracy, since many figures are estimates.) |

**Enhancing characteristics** — these make useful information *more* useful:

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Comparability** | Users can identify similarities and differences between items — across companies and across periods |
| **Verifiability** | Independent, knowledgeable observers could reach consensus that the depiction is faithful |
| **Timeliness** | Available in time to influence decisions — old information loses relevance |
| **Understandability** | Classified, characterised and presented clearly (assuming a reasonably diligent user) |

**Constraint:** **cost**. The benefit of reporting information must justify the cost of providing it.

> [!note] Relevance and faithful representation often conflict
> A current market valuation of a unique factory is highly **relevant** but hard to represent **faithfully** (nobody knows the true value). Its historical cost is faithfully representable but may be decades out of date and barely relevant. **Most accounting controversies are a trade-off between these two**, and knowing that framing makes exam discussion questions much easier to answer.

### 6. Accounting concepts and conventions

> [!warning] The source slide lists these **without explanation**
> Slide 18 gives eight bullet points and no definitions. The explanations below are reconstructed from standard IAS 1 / conceptual framework material.

| Concept | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Fair presentation** | Financial statements must faithfully represent the effects of transactions. Applying IFRS with additional disclosure where needed is presumed to achieve fair presentation. | The overriding requirement of IAS 1 |
| **Going concern** | Statements are prepared on the assumption that the entity will continue in operation for the foreseeable future. | If a business is closing down, assets must be valued at what they would fetch in a forced sale, not at cost. Auditors flag going-concern doubt prominently. |
| **Accruals** | Transactions are recognised **when they occur**, not when cash is received or paid. | **The most important concept in this course.** A sale on credit is revenue *today*, even though cash arrives next month. [[05 - Adjusting Entries]] exists entirely to implement this. |
| **Consistency** | The same accounting treatment is applied to like items within and across periods. | Without it, comparability collapses — you could raise profit every year just by changing method. Changes must be disclosed and justified. |
| **Materiality and aggregation** | Material items are presented separately; immaterial items may be aggregated. | You do not need a separate line for a £5 stapler. Materiality is judged by whether omission could influence a user's decision. |
| **Offsetting** | Assets and liabilities, and income and expenses, must **not** be offset unless a standard requires or permits it. | Netting a £1m receivable against a £1m payable would hide £2m of gross exposure. Reporting both preserves information. |
| **Business entity concept** | The business is treated as **separate from its owner(s)**. | The owner's personal car is not a business asset. This is why the owner putting money in creates *capital* (a liability of the business to the owner), which drives the whole accounting equation. |
| **Historical cost** | Assets are normally recorded at the amount paid for them. | Objective and verifiable, but potentially outdated — the classic relevance-vs-faithful-representation trade-off. Some assets are later remeasured to fair value. |

> [!important] The two concepts that generate the most exam marks
> **Accruals** and the **business entity concept**. Nearly every adjusting entry in [[05 - Adjusting Entries]] exists because of accruals, and nearly every confusion about capital and drawings dissolves once the business entity concept is applied.

### 7. Ethical considerations

> [!warning] Slides 19 and 20 are **both title-only and duplicated**
> The deck ends with two slides headed "6. Ethical considerations" and "7. Ethical considerations" — a numbering error as well as an omission — with **no content on either**. The following is standard material, not from the source.

**The ethical framework used in the Weygandt textbook** — three steps for any ethics case:

1. **Recognise the ethical situation and the ethical issues involved.** Use your personal ethics to identify ethical situations and issues; some businesses and professional organisations provide written codes of ethics for guidance.
2. **Identify and analyse the principal elements in the situation.** Identify the stakeholders — persons or groups who may be harmed or benefited. Ask: what are the responsibilities and obligations of the parties involved?
3. **Identify the alternatives and weigh the impact of each on various stakeholders.** Select the most ethical alternative, considering all consequences. Sometimes there will be one right answer; other situations involve more than one right solution, requiring an evaluation of each and a selection of the best alternative.

**Recurring pressures on accountants:** meeting earnings targets, pressure from management to "adjust" figures, conflicts of interest, confidentiality, and the tension between serving the employer and serving the public interest.

**The fundamental principles** of the IESBA Code (integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour) are the standard reference point and are worth memorising.

## ✏️ Exercises

### Exercise 1 — True or false

*(The lecture's own DO IT! exercise.)* Indicate whether each statement is true or false.

1. The three steps in the accounting process are identification, recording, and communication.
2. Bookkeeping encompasses all steps in the accounting process.
3. Accountants prepare, but do not interpret, financial reports.
4. The two most common types of external users are investors and company officers.
5. Managerial accounting activities focus on reports for internal users.

> [!example]- Solution
> 1. **True.** Identify → record → communicate. Memorise the order; it reflects the actual sequence of work.
> 2. **False.** Bookkeeping covers **only the recording** step. Accounting also includes identification (deciding what qualifies as a transaction) and communication (preparing and *interpreting* the statements). This is the distinction most often tested.
> 3. **False.** Accountants prepare **and interpret**. Interpretation — explaining the meaning, uses and limitations of the reported data — is part of the communication activity.
> 4. **False.** Investors and **creditors**. Company officers are **internal** users; the question is a trap on exactly that word.
> 5. **True.** By definition — managerial accounting serves users inside the organisation, which is why it needs no external standards.

### Exercise 2 — Classify the users

For each user, state whether they are internal or external, and give the primary question they want the accounts to answer.

(a) The production manager · (b) A bank considering a loan · (c) The Vietnamese tax authority · (d) A shareholder deciding whether to sell · (e) The marketing director · (f) A trade union negotiating pay · (g) A supplier deciding whether to offer 60-day credit

> [!example]- Solution
> | | User | Type | Primary question |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | (a) | Production manager | **Internal** | Which product line is most profitable? Are unit costs rising? |
> | (b) | Bank | **External** (creditor) | Can the company service and repay this loan? |
> | (c) | Tax authority | **External** | Has taxable profit been correctly computed and tax paid? |
> | (d) | Shareholder | **External** (investor) | Is the company earning satisfactory income, and how does it compare with alternatives? |
> | (e) | Marketing director | **Internal** | What price maximises profit? Is the advertising spend returning value? |
> | (f) | Trade union | **External** | Can the company afford pay and benefit increases? |
> | (g) | Supplier | **External** (creditor) | Will we be paid in 60 days? |
>
> **The pattern to extract.** Internal users ask **forward-looking, operational, disaggregated** questions and can get whatever detail they request. External users ask **backward-looking, summary, comparative** questions and must accept what is published.
>
> **Note (f) is a genuine edge case worth thinking about.** A trade union represents employees who work *inside* the company — but the union has no right of access to internal management reports and relies on published statements. **The test is access, not physical location.** Employees are conventionally classified as external users of financial statements.

### Exercise 3 — Which statement, and where?

For each item, name the financial statement it appears in and its position within that statement.

(a) Cash at bank · (b) Revenue from sales · (c) Retained earnings · (d) Cost of sales · (e) A bank loan repayable in 5 years · (f) Trade payables · (g) Taxation charge for the year · (h) Property, plant and equipment

> [!example]- Solution
> | | Item | Statement | Position |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | (a) | Cash at bank | Statement of financial position | **Current assets** — "Cash and cash equivalents" |
> | (b) | Revenue | Statement of profit or loss | **Top line** |
> | (c) | Retained earnings | Statement of financial position | **Capital and reserves** (equity) |
> | (d) | Cost of sales | Statement of profit or loss | Deducted from revenue to give **gross profit** |
> | (e) | 5-year bank loan | Statement of financial position | **Non-current liabilities** — "Loan notes" |
> | (f) | Trade payables | Statement of financial position | **Current liabilities** |
> | (g) | Taxation | Statement of profit or loss | Deducted from **profit before tax** |
> | (h) | Property, plant and equipment | Statement of financial position | **Non-current assets** — first line |
>
> **Two things worth noticing:**
>
> **(c) Retained earnings is the bridge between the two statements.** It is a balance-sheet item, but its *movement* over the year is the profit from the statement of profit or loss (less any dividends). Formally: closing retained earnings = opening retained earnings + profit for the period − dividends. **This is why the two statements cannot be prepared independently** — see [[02 - The Accounting Equation]].
>
> **(e) The five-year term is what makes it non-current.** Had the same loan been repayable within 12 months it would sit in current liabilities. **The classification depends on timing, not on the nature of the item** — and a long-term loan entering its final year *migrates* to current liabilities, which can make a balance sheet look suddenly much weaker without anything having actually changed.

### Exercise 4 — Apply the concepts

Identify which accounting concept each situation involves, and state the correct treatment.

(a) The owner of a sole trader uses the business bank account to pay for a family holiday.
(b) A company changes from valuing inventory at FIFO to weighted average, raising this year's profit by 12%.
(c) A business signs a contract in December to deliver goods in February; cash will be received in March. When is revenue recognised?
(d) A factory bought for £2m in 1995 is now worth £15m. At what value does it appear?
(e) A company owes a supplier £800,000 and is separately owed £750,000 by the same supplier. It reports a net payable of £50,000.
(f) The directors have decided to liquidate the company in three months.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Business entity concept.** The business is separate from its owner. The holiday is **not a business expense** — it is **drawings**, a reduction of the owner's capital. Treating it as an expense would understate profit and overstate the owner's remaining claim on the business. Every sole-trader question about personal spending is testing this.
>
> **(b) Consistency.** Changing method is not forbidden, but it breaks comparability with prior years. The change must be **justified** (the new method must give more relevant or reliable information — *not* merely a better profit figure), **disclosed**, and prior-period figures **restated** so users can compare like with like. A 12% profit increase arising purely from a method change is exactly the manipulation consistency is designed to expose.
>
> **(c) Accruals.** Revenue is recognised **in February**, when the goods are delivered and control passes — not in December (contract signed, nothing performed) and not in March (cash received). **Cash timing is irrelevant to profit measurement.** In February, revenue is recorded with a corresponding trade receivable, which converts to cash in March with no further effect on profit.
>
> **(d) Historical cost.** It appears at **£2m** (less accumulated depreciation), not £15m — under the cost model. This is deliberately conservative and verifiable, but it means the balance sheet can badly understate a company's real worth.
>
> Note the tension: the £15m figure is far more **relevant** to a user, but far less **faithfully representable**. IFRS permits a **revaluation model** for property, plant and equipment as an alternative, in which case £15m would be used with the £13m uplift going to a revaluation reserve in equity. **The choice must then be applied consistently to the whole class of assets.**
>
> **(e) Offsetting.** This is **not permitted** unless a standard requires or permits it (which generally needs a legally enforceable right of set-off *and* an intention to settle net). The company must report the **£800,000 payable and the £750,000 receivable gross**. Netting hides £1.55m of gross exposure — and if the supplier fails, the company still owes its £800,000 while its £750,000 receivable becomes worthless. **The gross figures carry information the net figure destroys.**
>
> **(f) Going concern — and its failure.** The going-concern assumption **no longer applies**. The statements must be prepared on a **break-up basis**: assets restated to their likely realisable amounts in a forced sale (usually far below carrying value), all liabilities reclassified as current, and provisions made for closure costs. The basis of preparation must be prominently disclosed. **This is the one concept whose failure changes every number on the face of the accounts.**

### Exercise 5 — Build a statement of profit or loss

A retailer reports for the year ended 31 December 20X8 (£000): revenue 4,200; cost of sales 2,520; distribution costs 410; administrative expenses 630; rental income from a sublet unit 95; interest paid on a bank loan 140; dividends received from investments 60; tax charge 130.

(a) Prepare the statement of profit or loss in IAS 1 format. (b) Compute the gross margin and the operating margin. (c) The bank asks whether the business could service more debt — which line do you point to, and why?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)**
>
> | | £000 |
> |---|---|
> | Revenue | 4,200 |
> | Cost of sales | (2,520) |
> | **Gross profit** | **1,680** |
> | Other operating income | 95 |
> | Distribution costs | (410) |
> | Administrative expenses | (630) |
> | **Profit from operations** | **735** |
> | Finance cost | (140) |
> | Investment income | 60 |
> | **Profit before tax** | **655** |
> | Taxation | (130) |
> | **Net profit for the period** | **525** |
>
> Check: $1{,}680+95-410-630 = 735$; $735-140+60 = 655$; $655-130 = 525$. ✓
>
> **Note the classification decisions:**
> - **Rental income (95)** is *other operating income* — it arises from operating an asset, so it sits above profit from operations.
> - **Dividends received (60)** are *investment income* — a return on financial investment, not operations, so they sit **below** profit from operations.
>
> This is not arbitrary. Placing both above the operating line would inflate the apparent profitability of the retail business itself. **The operating line is meant to isolate performance of the trade.**
>
> **(b)**
> $$\text{Gross margin} = \frac{1{,}680}{4{,}200} = \mathbf{40.0\%}
> \qquad
> \text{Operating margin} = \frac{735}{4{,}200} = \mathbf{17.5\%}$$
> A 40% gross margin says each £100 of sales leaves £40 after the direct cost of goods. The drop to 17.5% shows overheads (£1,040 of distribution and admin) consuming more than half of that.
>
> **(c) Point to profit from operations: £735k.**
>
> **Why that line and not net profit.** Debt-service capacity depends on earnings generated **before** financing costs, because the question is precisely "how much finance cost could this business bear?" Net profit of £525k is already *after* £140k of interest — using it would double-count the existing debt burden.
>
> The standard measure is **interest cover**:
> $$\text{Interest cover} = \frac{\text{Profit from operations}}{\text{Finance cost}} = \frac{735}{140} = \mathbf{5.25\times}$$
> Operating profit covers current interest 5.25 times over. Bank covenants commonly require 3× or 4×, so on this measure there is comfortable headroom — the business could roughly *double* its interest cost and still cover it 2.6 times.
>
> **Two caveats a careful analyst would add.** First, **profit is not cash** — see [[08 - Accounting for Receivables]]; a business can be profitable and still unable to pay interest if its cash is locked in receivables and inventory. Second, this is one year; a lender would want the trend and the balance sheet alongside it.

## 📝 Summary

- **Accounting identifies, records and communicates** the economic events of an organisation to interested users. **Bookkeeping is only the recording step** — accounting also involves judgement about what to record and interpretation of what it means.
- **Internal users** (managers) get managerial accounting: detailed, frequent, forward-looking, unregulated. **External users** — most commonly **investors and creditors** — get financial accounting: standardised and comparable, because they cannot demand more.
- **IAS 1 requires five statements:** statement of financial position, statement of profit or loss, statement of changes in equity, statement of cash flows, and notes. The US names are Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Statement of Owner's Equity, Statement of Cash Flows, Note Disclosure.
- **The statement of financial position is a snapshot** ("at 31 December"), listing assets (owned or **controlled**) and liabilities (owed), split **current** (within 12 months) and **non-current**. **Total assets always equals total equity and liabilities.**
- **The statement of profit or loss is a flow** ("for the year ended"), and gives four nested measures: **gross profit** → **profit from operations** → **profit before tax** → **net profit**. Profit from operations is the most comparable across companies.
- **GAAP** are standards that are generally accepted and universally practised, set by the **FASB** (US), overseen by the **SEC**, and internationally by the **IASB** (IFRS).
- **Effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behaviour.** Enron, WorldCom, HealthSouth and AIG prompted the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act**, because loss of confidence in accounting raises the cost of capital for everyone.
- **Eight concepts govern preparation:** fair presentation, going concern, **accruals**, consistency, materiality and aggregation, offsetting, **business entity**, and historical cost. Accruals and business entity generate the most exam marks.
- **Useful information is relevant and faithfully represented** (fundamental), and comparable, verifiable, timely and understandable (enhancing), subject to a **cost** constraint. Relevance and faithful representation frequently trade off against each other.

## ⚠️ Important Notes

> [!warning] Bookkeeping vs accounting — the classic trap
> Bookkeeping = **recording only**. Accounting = identify + record + communicate (including **interpretation**). Any exam statement saying bookkeeping "encompasses all steps", or that accountants "do not interpret", is **false**.

> [!warning] Company officers are internal, not external
> The two most common **external** users are **investors and creditors**. Officers, managers, and directors are internal. This exact distractor appears in the lecture's own DO IT! exercise.

> [!tip] Point in time vs period of time — check the heading
> Every financial statement heading tells you which it is:
> - "**at** 31 December 20X8" → a snapshot → statement of financial position
> - "**for the year ended** 31 December 20X8" → a flow → profit or loss, changes in equity, cash flows
>
> If you are unsure where an item belongs, ask: *is this a thing I possess right now, or something that happened over a period?*

> [!note] Current vs non-current is about **timing**, not importance
> **Current** = expected to be settled or realised within 12 months (or the normal operating cycle). A long-term loan entering its final year **moves** to current liabilities without anything real having changed — a genuine source of confusion when reading successive balance sheets.

> [!tip] Learn both vocabularies
> This course uses US and international names interchangeably. **Balance Sheet = Statement of Financial Position; Income Statement = Statement of Profit or Loss.** Use whichever the question uses, and never mix them within one answer.
