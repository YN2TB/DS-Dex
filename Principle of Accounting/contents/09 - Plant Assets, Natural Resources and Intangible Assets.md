# Plant Assets, Natural Resources and Intangible Assets

> [!abstract] Where this sits in the course
> [[05 - Adjusting Entries]] introduced depreciation in one sentence: *allocate the cost of an asset to expense over its useful life.* **This chapter is that sentence developed in full** — what goes into the cost, how the allocation is computed under three different methods, what happens when estimates change, and how the asset is removed when it goes.
>
> It then extends the same idea twice more: **depletion** for natural resources and **amortisation** for intangibles. **Three names, one principle.** This is the final chapter of the subject.

---

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Determining the cost of plant assets

> **Plant assets are resources that have physical substance (a definite size and shape), are used in the operations of a business, are not intended for sale to customers, and are expected to be of use to the company for a number of years.**

**Also referred to as property, plant and equipment; plant and equipment; or fixed assets.**

> [!note] All four conditions must hold
> - **Physical substance** — distinguishes plant assets from **intangibles** (§6).
> - **Used in operations** — distinguishes them from **investments**.
> - **Not intended for sale to customers** — distinguishes them from **inventory**. A delivery van is a plant asset to a courier and inventory to a dealership (the same purpose test as [[07 - Inventories]]).
> - **Use for a number of years** — distinguishes them from supplies and other current assets.

> **The Historical Cost Principle requires that companies record plant assets at cost. Cost consists of all expenditures necessary to acquire an asset and make it ready for its intended use.**

#### Land

**All necessary costs incurred in making the land ready for its intended use increase (debit) the Land account.** Costs typically include:

- **cash purchase price**
- **closing costs** such as title and attorney's fees
- **real estate brokers' commissions**
- **accrued property taxes and other liens on the land assumed by the purchaser**

> **Illustration:** Hayes Company acquires real estate at a cash cost of **$100,000**. The property contains an old warehouse that is razed at a **net cost of $6,000** ($7,500 in costs less $1,500 proceeds from salvaged materials). Additional expenditures are the attorney's fee **$1,000** and the real estate broker's commission **$8,000**.
>
> | | $ |
> |---|---|
> | Cash price of property | 100,000 |
> | Net removal cost of warehouse ($7,500 − $1,500) | 6,000 |
> | Attorney's fees | 1,000 |
> | Real estate broker's commission | 8,000 |
> | **Cost of Land** | **115,000** |

> [!important] Why demolition cost is added to **Land**, not expensed
> The warehouse was demolished to make the land usable — so it is a cost of **getting the land ready for its intended use**, and the historical cost principle capitalises it.
>
> Note also that the **$1,500 of salvage proceeds reduces the cost**; it is not separate income. **The cost is the net outlay.**

#### Land improvements

> **Structural additions made to land.** Cost includes all expenditures necessary to make the improvements ready for their intended use. **Examples: driveways, parking lots, fences, landscaping, and underground sprinklers.**
>
> **Limited useful lives — expense (depreciate) the cost of land improvements over their useful lives.**

> [!warning] Land is not depreciated; land improvements are
> **Land has an indefinite useful life** — it does not wear out — so it is never depreciated. **A car park does wear out** and must be resurfaced, so it is depreciated over its own useful life.
>
> **This makes the Land / Land Improvements split economically significant, not merely a classification nicety.** Putting a $200,000 car park into Land would understate expenses every year for its entire life.

#### Buildings

**Includes all costs related directly to purchase or construction.**

| | Costs included |
|---|---|
| **Purchase** | Purchase price, closing costs (attorney's fees, title insurance, etc.) and real estate broker's commission; **remodelling and replacing or repairing the roof, floors, electrical wiring and plumbing** |
| **Construction** | **Contract price plus payments for architects' fees, building permits, and excavation costs** |

#### Equipment

**Include all costs incurred in acquiring the equipment and preparing it for use.** Costs typically include:

- **cash purchase price**
- **sales taxes**
- **freight charges**
- **insurance during transit paid by the purchaser**
- **expenditures required in assembling, installing, and testing the unit**

> **Illustration:** Lenard Company purchases a delivery truck at a cash price of **$22,000**. Related expenditures are sales taxes **$1,320**, painting and lettering **$500**, motor vehicle licence **$80**, and a three-year accident insurance policy **$1,600**.
>
> | | $ |
> |---|---|
> | Cash price | 22,000 |
> | Sales taxes | 1,320 |
> | Painting and lettering | 500 |
> | **Cost of Delivery Truck** | **23,820** |
>
> **Journal entry:**
> $$\text{Dr Equipment } 23{,}820,\;\; \text{Dr Licence Expense } 80,\;\; \text{Dr Prepaid Insurance } 1{,}600 \;/\; \text{Cr Cash } 25{,}500$$

> [!important] The licence and the insurance are **not** part of the truck's cost
> This is the whole point of the example. Three payments were made on the same day, and they receive three different treatments:
>
> | Item | Treatment | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | Painting and lettering $500 | **Capitalised** | A one-off cost of preparing *this* truck for *its intended use* |
> | Motor vehicle licence $80 | **Expensed** | An **annual operating cost**, recurring — it does not prepare the asset |
> | Insurance $1,600 | **Prepaid asset** | Covers **three future years**; expensed over time |
>
> **The test is: is this a one-time cost of getting the asset ready, or a recurring cost of operating it?** One-time preparation costs are capitalised; recurring operating costs are not.

#### Expenditures during useful life

| | **Ordinary repairs** | **Additions and improvements** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | **Expenditures to maintain the operating efficiency and productive life of the unit** | **Costs incurred to increase the operating efficiency, productive capacity, or useful life of a plant asset** |
| Entry | **Debit Maintenance and Repair Expense** | **Debit the plant asset affected** |
| Known as | **Revenue expenditures** | **Capital expenditures** |

> [!important] Capital vs revenue expenditure — a favourite exam distinction
> **Does it merely restore the asset, or does it improve it?**
> - Replacing worn tyres → **maintains** → expense.
> - Fitting a refrigeration unit to a plain van → **increases capacity** → capitalise.
>
> **Getting it wrong distorts several years of profit.** Wrongly expensing a capital item understates this year's profit and overstates every later year's; wrongly capitalising a repair does the reverse — **and that is exactly the WorldCom fraud.**

> [!warning] Anatomy of a fraud — WorldCom, $7 billion
> **Bernie Ebbers** was founder and CEO of WorldCom. The company made a series of increasingly large, debt-financed acquisitions, which made it grow quickly and drove the stock price up. **Because the acquired companies all had different accounting systems, WorldCom's financial records were a mess.** When performance flattened, Ebbers coerced the accounting staff into **capitalising ordinary operating expenses** — treating routine costs as plant assets so they would be spread over years instead of hitting current profit.
>
> **The missing controls:**
> 1. **Documentation procedures.** The accounting system was a disorganised collection of non-integrated systems resulting from the acquisitions. **Top management took advantage of this disorganisation to conceal its fraudulent activities.**
> 2. **Independent internal verification.** A fraud of this size **should have been detected by a routine comparison of the actual physical assets with the list of physical assets shown in the accounting records.**
>
> **Total take: $7 billion.** Note that the fraud consisted entirely of **misclassifying revenue expenditure as capital expenditure** — the distinction in the table above, applied dishonestly at scale. Compare the Tasanee case in [[08 - Accounting for Receivables]]: **again, every entry balanced.**

### 2. Depreciation

> **Depreciation is the process of allocating to expense the cost of a plant asset over its useful (service) life in a rational and systematic manner.**
>
> - **A process of cost allocation, not asset valuation.**
> - **Applies to land improvements, buildings, and equipment — NOT land.**
> - Assets are depreciable because **the revenue-producing ability of the asset will decline over the asset's useful life.**

**Depreciation expense is reported on the income statement. Accumulated depreciation is reported on the balance sheet as a deduction from plant assets.**

#### Three factors in computing depreciation

| Factor | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cost** | All expenditures necessary to acquire the asset and make it ready for use (§1) |
| **Useful life** | Estimated life in years, units of activity, or other measure |
| **Salvage value** | Estimated value at the end of useful life — **also called residual value** |

$$
\text{Depreciable cost} = \text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value}
$$

> **Management selects the method it believes best measures an asset's contribution to revenue over its useful life.** The three methods are **straight-line**, **units-of-activity**, and **declining-balance**.

---

### 3. The three depreciation methods

> **Illustration:** Barb's Florists purchased a small delivery truck on **January 1, 2017**.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | Cost | **$13,000** |
> | Expected salvage value | **$1,000** |
> | Estimated useful life in years | **5** |
> | Estimated useful life in miles | **100,000** |
>
> Depreciable cost $= 13{,}000 - 1{,}000 = \mathbf{\$12{,}000}$ under every method.

#### 3.1 Straight-line

> **Expense is the same amount for each year.** Depreciable cost = cost less salvage value.

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Annual depreciation} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value}}{\text{Useful life in years}}\;}
$$

$$
\frac{13{,}000-1{,}000}{5} = \$2{,}400 \text{ per year}
\qquad\text{(a rate of } 20\%\text{ of depreciable cost)}
$$

| Year | Depreciable cost | Rate | Expense | Accumulated depreciation | Book value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | 12,000 | 20% | 2,400 | 2,400 | **10,600** |
| 2018 | 12,000 | 20% | 2,400 | 4,800 | 8,200 |
| 2019 | 12,000 | 20% | 2,400 | 7,200 | 5,800 |
| 2020 | 12,000 | 20% | 2,400 | 9,600 | 3,400 |
| 2021 | 12,000 | 20% | 2,400 | 12,000 | **1,000** |

*Book value = Cost − Accumulated depreciation. 2017: $13{,}000 - 2{,}400 = \$10{,}600$.*

**2017 journal entry:**
$$
\text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 2{,}400 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 2{,}400
$$

**Note that the final book value is exactly the $1,000 salvage value** — that is what makes the schedule correct.

##### Partial year

> **Assume the delivery truck was purchased on April 1, 2017.**

Then 2017 gets only **nine months**:
$$
2{,}400 \times \frac{9}{12} = \$1{,}800
$$
and the schedule shifts forward, running into 2022 for the final three months' worth.

#### 3.2 Units-of-activity

> **Companies estimate total units of activity to calculate depreciation cost per unit.** Expense **varies based on units of activity**. Depreciable cost is cost less salvage value. *(Also called the **units-of-production** method.)*

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Depreciation cost per unit} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value}}{\text{Total units of activity}}\;}
$$
$$
\text{Annual expense} = \text{Cost per unit} \times \text{Units of activity this year}
$$

$$
\frac{12{,}000}{100{,}000 \text{ miles}} = \$0.12 \text{ per mile}
$$

| Year | Miles | Cost/unit | Expense | Accumulated | Book value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | 15,000 | 0.12 | 1,800 | 1,800 | **11,200** |
| 2018 | 30,000 | 0.12 | 3,600 | 5,400 | 7,600 |
| 2019 | 20,000 | 0.12 | 2,400 | 7,800 | 5,200 |
| 2020 | 25,000 | 0.12 | 3,000 | 10,800 | 2,200 |
| 2021 | 10,000 | 0.12 | 1,200 | 12,000 | **1,000** |

**2017 journal entry:**
$$
\text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 1{,}800 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 1{,}800
$$

Total miles $= 15{,}000+30{,}000+20{,}000+25{,}000+10{,}000 = 100{,}000$ ✓ and total depreciation $= \$12{,}000$ ✓

> [!note] When units-of-activity is the right choice
> When **wear is driven by use rather than by time** — vehicles measured in kilometres, machines in operating hours, aircraft in flight cycles. **It matches expense to activity, hence to the revenue that activity generates**, which is the expense recognition principle applied precisely.
>
> Its drawback: it requires reliable measurement of activity, and the total-units estimate is as uncertain as the useful-life estimate under straight-line.

#### 3.3 Declining-balance

> **An accelerated method** — **decreasing annual depreciation expense over the asset's useful life.** **Twice the straight-line rate** with **double-declining-balance**. **The rate is applied to BOOK VALUE.**

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Annual expense} = \text{Book value at beginning of year} \times \text{Declining-balance rate}\;}
$$

Straight-line rate $=20\%$, so double-declining rate $=\mathbf{40\%}$.

| Year | Book value at start | Rate | Expense | Accumulated | Book value at end |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | 13,000 | 40% | **5,200** | 5,200 | 7,800 |
| 2018 | 7,800 | 40% | 3,120 | 8,320 | 4,680 |
| 2019 | 4,680 | 40% | 1,872 | 10,192 | 2,808 |
| 2020 | 2,808 | 40% | 1,123 | 11,315 | 1,685 |
| 2021 | 1,685 | 40% | **685*** | 12,000 | **1,000** |

*\* Computation of $674 ($1,685 × 40%) is adjusted to **$685** so that book value equals the $1,000 salvage value.*

**2017 journal entry:**
$$
\text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 5{,}200 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 5{,}200
$$

> [!important] Two features that make declining-balance different
> **1. Salvage value is IGNORED in the calculation** — the rate is applied to **book value**, which starts at full cost. Note the 2017 expense is $13{,}000\times40\%$, not $12{,}000\times40\%$. This is the opposite of the other two methods, and it is the most common error.
>
> **2. The final year is a plug.** The formula would give $674, leaving book value at $1,011 — below cost but *above* salvage would be fine, yet the schedule must land exactly on $1,000. So the last year's charge is **adjusted to $685**.
>
> **Under any method, depreciation stops when the asset's book value equals expected salvage value.**

#### Comparison

| Year | Straight-line | Units-of-activity | Declining-balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2017 | 2,400 | 1,800 | **5,200** |
| 2018 | 2,400 | 3,600 | 3,120 |
| 2019 | 2,400 | 2,400 | 1,872 |
| 2020 | 2,400 | 3,000 | 1,123 |
| 2021 | 2,400 | 1,200 | 685 |
| **Total** | **12,000** | **12,000** | **12,000** |

> [!important] **Every method totals $12,000.** They differ only in *timing*.
> The choice of method never changes the total expense over the asset's life — it changes which years bear it. **Declining-balance front-loads the charge**, which is why it is called accelerated, and which suits assets that are most productive when new or that face rising repair costs as they age.
>
> **Exactly the same structural point as the cost flow methods in [[07 - Inventories]]:** the total is fixed; only its allocation between periods is a matter of assumption.

#### Depreciation and income taxes

> **The IRS does not require a taxpayer to use the same depreciation method on the tax return that is used in preparing financial statements.**
>
> **Taxpayers must use the straight-line method or a special accelerated-depreciation method called the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS). MACRS is NOT acceptable under GAAP.**

> [!note] Contrast with the LIFO conformity rule
> [[07 - Inventories]] noted that a company using LIFO for tax **must** use it for reporting. **Depreciation has no such rule** — a company can take accelerated depreciation for tax (deferring tax) while reporting straight-line to shareholders (showing higher profit).
>
> **This is why almost every company does exactly that**, and it is the main source of deferred tax in published accounts.

### 4. Revising periodic depreciation

Useful life and salvage value are **estimates**, and estimates change.

> **A revision is accounted for in the period of change and future periods (a change in estimate).**
> - **No change in depreciation is reported for prior years.**
> - **It is NOT considered an error.**

**The step-by-step approach:** (1) determine the **new depreciable cost**; (2) **divide by the remaining useful life**.

> **Illustration:** Arcadia HS purchased equipment for **$510,000**, estimated to have a useful life of **10 years** with a salvage value of **$10,000**. Depreciation has been recorded for **7 years** on a straight-line basis. In 2015 (year 8), it is determined that the **total estimated life should be 15 years** with a salvage value of **$5,000**.
>
> **Question 1: What is the journal entry to correct the prior years' depreciation?**
>
> $$\textbf{No entry required.}$$
>
> **Question 2: Calculate the depreciation expense for 2015.**
>
> *Step 1 — establish net book value at the date of change:*
>
> | | $ |
> |---|---|
> | Equipment cost | 510,000 |
> | Less: salvage value (original) | (10,000) |
> | **Depreciable base** | **500,000** |
> | ÷ useful life (original) | 10 years |
> | **Annual depreciation** | **50,000** |
> | × 7 years | **350,000** accumulated |
>
> Balance sheet at 31 December 2014: Equipment $510,000 − accumulated depreciation $350,000 = **NBV $160,000**.
>
> *Step 2 — apply the new estimates to the remaining life:*
>
> | | $ |
> |---|---|
> | Net book value | 160,000 |
> | Less: salvage value (new) | (5,000) |
> | **Depreciable base** | **155,000** |
> | ÷ useful life remaining (15 − 7) | 8 years |
> | **Annual depreciation** | **19,375** |
>
> $$\text{2015 and future years:}\quad \text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 19{,}375 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 19{,}375$$

> [!important] Why prior years are never restated
> A change in estimate is **not** an error — the original figures were the best information available at the time. **Accounting does not punish honest estimates that turn out wrong;** it simply applies the improved information going forward.
>
> **Contrast with an *error*** (say, a depreciation calculation that was arithmetically wrong), which **would** require restatement of prior periods.
>
> **The mechanical rule to remember:** *new depreciable base ÷ remaining life*, where the new depreciable base is **current NBV minus the new salvage value** — **not** original cost minus new salvage.

### 5. Disposing of plant assets

> **Companies dispose of plant assets in three ways — retirement, sale, or exchange.**
>
> - **Record depreciation up to the date of disposal.**
> - **Eliminate the asset by (1) debiting Accumulated Depreciation and (2) crediting the asset account.**

#### Retirement

> **No cash is received.** Decrease (credit) the asset account for its original cost; decrease (debit) Accumulated Depreciation for the full amount taken over the asset's life.

> **Fully depreciated:** Hobart Enterprises retires computer printers costing **$32,000** with accumulated depreciation of **$32,000**:
> $$\text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 32{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 32{,}000$$
>
> **What happens if a fully depreciated plant asset is still useful?** **The company continues to use the asset with no additional depreciation being recorded, as the asset is fully depreciated.**
>
> **Not fully depreciated:** Sunset Company discards delivery equipment costing **$18,000** with accumulated depreciation of **$14,000**:
> $$\text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 14{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Loss on Disposal of Plant Assets } 4{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 18{,}000$$
>
> **Companies report a loss on disposal in the "Other expenses and losses" section of the income statement.**

> [!note] A fully depreciated asset still in use
> Book value is zero, but the asset keeps working. **No further depreciation is recorded** — you cannot depreciate below cost. The asset stays on the books at cost with equal accumulated depreciation until it is disposed of.
>
> **This is a signal, not a problem.** A company whose plant is largely fully depreciated is using old assets, and a large gap between gross cost and net book value tells a reader the asset base is ageing — one of the reasons [[05 - Adjusting Entries]] insists on showing both figures.

#### Sale

> **Compare the book value of the asset with the proceeds received.**
> - **If proceeds exceed book value → gain on disposal.**
> - **If proceeds are less than book value → loss on disposal.**

> **Illustration:** On July 1, 2017, Wright Company sells office furniture for **$16,000** cash. The furniture originally cost **$60,000**. As of January 1, 2017 it had accumulated depreciation of **$41,000**. Depreciation for the first six months of 2017 is **$8,000**.
>
> **Step 1 — record depreciation to the date of sale:**
> $$\text{Jul 1:}\quad \text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 8{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 8{,}000$$
>
> **Step 2 — compute book value:**
> $$\text{Accumulated depreciation} = 41{,}000+8{,}000 = \$49{,}000$$
> $$\text{Book value} = 60{,}000-49{,}000 = \$11{,}000$$
>
> **Step 3 — record the sale.** Proceeds $16,000 > book value $11,000 → **gain of $5,000**:
> $$\text{Dr Cash } 16{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 49{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 60{,}000,\;\; \text{Cr Gain on Disposal } 5{,}000$$
>
> **If instead Wright sells it for $9,000** → loss of $2,000:
> $$\text{Dr Cash } 9{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 49{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Loss on Disposal } 2{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 60{,}000$$

> [!important] Step 1 is the one candidates forget
> **Depreciation must be brought up to the date of disposal before computing book value.** Omitting the $8,000 would leave accumulated depreciation at $41,000, book value at $19,000, and turn a $5,000 **gain** into a $3,000 **loss** — the wrong sign, not just the wrong number.
>
> **A gain or loss on disposal is not a "real" economic event so much as a correction.** It tells you the depreciation estimates were slightly wrong: a gain means you depreciated too fast, a loss too slowly. **This is why gains and losses on disposal appear in "other revenues/expenses" rather than in operating results** — they are artefacts of estimation, not of trading.

### 6. Natural resources and depletion

> **Natural resources consist of standing timber and underground deposits of oil, gas, and minerals.** Distinguishing characteristics:
> - **Physically extracted in operations.**
> - **Replaceable only by an act of nature.**
>
> **Cost is the price needed to acquire the resource and prepare it for its intended use.**

> **Depletion is the allocation of the cost to expense in a rational and systematic manner over the resource's useful life.** Companies **generally use the units-of-activity method**, since **depletion is generally a function of the units extracted.**

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Depletion cost per unit} = \frac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage value}}{\text{Total estimated units}}\;}
$$

> **Illustration:** Lane Coal Company invests **$5 million** in a mine estimated to have **1 million tons** of coal and **no salvage value**. In the first year, Lane extracts and sells **250,000 tons**.
> $$\$5{,}000{,}000 \div 1{,}000{,}000 = \$5.00 \text{ depletion cost per ton}$$
> $$\$5.00 \times 250{,}000 = \$1{,}250{,}000 \text{ annual depletion expense}$$
> $$\text{Dr Inventory (coal) } 1{,}250{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depletion } 1{,}250{,}000$$

> [!important] Depletion is debited to **Inventory**, not to an expense account
> This surprises people. The extracted coal is a **product** — an asset — until it is sold. **The depletion becomes an expense only through cost of goods sold**, when the coal is sold ([[06 - Accounting for Merchandising Operations]]).
>
> If Lane extracted 250,000 tons but sold only 200,000, then $\$5 \times 50{,}000 = \$250{,}000$ would remain in inventory at the year end.
>
> **Accumulated Depletion is a contra-asset**, exactly like accumulated depreciation, deducted from the natural resource on the balance sheet.

### 7. Intangible assets

> **Intangible assets are rights, privileges, and competitive advantages that result from ownership of long-lived assets that do not possess physical substance.**

**Common types:** patents, copyrights, trademarks and trade names, franchises, goodwill. Each has either a **limited life** or an **indefinite life**.

| | **Limited-life intangibles** | **Indefinite-life intangibles** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Life is limited by law, contract or economics | **No foreseeable limit on the time the asset is expected to provide cash flows** |
| Treatment | **Amortise to expense; credit the asset account** | **No amortisation** |

> **Amortisation is to intangibles what depreciation is to plant assets and depletion is to natural resources.**

> [!important] Amortisation credits the asset account directly
> Unlike depreciation and depletion, **amortisation does not usually use a contra account.** The credit goes straight to the intangible:
> $$\text{Dr Amortisation Expense} \;/\; \text{Cr Patents}$$
> So the balance sheet shows only the **net** carrying amount. The gross cost and cumulative amortisation are disclosed in the notes rather than on the face of the statement.

#### The five types

| Intangible | Definition and legal life | Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| **Patents** | **Exclusive right to manufacture, sell, or otherwise control an invention for 20 years from the date of grant** | **Capitalise the cost of purchasing** a patent and **amortise over its 20-year life or its useful life, whichever is shorter**. **Expense any R&D costs** in developing it. **Legal fees incurred successfully defending a patent are capitalised** to the Patent account |
| **Copyrights** | **Exclusive right to reproduce and sell an artistic or published work**; extends for **the life of the creator plus 70 years** | Cost is **acquiring and defending** it; **amortised over useful life** |
| **Trademarks and trade names** | **Word, phrase, jingle, or symbol that identifies a particular enterprise or product** — Wheaties, Monopoly, Kleenex, Coca-Cola, Big Mac, Jeep. **Legal protection for an indefinite number of 20-year renewal periods** | **Capitalise acquisition costs. NO amortisation** |
| **Franchises** | **Contractual arrangement between a franchisor and a franchisee** — Shell, Subway, Rent-A-Wreck | **Limited life → amortise** over useful life; **indefinite life → do not amortise** |
| **Goodwill** | **Exceptional management, desirable location, good customer relations, skilled employees, high-quality products** | **Only recorded when an entire business is purchased.** Recorded as the **excess of purchase price over the fair value of the net assets acquired. NOT amortised** |

> **Illustration:** National Labs purchases a patent at a cost of **$60,000**, with an estimated useful life of **eight years**:
> $$\frac{\$60{,}000}{8} = \$7{,}500 \text{ per year}$$
> $$\text{Dr Amortisation Expense } 7{,}500 \;/\; \text{Cr Patents } 7{,}500$$

**Amortise over the *shorter* of legal life and useful life** — here 8 years, not the 20-year legal life.

> [!important] Goodwill is the strangest item in accounting
> **Only recorded when an entire business is purchased**, as the excess of the purchase price over the fair value of the net assets acquired.
>
> $$\text{Goodwill} = \text{Purchase price} - \text{Fair value of identifiable net assets}$$
>
> **Why only on purchase?** Every successful business generates goodwill — reputation, staff, customer relationships — but **internally generated goodwill is never recorded**, because there is no transaction to measure it by and any estimate would be pure speculation. Only when a buyer actually pays a premium does an objective, verifiable number exist.
>
> **Why not amortised?** Goodwill has no determinable useful life. Instead it is **tested annually for impairment**, and written down if the acquired business underperforms. **Large goodwill write-offs are the accounting signal of an acquisition that failed** — which is why they make headlines.
>
> **This also explains why a company's market value routinely exceeds its book value**: the monetary unit assumption from [[05 - Adjusting Entries]] keeps internally generated goodwill off the balance sheet entirely.

#### Research and development costs

> **Expenditures that may lead to patents, copyrights, new processes, and new products.**
>
> $$\textbf{ALL R\&D costs are expensed when incurred.}$$
>
> **R&D costs are not intangible assets** — but because they may lead to patents and copyrights, they are discussed alongside them.

> [!warning] The R&D rule produces a striking asymmetry
> **Buy a patent for $60,000 → an asset**, amortised over 8 years.
> **Spend $60,000 developing the same invention → an expense**, all in year one.
>
> Same invention, same economics, opposite accounting. **The justification is uncertainty**: at the time R&D is incurred, nobody knows whether it will produce anything. Capitalising it would put an asset of unknown value on the balance sheet.
>
> **The consequence:** research-intensive companies — pharmaceutical, software, biotech — systematically understate their assets and understate profit in years of heavy investment. **IFRS softens this slightly**, permitting capitalisation of *development* costs once technical feasibility and commercial intent are demonstrated, while still expensing *research*. **US GAAP expenses both.**

### Exercise 1 — Cost of plant assets (lecture DO IT!)

Drummond Heating and Cooling Co. purchases a delivery truck for **$15,000** cash, plus sales taxes of **$900** and delivery costs of **$500**. The buyer also pays **$200** for painting and lettering, **$600** for an annual insurance policy, and **$80** for a motor vehicle licence. Explain how each cost would be accounted for.

> [!example]- Solution
> | Item | $ | Treatment |
> |---|---|---|
> | Purchase price | 15,000 | **Capitalise** |
> | Sales taxes | 900 | **Capitalise** |
> | Delivery costs | 500 | **Capitalise** |
> | Painting and lettering | 200 | **Capitalise** |
> | **Cost of truck** | **16,600** | |
> | Annual insurance | 600 | **Expense** (or prepay and expense over the year) |
> | Motor vehicle licence | 80 | **Expense** |
>
> **The first four payments ($15,000, $900, $500 and $200) are included in the cost of the truck ($16,600). The payments for insurance and the licence are operating costs and are therefore expensed.**
>
> ---
> **The test in one question: is this a one-off cost of getting *this* asset ready for *its intended* use?**
>
> - **Sales taxes and delivery** — unavoidable, one-off, incurred to get the truck to the premises → capitalise.
> - **Painting and lettering** — one-off, and arguably prepares the truck for its intended use as a branded delivery vehicle → capitalise.
> - **Insurance and licence** — **recurring annual costs of operating** the truck, not of acquiring it → expense.
>
> **Compare with the Lenard example in §1**, where the insurance ran for *three* years and was therefore recorded as **Prepaid Insurance** (an asset expensed over three years). Here the policy is annual, so expensing it is reasonable. **The distinction is the period covered, not the nature of the cost.**

### Exercise 2 — Three methods compared

Meridian Logistics buys a forklift on 1 January 2024 for **$48,000**, with an estimated salvage value of **$3,000**, a useful life of **5 years**, and estimated total operating hours of **15,000**. Actual hours: 2024 — 4,000; 2025 — 3,500; 2026 — 3,000; 2027 — 2,500; 2028 — 2,000.

(a) Prepare depreciation schedules under straight-line, units-of-activity and double-declining-balance. (b) Which gives the highest expense in 2024, and the lowest? (c) What is the book value at the end of 2026 under each?

> [!example]- Solution
> **Depreciable cost** $= 48{,}000 - 3{,}000 = \$45{,}000$ (straight-line and units-of-activity only).
>
> **(a) Straight-line:** $45{,}000/5 = \$9{,}000$ per year.
>
> | Year | Expense | Accumulated | Book value |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 2024 | 9,000 | 9,000 | 39,000 |
> | 2025 | 9,000 | 18,000 | 30,000 |
> | 2026 | 9,000 | 27,000 | **21,000** |
> | 2027 | 9,000 | 36,000 | 12,000 |
> | 2028 | 9,000 | 45,000 | **3,000** |
>
> **Units-of-activity:** $45{,}000/15{,}000 = \$3.00$ per hour.
>
> | Year | Hours | Expense | Accumulated | Book value |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 2024 | 4,000 | 12,000 | 12,000 | 36,000 |
> | 2025 | 3,500 | 10,500 | 22,500 | 25,500 |
> | 2026 | 3,000 | 9,000 | 31,500 | **16,500** |
> | 2027 | 2,500 | 7,500 | 39,000 | 9,000 |
> | 2028 | 2,000 | 6,000 | 45,000 | **3,000** |
>
> Total hours = 15,000 ✓, total depreciation = $45,000 ✓
>
> **Double-declining-balance:** straight-line rate $=1/5 = 20\%$, so DDB rate $=\mathbf{40\%}$, applied to **book value** (salvage ignored in the calculation).
>
> | Year | Book value at start | Expense | Accumulated | Book value at end |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 2024 | 48,000 | **19,200** | 19,200 | 28,800 |
> | 2025 | 28,800 | 11,520 | 30,720 | 17,280 |
> | 2026 | 17,280 | 6,912 | 37,632 | **10,368** |
> | 2027 | 10,368 | 4,147 | 41,779 | 6,221 |
> | 2028 | 6,221 | **3,221*** | 45,000 | **3,000** |
>
> *\* The formula gives $6{,}221\times40\% = \$2{,}488$, which would leave book value at $3,733 — above salvage. The final charge is increased to $3,221 so that book value lands exactly on the $3,000 salvage value.*
>
> **(b) 2024:** highest is **DDB at $19,200**; lowest is **straight-line at $9,000**. DDB charges **more than twice** as much in year one.
>
> **(c) Book value at end of 2026:**
>
> | | Straight-line | Units-of-activity | DDB |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Book value | **21,000** | 16,500 | **10,368** |
>
> A **$10,632 spread** in the reported value of the same forklift — over 22% of its original cost.
>
> ---
> **Three points:**
>
> **1. All three total $45,000.** Only the timing differs. Over the asset's life the choice is irrelevant; in any single year it is very relevant.
>
> **2. Note how units-of-activity tracks the DDB pattern here**, because hours happen to decline each year. Had usage risen over time, units-of-activity would have looked more like a *reverse*-accelerated method — **it follows activity, not the calendar.**
>
> **3. DDB's final-year plug is the standard adjustment.** Depreciation always stops at salvage value, so the last year absorbs whatever is needed.

### Exercise 3 — Revising depreciation

Coastal Freight bought a vessel on 1 January 2019 for **$2,400,000**, with an estimated useful life of **20 years** and salvage value of **$400,000**. Straight-line depreciation has been recorded. On 1 January 2025, an inspection concludes the total useful life will be only **15 years** and the salvage value only **$150,000**.

(a) Compute the annual depreciation charged 2019–2024 and the book value at 31 December 2024. (b) Is a prior-year adjustment needed? (c) Compute the revised annual charge. (d) What if instead the revised total life were **25 years**?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Original schedule.**
> $$\text{Annual depreciation} = \frac{2{,}400{,}000 - 400{,}000}{20} = \$100{,}000 \text{ per year}$$
> Six years recorded (2019–2024): accumulated depreciation $= 6 \times 100{,}000 = \$600{,}000$.
> $$\text{Book value at 31 Dec 2024} = 2{,}400{,}000 - 600{,}000 = \mathbf{\$1{,}800{,}000}$$
>
> **(b) No prior-year adjustment.** This is a **change in estimate**, not an error. The original figures were the best information available at the time. **No entry is required to correct prior years, and prior statements are not restated.**
>
> **(c) Revised charge.** Remaining life $= 15 - 6 = \mathbf{9}$ years:
> $$\frac{\text{NBV } 1{,}800{,}000 - \text{new salvage } 150{,}000}{9} = \frac{1{,}650{,}000}{9} = \mathbf{\$183{,}333}$$
> $$\text{Dr Depreciation Expense } 183{,}333 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depreciation } 183{,}333$$
> for 2025 and each of the next eight years.
>
> **The charge rises by 83%** — shortening the life and cutting the salvage value both accelerate the remaining depreciation.
>
> > [!warning] Use **NBV**, not original cost
> > A common error is $\frac{2{,}400{,}000-150{,}000}{9} = \$250{,}000$, which **re-depreciates the six years already charged.** The revised base is always **current net book value minus the new salvage value.**
>
> **(d) If the revised life were 25 years.** Remaining life $= 25-6 = 19$ years:
> $$\frac{1{,}800{,}000 - 150{,}000}{19} = \frac{1{,}650{,}000}{19} = \mathbf{\$86{,}842}$$
>
> **The charge falls by 13%** and profit rises correspondingly.
>
> **A point worth noticing.** Management chooses these estimates, and **extending an asset's useful life raises reported profit immediately and for every remaining year.** With no prior-year restatement and no "error" label, this is a legitimate accounting judgement that is also a well-known route to flattering results. **It is disclosed, but it is easy to miss** — which is why analysts watch for unexplained changes in depreciation rates.

### Exercise 4 — Disposal (lecture DO IT!, extended)

Overland Trucking has an old truck that cost **$30,000**, with accumulated depreciation of **$16,000**.

(a) Record the sale for **$17,000** cash. (b) Record the sale for **$10,000** cash. (c) Record its retirement with no proceeds. (d) At what price would there be neither gain nor loss, and what does that price represent?

> [!example]- Solution
> **Book value** $= 30{,}000 - 16{,}000 = \mathbf{\$14{,}000}$ in every part.
>
> **(a) Sold for $17,000 — proceeds exceed book value → gain of $3,000:**
> $$\text{Dr Cash } 17{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 16{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 30{,}000,\;\; \text{Cr Gain on Disposal } 3{,}000$$
> $$[\,17{,}000 - (30{,}000-16{,}000)\,] = \$3{,}000$$
>
> **(b) Sold for $10,000 — proceeds below book value → loss of $4,000:**
> $$\text{Dr Cash } 10{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 16{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Loss on Disposal } 4{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 30{,}000$$
> $$[\,10{,}000 - (30{,}000-16{,}000)\,] = -\$4{,}000$$
>
> **(c) Retired with no proceeds — the entire book value is a loss:**
> $$\text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation } 16{,}000,\;\; \text{Dr Loss on Disposal } 14{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Equipment } 30{,}000$$
>
> **(d) At exactly $14,000 — the book value — there is no gain or loss.**
>
> ---
> **What the gain or loss actually means.**
>
> Book value is what the **depreciation estimates predicted** the truck would be worth now. The sale price is what it is **actually** worth. So:
>
> - **Gain** → depreciation was charged **too fast**; the asset was written down below its real value, and past profits were understated.
> - **Loss** → depreciation was **too slow**; past profits were overstated.
> - **Neither** → the estimates were exactly right (which essentially never happens).
>
> **A gain on disposal is therefore not really a profit from trading — it is a correction of prior years' depreciation, recognised all at once in the year of sale.** That is why it belongs in "other revenues and gains" and not in operating results, and why a company reporting large recurring disposal gains is telling you its depreciation policy is too aggressive.
>
> **Note the structure of every disposal entry:** debit accumulated depreciation to clear it, credit the asset at **cost** to clear it, debit cash for whatever was received, and let the gain or loss balance the entry. **Always cost and accumulated depreciation, never book value.**

### Exercise 5 — Classification, depletion and intangibles

**(a)** *(Lecture DO IT!)* Identify the term most directly associated with each statement:
1. The allocation of the cost of a natural resource to expense in a rational and systematic manner.
2. Rights, privileges, and competitive advantages that result from the ownership of long-lived assets that do not possess physical substance.
3. An exclusive right granted by the federal government to reproduce and sell an artistic or published work.

**(b)** Northern Minerals pays $18,000,000 for a deposit estimated to contain 4,000,000 tonnes of ore, with a residual land value of $2,000,000. In year 1 it extracts 700,000 tonnes and sells 600,000. Compute depletion and record it.

**(c)** Vega Corp acquires all the assets of a competitor for $9,500,000. The fair value of identifiable net assets is $8,100,000. It also spends $400,000 on research and $250,000 on a patent with a 10-year remaining legal life but only 5 years of expected usefulness. Record all three.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)**
> 1. **Depletion**
> 2. **Intangible assets**
> 3. **Copyrights**
>
> *(Note the deliberate near-miss: statement 3 says "artistic or published work", which is a **copyright**. A **patent** covers an *invention*. Both are 20-year-ish exclusive rights granted by government, and the distinction is what the right covers.)*
>
> **(b) Depletion.**
> $$\text{Depletion per tonne} = \frac{18{,}000{,}000 - 2{,}000{,}000}{4{,}000{,}000} = \$4.00 \text{ per tonne}$$
> $$\text{Year 1 depletion} = 700{,}000 \times 4.00 = \mathbf{\$2{,}800{,}000}$$
> $$\text{Dr Inventory (ore) } 2{,}800{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Accumulated Depletion } 2{,}800{,}000$$
>
> **Only 600,000 tonnes were sold**, so of the $2,800,000:
> $$600{,}000 \times 4.00 = \$2{,}400{,}000 \text{ transfers to cost of goods sold}$$
> $$100{,}000 \times 4.00 = \$400{,}000 \text{ remains in inventory at the year end}$$
>
> **This is the point of debiting Inventory rather than an expense.** Depletion attaches to the ore; it becomes an expense only when the ore is sold. **Extraction is not consumption.**
>
> **(c) Three very different treatments.**
>
> *Goodwill:*
> $$\text{Goodwill} = 9{,}500{,}000 - 8{,}100{,}000 = \$1{,}400{,}000$$
> Recorded as an asset, **not amortised**, tested annually for impairment.
>
> *Research — $400,000:*
> $$\text{Dr Research Expense } 400{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Cash } 400{,}000$$
> **All R&D costs are expensed when incurred.** No asset, regardless of how promising the research is.
>
> *Patent — $250,000, amortised over the **shorter** of legal life (10 years) and useful life (5 years):*
> $$\frac{250{,}000}{5} = \$50{,}000 \text{ per year}$$
> $$\text{Dr Amortisation Expense } 50{,}000 \;/\; \text{Cr Patents } 50{,}000$$
> Note the credit goes **directly to Patents**, not to a contra account.
>
> ---
> **The three treatments side by side make the chapter's logic visible:**
>
> | | Life | Treatment | Why |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **Goodwill** $1,400,000 | Indefinite | **No amortisation**; impairment test | No determinable life; only exists because a purchase price measured it |
> | **Research** $400,000 | Unknown | **Expensed immediately** | Too uncertain to be an asset at all |
> | **Patent** $250,000 | 5 years (useful < legal) | **Amortised** $50,000/year | Definite life, definite cost, definite benefit |
>
> **And note the asymmetry the R&D rule creates:** had Vega's $400,000 of research produced the very patent it instead bought for $250,000, **nothing would appear on the balance sheet.** Same invention, same company, opposite accounting — determined entirely by whether the cost was incurred internally or paid to someone else.

## 📝 Summary

- **Plant assets** have physical substance, are used in operations, are not for sale to customers, and last several years. **Cost includes all expenditures necessary to acquire the asset and make it ready for its intended use** — but **not** recurring operating costs such as licences and annual insurance.
- **Land is never depreciated; land improvements are.** Demolition costs of clearing a site are added to **Land**, net of salvage proceeds.
- **Ordinary repairs are revenue expenditures** (expensed); **additions and improvements are capital expenditures** (added to the asset). **Misclassifying between them was the entire WorldCom fraud — $7 billion.**
- **Depreciation is cost allocation, not valuation.** Three factors: **cost, useful life, salvage value**, with depreciable cost = cost − salvage.
- **Three methods, same total, different timing:**
  - **Straight-line:** $\dfrac{\text{Cost}-\text{Salvage}}{\text{Useful life}}$ — equal amounts.
  - **Units-of-activity:** $\dfrac{\text{Cost}-\text{Salvage}}{\text{Total units}} \times$ units this year — varies with use.
  - **Declining-balance:** rate × **book value**, at twice the straight-line rate for DDB — **accelerated, and salvage value is ignored in the calculation** until the final year, which is adjusted so book value lands on salvage.
- **Depreciation for tax may differ from depreciation for reporting** (MACRS is not acceptable under GAAP) — unlike the LIFO conformity rule for inventory.
- **A revision of useful life or salvage value is a change in estimate:** **no prior-year adjustment, not an error.** New charge = $\dfrac{\text{Current NBV} - \text{New salvage}}{\text{Remaining life}}$.
- **On disposal, record depreciation to the date of disposal first**, then compare **proceeds against book value**: proceeds > BV → **gain**; proceeds < BV → **loss**. Always clear the asset at **cost** and clear the full accumulated depreciation.
- **Depletion** allocates natural resource cost by **units extracted**, and is debited to **Inventory** — it becomes an expense only when the resource is sold.
- **Intangibles** have no physical substance. **Limited life → amortise** (credit the asset directly, over the **shorter** of legal and useful life); **indefinite life → do not amortise**. **Goodwill is recorded only on purchase of an entire business**, as purchase price less fair value of identifiable net assets, and is **never amortised**. **All R&D costs are expensed when incurred.**

## ⚠️ Important Notes

> [!warning] Capital vs revenue expenditure
> **Maintains → expense. Improves → capitalise.** Wrongly capitalising a repair inflates current profit and every future year's asset base; wrongly expensing an improvement does the reverse. **This single distinction, applied dishonestly, was a $7 billion fraud.**

> [!warning] Declining-balance ignores salvage value in the calculation
> Apply the rate to **book value**, which starts at **full cost**. The first year's charge is $\text{Cost}\times\text{rate}$, **not** $(\text{Cost}-\text{Salvage})\times\text{rate}$. Only the **final year** is adjusted, so that book value ends exactly at salvage value.

> [!warning] Revising depreciation uses NBV, not original cost
> $$\text{New annual charge} = \frac{\text{Current net book value} - \text{New salvage value}}{\text{Remaining useful life}}$$
> Using original cost re-depreciates years already charged. And **remaining life = new total life − years already elapsed**, not the new total life itself.

> [!warning] Bring depreciation up to date before computing a gain or loss
> Omitting the part-year depreciation to the date of sale changes book value and can flip the sign of the result. **Step 1 is always the depreciation entry; step 2 is the disposal.**

> [!tip] Every disposal entry has the same skeleton
> $$\text{Dr Cash (if any)},\;\; \text{Dr Accumulated Depreciation (full amount)} \;/\; \text{Cr Asset (at COST)}$$
> then a **gain (credit)** or **loss (debit)** to make it balance. **Never credit the asset at book value** — cost and accumulated depreciation must both be cleared.

> [!note] Three names, one principle
> | Asset type | Allocation process |
> |---|---|
> | Plant assets | **Depreciation** |
> | Natural resources | **Depletion** |
> | Limited-life intangibles | **Amortisation** |
>
> All three spread a cost over the periods that benefit from it — the expense recognition principle of [[05 - Adjusting Entries]]. Only the name and the usual method change.

> [!important] Goodwill and R&D: the two rules that seem inconsistent but are not
> **Goodwill is recognised only when purchased**; internally generated goodwill never appears. **R&D is expensed even though it may create enormous value.** Both rules follow from the same requirement: **an asset must be measurable with reasonable certainty.** A purchase price provides that; an internal estimate does not.
>
> The consequence is that **the balance sheet systematically understates knowledge-intensive businesses**, and this is the standard explanation for why market value exceeds book value.
