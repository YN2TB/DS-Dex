# Limit Theorems

> [!abstract] What this chapter is for
> **These are the two theorems that make statistics possible.** Everything before this chapter computes probabilities for a *given* distribution; this chapter says what happens when $n$ gets large — **and the answers barely depend on the distribution at all.**
>
> | | Statement | What it justifies |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Law of large numbers** | $\dfrac{X_1+\dots+X_n}{n}\to\mu$ | **estimation** — averages converge to the truth |
> | **Central limit theorem** | $\dfrac{\sum X_i-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\to N(0,1)$ | **inference** — confidence intervals, $p$-values, error bars |
>
> **The LLN says where you end up; the CLT says how fast you get there, and in what shape.** Together they explain why a bell curve appears in data generated by mechanisms that have nothing bell-shaped about them.
>
> | § | Topic | The thing to take away |
> |---|---|---|
> | **1** | Markov & Chebyshev | Bounds from **almost no information** — necessarily loose |
> | **2** | **Central limit theorem** | Sums are approximately normal, whatever the summands |
> | **3** | Strong law | Convergence **with probability 1**, not merely in probability |
> | **4** | Sharper inequalities | One-sided Chebyshev, **Chernoff** (exponentially better), Jensen |
> | **5** | Poisson error bound | The binomial–Poisson approximation error is $\le\sum p_i^2$ |
> | **6** | Lorenz curve & Gini | The LLN applied to inequality measurement |
>
> **A theme to watch:** each result costs an assumption. Markov needs only $X\ge0$; Chebyshev needs a variance; Chernoff needs an MGF. **More assumptions buy exponentially tighter bounds** — Exercise 4 makes this a numerical ladder.

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Markov's and Chebyshev's inequalities

> [!important] Proposition 2.1 — Markov's inequality
> If $X\ge0$ then for every $a>0$,
> $$P\{X\ge a\}\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$$

The proof is two lines and worth remembering: let $I=\mathbb{1}\{X\ge a\}$; since $X\ge0$ we have $I\le X/a$ pointwise; take expectations.

> [!important] Proposition 2.2 — Chebyshev's inequality
> If $X$ has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$ then for every $k>0$,
> $$P\{|X-\mu|\ge k\}\le\frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad P\{|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\}\le\frac1{k^2}$$

**It is Markov applied to $(X-\mu)^2$ with $a=k^2$** — nothing more. And it gives a corollary worth stating:

> [!tip] Proposition 2.3 — zero variance means constant
> $\mathrm{Var}(X)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ P\{X=\mathbb{E}[X]\}=1$.
> **This is what licenses the equality cases in [[07 - Properties of Expectation|ch. 07]]** — $\rho=\pm1$ implying an exact linear relationship rests on exactly this.

> [!warning] These bounds are *deliberately* weak — that is the point
> Because they hold for **every** distribution with the given moments, they must be loose for any particular one.
>
> | Case | Chebyshev bound | Truth | Ratio |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $X\sim U(0,10)$, $P\{|X-5|>4\}$ | $.521$ | $.20$ | 2.6× |
> | $X$ normal, $P\{|X-\mu|>2\sigma\}$ | $.25$ | $.0455$ | 5.5× |
>
> **Use them when you know almost nothing** — a mean, maybe a variance — and need a guarantee. **Do not use them when you know the distribution**, and never quote them as an estimate. **A guaranteed bound and a good approximation are different products.**

> [!important] Theorem 2.1 — the weak law of large numbers
> For i.i.d. $X_i$ with finite mean $\mu$, for every $\varepsilon>0$,
> $$P\left\{\left|\frac{X_1+\dots+X_n}{n}-\mu\right|\ge\varepsilon\right\}\to0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty$$

**The proof, assuming finite variance, is one application of Chebyshev:** $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$, so the probability is at most $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\to0$. *(The theorem is true without the variance assumption — Khintchine — but the proof is harder.)*

**Historical note:** Bernoulli proved the Bernoulli case in *Ars Conjectandi* (published 1713, eight years after his death), **without Chebyshev's inequality**, which did not yet exist. The elementary proof above was two centuries away.

### 2. The central limit theorem

> [!important] Theorem 3.1 — the central limit theorem
> For i.i.d. $X_i$ with mean $\mu$ and **finite** variance $\sigma^2$,
> $$P\left\{\frac{X_1+\dots+X_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\le a\right\}\ \longrightarrow\ \Phi(a)\qquad\text{for every }a$$

**The distribution of the $X_i$ appears nowhere in the limit** — only $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ survive. That is the entire content, and it is remarkable.

> [!tip] The proof in one idea
> Standardise so $\mu=0,\sigma^2=1$, and let $L(t)=\log M(t)$. Then $L(0)=0$, $L'(0)=\mu=0$, $L''(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]=1$. The MGF of $\sum X_i/\sqrt n$ is $[M(t/\sqrt n)]^n$, and two applications of L'Hôpital's rule give
> $$n\,L\!\left(\tfrac t{\sqrt n}\right)\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{t^2}2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \left[M\!\left(\tfrac t{\sqrt n}\right)\right]^n\to e^{t^2/2}$$
> which is the standard normal MGF; **convergence of MGFs implies convergence of distributions** (Lemma 3.1).
>
> **Notice what the proof actually uses: only the first two derivatives of $L$ at 0.** *That* is why nothing but $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ appears in the answer — the third and higher moments are washed out by the $1/\sqrt n$ scaling.

> [!warning] The finite-variance hypothesis is not a technicality
> **If $\sigma^2=\infty$ the theorem is false.** The Cauchy is the standard counterexample ([[05 - Continuous Random Variables|ch. 05]]): the average of $n$ i.i.d. Cauchys is Cauchy for **every** $n$ — it never concentrates and never becomes normal. **Heavy-tailed data can break both limit theorems at once**, and financial returns, city sizes and file sizes are all suspected of tails heavy enough to matter.

#### 2a. Worked applications

> [!example] Example 3a — how many measurements? (and two answers)
> Measurements of a star's distance are i.i.d. with mean $d$ and variance 4. **How many are needed to be 95% sure the average is within $\pm0.5$ light-years?**
>
> **By the CLT:** $2\Phi\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt n}4\right)-1=.95\Rightarrow\tfrac{\sqrt n}4=1.96\Rightarrow n=(7.84)^2=61.47$, so **62 measurements**.
>
> **By Chebyshev** (assuming nothing about the shape): $\dfrac{4}{n(.5)^2}=\dfrac{16}n\le.05\Rightarrow$ **320 measurements**.
>
> **The CLT answer is five times cheaper — and it is an approximation, while Chebyshev's is a guarantee.** This trade-off is the entire practical content of the chapter. *(Exercise 2 quantifies it again for polling.)*

> [!example] Examples 3b–3e — the CLT in four lines each
> | Problem | Setup | CLT answer | Exact |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Enrolment $\ge120$, Poisson(100) | $\mu=\sigma^2=100$, cc at $119.5$ | $1-\Phi(1.95)=.0256$ | $.0282$ |
> | Sum of 10 dice in $[30,40]$ | $\mu=35$, $\sigma^2=\tfrac{350}{12}$, cc | $2\Phi(1.0184)-1=.692$ | $.687$ |
> | $\sum_1^{10}U_i>6$ | $\mu=5$, $\sigma^2=\tfrac{10}{12}$ | $1-\Phi(\sqrt{1.2})=.1367$ | $.1389$ |
> | Grade 25 exams in 450 min | $\mu=500$, $\sigma^2=400$ | $1-\Phi(2.5)=.0062$ | — |
>
> **Two lessons from the exact column.** With $n=10$ uniforms the CLT is already accurate to 0.2 percentage points — **the CLT converges astonishingly fast for symmetric, light-tailed summands.** But for the Poisson it is 9% low ($.0256$ vs $.0282$) even at $\lambda=100$, **because the Poisson is right-skewed and the normal is not** — skewness is the first thing the CLT gets wrong, and it fades only like $1/\sqrt n$.
>
> **The continuity correction is doing real work in the first two rows** ([[05 - Continuous Random Variables|ch. 05]]), and omitting it is a bigger error than anything else in the table.

> [!important] Theorem 3.2 — the CLT without identical distributions
> If the $X_i$ are independent, **uniformly bounded** ($P\{|X_i|<M\}=1$ for all $i$), and $\sum_i\sigma_i^2=\infty$, then
> $$\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu_i)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n\sigma_i^2}}\ \longrightarrow\ N(0,1)$$
> **This is the version that matters in practice**, because real data are rarely identically distributed. The condition $\sum\sigma_i^2=\infty$ says **no single term may dominate** — the theorem fails if one variable carries most of the variance.

> [!tip] Why the normal distribution is everywhere
> Laplace came to the CLT from measurement error, reasoning that an error is the **sum of many tiny independent forces**. That is the general recipe: **any quantity built additively from many small, roughly independent contributions will look normal**, regardless of the contributions' own shapes.
>
> **And the converse warning: quantities built *multiplicatively* are lognormal, not normal** — the CLT applies to the *logarithm* ([[06 - Jointly Distributed Random Variables|ch. 06 §3d]]). **Additive $\Rightarrow$ normal; multiplicative $\Rightarrow$ lognormal.** Getting this backwards is the standard modelling error with prices, sizes and concentrations.

### 3. The strong law of large numbers

> [!important] Theorem 4.1 — the strong law
> For i.i.d. $X_i$ with finite mean $\mu$,
> $$P\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_1+\dots+X_n}{n}=\mu\right\}=1$$
> **Only a finite mean is required — no variance.**

> [!important] Weak versus strong — the distinction that confuses everyone
> | | Statement | What it allows |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Weak** | For each fixed large $n$, $\bar X_n$ is *probably* near $\mu$ | $|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon$ **infinitely often** (rarely, but forever) |
> | **Strong** | The *sequence* $\bar X_n$ converges to $\mu$ | $|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon$ only **finitely many times** |
>
> **The weak law is a statement about each $n$ separately; the strong law is a statement about the whole path.** The weak law leaves open that the average wanders away from $\mu$ again and again, at ever rarer intervals. **The strong law closes that door: after some finite (random) point, it never happens again.**

**The application that justifies the entire frequentist interpretation of probability:** let $X_i=\mathbb{1}\{E$ occurs on trial $i\}$. The strong law gives, with probability 1,

$$\frac{\#\{\text{occurrences of } E \text{ in } n \text{ trials}\}}{n}\ \longrightarrow\ P(E)$$

**"Probability is long-run relative frequency" is a *theorem*, not a definition** — derived from axioms that never mentioned frequencies ([[02 - Axioms of Probability|ch. 02]]).

**Proof sketch** (assuming a finite fourth moment $\mathbb{E}[X_i^4]=K$): expanding $\mathbb{E}[S_n^4]$, all cross terms with an isolated factor vanish, leaving $\mathbb{E}[S_n^4]=nK+3n(n-1)(\mathbb{E}[X_i^2])^2\le nK+3n(n-1)K$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[S_n^4/n^4]\le K/n^3+3K/n^2$, so $\sum_n\mathbb{E}[S_n^4/n^4]<\infty$, so $\sum_n S_n^4/n^4<\infty$ **with probability 1** — and a convergent series has terms tending to 0, forcing $S_n/n\to0$.

> [!tip] The key move, isolated
> **A finite expected sum forces the sum itself to be finite with probability 1** (otherwise the expectation would be infinite), and a convergent series has vanishing terms. **This "sum the expectations, conclude almost-sure convergence" device is the Borel–Cantelli argument**, and it is how almost every strong law is proved.

### 4. Sharper inequalities

#### 4a. The one-sided Chebyshev inequality

> [!important] Proposition 5.1 and Corollary 5.1
> If $\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$ then for $a>0$,
> $$P\{X\ge\mu+a\}\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2},\qquad P\{X\le\mu-a\}\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2}$$

The proof is a nice trick: $X\ge a\iff X+b\ge a+b$, so $P\{X\ge a\}\le P\{(X+b)^2\ge(a+b)^2\}\le\frac{\sigma^2+b^2}{(a+b)^2}$ by Markov — **then optimise over the free parameter $b$**, which turns out to be $b=\sigma^2/a$.

**It is always at least as good as two-sided Chebyshev for one-tailed questions**, since $\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2}<\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}$.

> [!example] Example 5b — man–woman pairs
> 100 men and 100 women randomly split into 100 pairs; $X$ = number of mixed pairs. With $X=\sum_i\mathbb{1}\{$man $i$ paired with a woman$\}$ and $P\{X_i=1\}=\tfrac{100}{199}$, $P\{X_i=X_j=1\}=\tfrac{100}{199}\tfrac{99}{197}$:
> $$\mathbb{E}[X]=50.25,\qquad \mathrm{Var}(X)=25.126$$
> $$P\{X\le30\}\le\underbrace{\frac{25.126}{(20.25)^2}=.061}_{\text{Chebyshev}}\qquad\text{vs}\qquad\underbrace{\frac{25.126}{25.126+(20.25)^2}=.058}_{\text{one-sided}}$$
> *(Both verified.)* **The improvement here is small because $a\gg\sigma$**; the one-sided bound gains most when $a$ is comparable to $\sigma$, where two-sided Chebyshev can exceed 1 and say nothing at all.

#### 4b. Chernoff bounds

> [!important] Proposition 5.2 — Chernoff bounds
> With $M(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]$,
> $$P\{X\ge a\}\le e^{-ta}M(t)\ \ \text{for all }t>0,\qquad P\{X\le a\}\le e^{-ta}M(t)\ \ \text{for all }t<0$$
> **and one then minimises over $t$.**

This is just Markov applied to $e^{tX}$ — but because $t$ is free, **optimising it converts a polynomial bound into an exponential one.**

| $X$ | Optimal $t$ | Bound |
|---|---|---|
| $Z\sim N(0,1)$ | $t=a$ | $P\{Z\ge a\}\le e^{-a^2/2}$ |
| Poisson$(\lambda)$, $i>\lambda$ | $e^t=i/\lambda$ | $P\{X\ge i\}\le \dfrac{e^{-\lambda}(e\lambda)^i}{i^i}$ |
| $\pm1$ random walk | $t=a/n$ | $P\{S_n\ge a\}\le e^{-a^2/2n}$ |

> [!example] Example 5e — the gambler's random walk
> $P\{X_i=\pm1\}=\tfrac12$, $S_n=\sum X_i$. Using the Maclaurin series and $(2n)!\ge n!2^n$,
> $$\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]=\frac{e^t+e^{-t}}2\ \le\ e^{t^2/2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[e^{tS_n}]\le e^{nt^2/2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{P\{S_n\ge a\}\le e^{-a^2/2n}}$$
> With $n=10$, $a=6$: the bound is $e^{-1.8}=.1653$ against the exact $\tfrac{56}{1024}=.0547$.
>
> **This is Hoeffding's inequality in its simplest form**, and the reason concentration bounds dominate machine-learning theory: **the bound decays like $e^{-a^2/2n}$, whereas Chebyshev gives only $n/a^2$.** For large deviations the difference is enormous — see Exercise 4.

#### 4c. Jensen's inequality, and a correlation inequality

> [!important] Proposition 5.3 — Jensen
> $f$ convex $\Rightarrow \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\ge f(\mathbb{E}[X])$; concave reverses it.

**Proof by Taylor:** $f(x)\ge f(\mu)+f'(\mu)(x-\mu)$ because the remainder term $f''(\xi)(x-\mu)^2/2\ge0$; take expectations and the linear term vanishes. **A convex function lies above every tangent line — that is the whole geometry.**

> [!example] Example 5f — risk aversion is concavity
> An investor chooses between a risky return $X$ with $\mathbb{E}[X]=m$ and a certain $m$, maximising $\mathbb{E}[u(R)]$. By Jensen:
> - **$u$ concave $\Rightarrow \mathbb{E}[u(X)]\le u(m)$ — take the certain amount. This *is* risk aversion.**
> - $u$ convex $\Rightarrow$ take the gamble.
>
> **"Risk-averse" and "concave utility" are the same statement**, which is why diminishing marginal utility and insurance markets are the same phenomenon ([[Microeconomics/contents/00-Index|Microeconomics]]).

> [!important] Proposition 5.4 — increasing functions are positively correlated
> If $f$ and $g$ are both increasing, $\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(X)]\ge\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\mathbb{E}[g(X)]$; if one increases and the other decreases, the inequality reverses.

**The proof is a gem:** take $X,Y$ i.i.d.; then $(f(X)-f(Y))(g(X)-g(Y))\ge0$ pointwise (both factors share the sign of $X-Y$); expand and take expectations. This is the **FKG / Chebyshev sum inequality**, and §6 uses it to prove the Lorenz curve lies below the diagonal.

#### 4d. Two Poisson results

**A Poisson limit (Example 5h).** If $Y\sim$ NegBin$(r,p)$ with $p=\frac r{r+\lambda}$ then $\mathbb{E}[Y]=r+\lambda$ and $\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\frac{\lambda(r+\lambda)}r\to\lambda$. **The number of failures $X=Y-r$ converges in distribution to Poisson$(\lambda)$ as $r\to\infty$** — so the negative binomial is an *over-dispersed* Poisson, and reduces to it in the limit. *(This is why the negative binomial is the standard fix for over-dispersed count data.)*

**A Poisson error bound (§8.6).** Coupling independent Bernoullis $X_i$ to Poissons $Y_i$ so that $P\{X_i\ne Y_i\}\le p_i^2$ yields, for $\lambda=\sum p_i$ and **any** set $A$,

$$\boxed{\ \left|P\left\{\sum_i X_i\in A\right\}-\sum_{i\in A}\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^i}{i!}\right|\ \le\ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2\ }$$

and when all $p_i=p$ the bound is $np^2$.

> [!tip] This finally makes "the Poisson paradigm" precise
> [[04 - Random Variables|Ch. 04]] said the binomial is "close to" Poisson$(np)$ when $p$ is small. **Here is the actual error bound: $np^2=\lambda p$.** With $n=1000$, $p=.001$ the total-variation error is at most $.001$ — **and it holds simultaneously for every event $A$**, which is far stronger than agreement of individual probabilities.
>
> **The technique is coupling:** build the two random variables on one probability space so they usually coincide, then bound the disagreement. **It is the standard modern tool for proving approximation theorems.**

### 5. The Lorenz curve and the Gini index

$L(p)$ is the fraction of total income earned by the poorest $100p\%$ of a population. Applying the **strong law** separately to numerator and denominator of the sample version gives

$$L(p)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X\,I(X)]}{\mathbb{E}[X]}=\frac1{\mathbb{E}[X]}\int_0^{\xi_p}xf(x)\,dx,\qquad\text{where }F(\xi_p)=p$$

and, conditioning the other way,

$$1-L(p)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X\mid X\ge\xi_p]\,(1-p)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}$$

— **usually the easier form**, since it needs only a conditional mean.

| Income distribution | $L(p)$ | Gini $G$ |
|---|---|---|
| All equal | $p$ | $0$ |
| Uniform$(0,b)$ | $p^2$ | $\tfrac13$ |
| Exponential | $p+(1-p)\log(1-p)$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| Pareto$(a,\lambda)$, $\lambda>1$ | $1-(1-p)^{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}$ | $\dfrac1{2\lambda-1}$ |

> [!important] Proposition 7.1 — $L$ is increasing, convex, and $L(p)\le p$
> **Convexity** holds because equal-sized slices of the population higher up the distribution earn more. **$L(p)\le p$ follows from Proposition 5.4**: $I(x)$ is decreasing and $h(x)=x$ increasing, so $\mathbb{E}[XI(X)]\le\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[I(X)]=\mathbb{E}[X]p$.
>
> **The Gini index is the normalised area of the gap:**
> $$G=\frac{1/2-\int_0^1L(p)\,dp}{1/2}=1-2\int_0^1 L(p)\,dp\ \in[0,1]$$

> [!tip] Why this section belongs in a limit-theorems chapter
> **Every quantity here is defined as an almost-sure limit**, and only the strong law makes those definitions legitimate. **$L(p)$ is not a formula someone invented — it is what the sample Lorenz curve converges to.**
>
> **And the exponential's $G=\tfrac12$ is a useful calibration:** a distribution with the mildest possible right skew already produces a Gini of 0.5, near the top of the range observed in real economies. **Inequality statistics are extremely sensitive to tail shape** — Exercise 5 shows a Pareto matching the "80/20 rule" has $G=.756$. See [[Macroeconomics/contents/00-Index|Macroeconomics]].

## ✏️ Exercises

> [!question] Exercise 1 — the bound ladder *(warm-up)*
> A factory's weekly output has mean 500 units and standard deviation 40.
>
> (i) Bound $P\{X\ge600\}$ using Markov's inequality. What did you have to assume?
> (ii) Bound $P\{|X-500|\ge100\}$ using Chebyshev's inequality.
> (iii) Bound $P\{X\ge600\}$ using the one-sided Chebyshev inequality.
> (iv) Suppose output is in fact normal. Compute the exact probability and compare all four numbers.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(i)** $P\{X\ge600\}\le\dfrac{500}{600}=\boxed{.833}$. **This assumes $X\ge0$** — Markov's only hypothesis, and it is satisfied here since output cannot be negative. **The variance was not used at all**, which is why the bound is nearly vacuous.
>
> **(ii)** $P\{|X-500|\ge100\}\le\dfrac{40^2}{100^2}=\dfrac{1600}{10000}=\boxed{.16}$
>
> **(iii)** $P\{X\ge500+100\}\le\dfrac{1600}{1600+10000}=\dfrac{1600}{11600}=\boxed{.138}$
>
> **(iv)** $z=\dfrac{600-500}{40}=2.5$, so $P=1-\Phi(2.5)=\boxed{.0062}$.
>
> | Method | Bound | Assumption used | Factor above truth |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Markov | $.833$ | $X\ge0$ | **134×** |
> | Chebyshev (two-sided) | $.16$ | $+$ variance | 26× |
> | Chebyshev (one-sided) | $.138$ | $+$ one tail only | 22× |
> | Exact (normal) | $.0062$ | full distribution | — |
>
> **Each assumption buys roughly an order of magnitude.** **These are bounds, not estimates** — reporting $.16$ as "the probability" would be wrong by a factor of 26. Their value is that the first three hold **whatever** the distribution, and the last does not.

> [!question] Exercise 2 — how large a sample?
> A pollster wants $|\hat p-p|<0.03$ with probability at least $.95$, where $\hat p$ is the sample proportion from $n$ independent respondents.
>
> (i) Find the required $n$ using Chebyshev's inequality. *(Hint: $p(1-p)\le\tfrac14$.)*
> (ii) Find the required $n$ using the central limit theorem.
> (iii) Explain the gap, and say which number a real pollster should use.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(i)** $\mathrm{Var}(\hat p)=\dfrac{p(1-p)}n\le\dfrac1{4n}$, so Chebyshev gives
> $$P\{|\hat p-p|\ge.03\}\le\frac{1/(4n)}{(.03)^2}\le.05\ \Longrightarrow\ n\ge\frac1{4(.0009)(.05)}=5555.6\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{n=5556}$$
>
> **(ii)** By the CLT, $|\hat p-p|<1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ with probability $.95$, so
> $$1.96\sqrt{\frac{1/4}{n}}\le.03\ \Longrightarrow\ n\ge\frac{(1.96)^2(0.25)}{(.03)^2}=1067.1\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{n=1068}$$
>
> **(iii)** **Chebyshev demands 5.2 times the sample.** The gap is the price of a distribution-free *guarantee*: Chebyshev must remain valid for the worst distribution with that variance, while the CLT exploits the fact that a sum of $n$ Bernoullis really is nearly normal.
>
> **The pollster should use the CLT number** — and $n\approx1000$ for a $\pm3\%$ margin is exactly what real polls report. **Two caveats that matter more than the arithmetic:**
> - **The bound used $p(1-p)\le\tfrac14$, worst case at $p=\tfrac12$.** If $p\approx.1$ then $p(1-p)=.09$ and $n\approx385$ suffices. **Margins of error quoted for polls are the $p=\tfrac12$ worst case.**
> - **Neither $n$ depends on the population size** — the finite-population correction ([[07 - Properties of Expectation|ch. 07 §3]]) is $\approx1$ whenever $N\gg n$. **A national poll and a city poll need the same sample.**

> [!question] Exercise 3 — the CLT in practice
> (i) An insurer has 10,000 policyholders. Yearly claims per policyholder have mean \$240 and standard deviation \$800, independently. Approximate $P\{\text{total claims}>\$2.7\text{ million}\}$.
> (ii) A fair die is rolled repeatedly until the running total exceeds 300. Approximate the probability that **at least 80 rolls** are needed.
> (iii) 100 light bulbs have i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\text{mean }5\text{ hours})$ lifetimes and are used one at a time, each replaced instantly on failure. Approximate the probability that a bulb is still working after 525 hours.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(i)** $\mathbb{E}[T]=10{,}000(240)=\$2{,}400{,}000$; $\mathrm{Var}(T)=10{,}000(800)^2=6.4\times10^9$, so $\mathrm{SD}=\$80{,}000$.
> $$P\{T>2{,}700{,}000\}\approx1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{300{,}000}{80{,}000}=3.75\right)=\boxed{8.8\times10^{-5}}$$
> **About 1 in 11,000.** Note the individual claim distribution is wildly non-normal (most policyholders claim \$0), **yet aggregating 10,000 of them makes the total normal** — this is the actuarial use of the CLT, and the reason insurance works.
>
> **(ii)** **Translate the event.** "At least 80 rolls are needed" $\iff$ "the first 79 rolls have not yet exceeded 300" $\iff S_{79}\le300$. With $\mathbb{E}[X_i]=3.5$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\tfrac{35}{12}$:
> $$\mathbb{E}[S_{79}]=276.5,\qquad \mathrm{SD}=\sqrt{79\cdot\tfrac{35}{12}}=15.18$$
> $$P\{S_{79}\le300\}\approx\Phi\!\left(\frac{300.5-276.5}{15.18}=1.581\right)=\boxed{.943}$$
> **The translation from "number of rolls" to "sum after a fixed number of rolls" is the whole exercise** — a *counting* variable is turned into a *sum* variable, to which the CLT applies. **This inversion is the standard device for renewal problems.**
>
> **(iii)** Same inversion: a bulb is working at 525 $\iff$ the total lifetime of all 100 exceeds 525. Since $\mathrm{Exp}(1/5)$ has mean 5 and **standard deviation also 5**:
> $$\mathbb{E}[S_{100}]=500,\quad \mathrm{SD}=\sqrt{100\cdot25}=50,\qquad P\{S_{100}>525\}\approx1-\Phi(0.5)=\boxed{.309}$$
> **The summands here are strongly right-skewed exponentials, yet $n=100$ is ample.** *(Exact: $S_{100}\sim\Gamma(100,1/5)$, so this could be computed exactly — but the CLT is within a percentage point.)*

> [!question] Exercise 4 — Chebyshev versus Chernoff *(medium–hard)*
> A fair coin is flipped 100 times; $S$ is the number of heads. Bound $P\{S\ge65\}$:
>
> (i) by Chebyshev's inequality;
> (ii) by the one-sided Chebyshev inequality;
> (iii) by the Chernoff bound $P\{S_n\ge a\}\le e^{-a^2/2n}$ for a $\pm1$ walk, after converting;
> (iv) exactly. Then explain **why** the Chernoff bound is so much better.

> [!example]- Solution
> $\mathbb{E}[S]=50$, $\mathrm{Var}(S)=25$, $\sigma=5$; the deviation is $15=3\sigma$.
>
> **(i)** $P\{S\ge65\}\le P\{|S-50|\ge15\}\le\dfrac{25}{225}=\boxed{.111}$
>
> **(ii)** $P\{S\ge50+15\}\le\dfrac{25}{25+225}=\boxed{.100}$
>
> **(iii) Convert to a $\pm1$ walk.** With $X_i=\pm1$ per flip, $S_n=\sum X_i=2S-100$, so $S\ge65\iff S_n\ge30$ with $n=100$:
> $$P\{S_n\ge30\}\le e^{-30^2/(2\cdot100)}=e^{-4.5}=\boxed{.0111}$$
>
> **(iv)** $P\{S\ge65\}=\sum_{k=65}^{100}\binom{100}k2^{-100}=\boxed{.00176}$
>
> | Method | Bound | Factor above truth |
> |---|---|---|
> | Chebyshev | $.111$ | 63× |
> | One-sided Chebyshev | $.100$ | 57× |
> | **Chernoff** | $.0111$ | **6.3×** |
> | Exact | $.00176$ | — |
>
> **Why Chernoff wins.** Chebyshev uses only the second moment, so its bound can decay no faster than $1/a^2$ — polynomially. **Chernoff uses the entire moment generating function**, i.e. *all* the moments, and then optimises over a free parameter $t$; the result decays like $e^{-a^2/2n}$ — **exponentially.**
>
> **The cost is the extra assumption:** Chebyshev needs a variance; Chernoff needs a finite MGF near 0, i.e. **exponentially light tails** ([[07 - Properties of Expectation|ch. 07 §6]]). **For heavy-tailed variables no MGF exists and Chernoff is simply unavailable** — Chebyshev is then all you have, and its polynomial decay is not a weakness of the method but an honest reflection of the tails.
>
> **This is why concentration inequalities of Chernoff–Hoeffding type underpin learning theory:** generalisation bounds need failure probabilities like $e^{-n\varepsilon^2}$, and $1/(n\varepsilon^2)$ would be useless.

> [!question] Exercise 5 — Lorenz curves and the Gini index *(hard)*
> (a) Incomes are exponential. Using $1-L(p)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X\mid X\ge\xi_p]\,(1-p)}{\mathbb{E}[X]}$ and memorylessness, derive $L(p)=p+(1-p)\log(1-p)$. Evaluate $L(.5)$ and $1-L(.9)$ and interpret.
> (b) Incomes are Pareto$(a,\lambda)$ with $\lambda>1$, so $L(p)=1-(1-p)^{(\lambda-1)/\lambda}$. Show that $G=\dfrac1{2\lambda-1}$, and check the limits $\lambda\to1^+$ and $\lambda\to\infty$.
> (c) Find the $\lambda$ for which the "80/20 rule" holds exactly — the richest 20% earn 80% of income — and give the corresponding Gini index.
> (d) Prove $L(p)\le p$ for every distribution.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** With mean 1, $p=F(\xi_p)=1-e^{-\xi_p}$ so $\xi_p=-\log(1-p)$. **Memorylessness** ([[05 - Continuous Random Variables|ch. 05]]) gives $\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>\xi_p]=\xi_p+1$, hence
> $$1-L(p)=(\xi_p+1)(1-p)=\big(1-\log(1-p)\big)(1-p)\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{L(p)=p+(1-p)\log(1-p)}$$
> - $L(.5)=.5+.5\log.5=\boxed{.153}$ — **the poorer half of the population earns 15.3% of all income.**
> - $1-L(.9)=1-.6697=\boxed{.330}$ — **the richest 10% earn 33%.**
>
> **The memorylessness step is what makes this tractable**: for any threshold, the conditional mean of the excess is the same, so $\mathbb{E}[X\mid X>\xi_p]$ is just $\xi_p+1$.
>
> **(b)** $$\int_0^1 L(p)\,dp=1-\int_0^1(1-p)^{\frac{\lambda-1}\lambda}dp=1-\frac{1}{\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}+1}=1-\frac{\lambda}{2\lambda-1}=\frac{\lambda-1}{2\lambda-1}$$
> $$G=1-2\cdot\frac{\lambda-1}{2\lambda-1}=\frac{2\lambda-1-2\lambda+2}{2\lambda-1}=\boxed{\frac1{2\lambda-1}}$$
> *(Confirmed numerically at $\lambda=1.5$ and $\lambda=2$: $G=.5$ and $G=.333$.)*
> - **$\lambda\to1^+$: $G\to1$** — maximal inequality. Sensible, since $\mathbb{E}[X]=\frac{\lambda a}{\lambda-1}\to\infty$: the tail carries everything.
> - **$\lambda\to\infty$: $G\to0$** — the distribution concentrates at $a$ and everyone earns the same.
>
> **Note $\lambda=1.5$ gives $G=\tfrac12$, identical to the exponential** — so a Gini index does not identify a distribution; very different shapes share one.
>
> **(c)** The richest 20% earn 80% means $L(.8)=.2$:
> $$1-(0.2)^{\frac{\lambda-1}\lambda}=0.2\ \Longrightarrow\ \frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}=\frac{\log 0.8}{\log 0.2}=0.13865\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{\lambda=1.161}$$
> $$G=\frac1{2(1.161)-1}=\boxed{.756}$$
> **Pareto's original 80/20 observation corresponds to $\lambda\approx1.16$ and a Gini of 0.76** — far above any real national income Gini (typically 0.25–0.60), which is a useful reminder that **the 80/20 rule describes wealth concentration far more extreme than income concentration.**
>
> **(d)** $L(p)=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[X\,I(X)]}{\mathbb{E}[X]}$ where $I(x)=\mathbb{1}\{x<\xi_p\}$ is **decreasing** and $h(x)=x$ is **increasing**. By the corollary to Proposition 5.4,
> $$\mathbb{E}[X\,I(X)]\le\mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[I(X)]=\mathbb{E}[X]\,p\ \Longrightarrow\ L(p)\le p\ \blacksquare$$
> **In words: the poorest $p$ fraction of earners always earn at most a $p$ fraction of income** — with equality exactly when everyone earns the same. **The whole of inequality measurement is the size of that gap.**

## 📝 Summary

- **Markov: $P\{X\ge a\}\le\mathbb{E}[X]/a$ for $X\ge0$.** **Chebyshev: $P\{|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\}\le1/k^2$**, which is Markov applied to $(X-\mu)^2$. **They are guarantees, not estimates** — typically 20–130× above the truth (Exercise 1) — and their value is that they hold for *every* distribution with those moments.
- **$\mathrm{Var}(X)=0\Rightarrow X$ is constant with probability 1** — the fact behind every "equality holds iff…" clause in [[07 - Properties of Expectation|ch. 07]].
- **Weak law:** $\bar X_n\to\mu$ *in probability*, proved in one line from Chebyshev since $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/n$.
- **Central limit theorem:** for i.i.d. $X_i$ with **finite variance**, $\dfrac{\sum X_i-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\to N(0,1)$. **The summands' distribution vanishes from the answer** — only $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ survive, because the proof uses only $L'(0)$ and $L''(0)$.
- **Convergence is fast for symmetric light-tailed summands** ($n=10$ uniforms is already accurate to 0.2pp) **and slower under skew** (Poisson$(100)$ is still 9% off). **Always apply the continuity correction** for integer-valued sums.
- **Finite variance is essential.** The Cauchy average is Cauchy for every $n$ — no LLN, no CLT.
- **Additive mechanisms give normals; multiplicative mechanisms give lognormals** — the CLT applies to the logarithm in the second case.
- **Strong law:** $\bar X_n\to\mu$ **with probability 1**, needing only a finite mean. **The difference from the weak law is "infinitely often" versus "finitely often"**: the weak law permits the average to stray again and again; the strong law forbids it after some finite random time. **It is the theorem that makes "probability = long-run relative frequency" a result rather than a definition.**
- **One-sided Chebyshev, $P\{X\ge\mu+a\}\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2+a^2}$, always beats two-sided for one-tailed questions** — obtained by shifting by a free $b$ and optimising.
- **Chernoff: $P\{X\ge a\}\le\min_{t>0}e^{-ta}M(t)$.** Using every moment plus an optimisation gives **exponential** decay where Chebyshev gives polynomial: $e^{-a^2/2n}$ versus $n/a^2$ for a $\pm1$ walk. **The price is needing a finite MGF, i.e. light tails.**
- **Jensen: $\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\ge f(\mathbb{E}[X])$ for convex $f$** — a convex function lies above its tangents. **Concave utility *is* risk aversion.**
- **Two increasing functions of the same variable are positively correlated** (Proposition 5.4) — the tool that proves $L(p)\le p$.
- **The binomial–Poisson approximation error is bounded by $\sum p_i^2$ ($=np^2$ in the i.i.d. case), simultaneously over all events** — proved by **coupling**, which finally makes the "Poisson paradigm" quantitative.
- **The Lorenz curve $L(p)$ and Gini index $G=1-2\int_0^1L$ are defined as almost-sure limits, so the strong law is what legitimises them.** Uniform $\to G=\tfrac13$; exponential $\to G=\tfrac12$; Pareto$(\lambda)\to G=\frac1{2\lambda-1}$. **Gini does not identify a distribution** — exponential and Pareto$(1.5)$ both give $\tfrac12$.

## ⚠️ Important Notes

> [!warning] A bound is not an estimate
> Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff produce **upper bounds valid for every distribution with the stated moments.** They are therefore *guaranteed* to be loose for any specific one — in Exercise 1, Markov was 134× the truth.
>
> **Report a bound as a bound.** "The probability is at most 16%" is correct; "the probability is 16%" is wrong by a factor of 26. **Use them when the distribution is unknown and a guarantee is needed** — worst-case analysis, algorithm design, capacity planning — **and use the actual distribution otherwise.**

> [!warning] Markov needs non-negativity; Chebyshev needs a finite variance
> - **Markov applied to a variable that can be negative is simply false.** With $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$, $P\{X\ge a\}\le0$ would be absurd. **Check $X\ge0$ first, every time.**
> - **Chebyshev is vacuous when $k\le1$** ($1/k^2\ge1$), and unavailable when the variance is infinite.
>
> **This is a ladder of hypotheses, and each rung buys strength:** non-negativity $\to$ Markov; variance $\to$ Chebyshev; MGF $\to$ Chernoff.

> [!warning] The CLT needs finite variance — and this is not a formality
> **For infinite-variance data the CLT is false**, not merely slow. The Cauchy average is Cauchy for every $n$.
>
> **Practical symptoms of heavy tails:** the running sample mean never settles; a few observations dominate the sum; the sample variance grows with $n$ instead of stabilising. **When you see these, stop applying normal-theory intervals** — they will be confidently wrong. **Diagnose before you standardise.**

> [!warning] The CLT is about *sums*, not about *data*
> **"$n>30$ so my data are normal" is a misreading.** The CLT says the *sampling distribution of the mean* becomes normal; it says nothing about the individual observations, which keep whatever shape they had.
>
> **Two consequences.**
> - A $t$-interval for the mean can be fine while the data are wildly skewed.
> - **Prediction intervals for a new observation are *not* justified by the CLT** — those need the data distribution itself. Confusing the two produces intervals that are far too narrow.
>
> **And "$n>30$" is folklore, not a theorem.** How large $n$ must be depends entirely on skewness and tail weight: 10 uniforms suffice; a lognormal with $\sigma=2$ may need thousands.

> [!warning] Weak law versus strong law
> | | Guarantees | Leaves open |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Weak** | For each fixed $n$, $\bar X_n$ is probably near $\mu$ | $\bar X_n$ straying **infinitely often** |
> | **Strong** | The whole sequence converges | nothing — straying happens finitely often |
>
> **Both are true; the strong law is genuinely stronger** and needs only a finite mean. **The distinction is not pedantic** — the frequentist interpretation of probability needs the *strong* law, because it is a claim about the limit of a single infinite sequence of trials, not about each $n$ separately.

> [!warning] The gambler's fallacy is not refuted by the LLN — it is explained by it
> The LLN says the *average* converges to $\mu$. **It does not say deviations get corrected.** For a fair coin, $\mathbb{E}[|S_n|]$ grows like $\sqrt n$: the absolute lead **increases** without bound, while the *proportion* $S_n/n\to0$ because $n$ grows faster.
>
> **"Due for a head" is wrong; "the excess will be diluted" is right.** The gambler's fallacy substitutes the first for the second.

> [!warning] Additive mechanisms give normals; multiplicative ones give lognormals
> **Ask what operation the mechanism performs.** Measurement error accumulates additively $\to$ normal. Prices, populations, concentrations and file sizes compound multiplicatively $\to$ **lognormal**, with the CLT applying to $\log X$.
>
> **The symptom of getting this wrong is a fitted normal that assigns real probability to negative values** — and lognormal data fitted with a normal will look "skewed with outliers" when nothing is outlying at all.

> [!warning] The Gini index does not identify a distribution
> Exponential incomes and Pareto$(\lambda=1.5)$ incomes both give $G=\tfrac12$, with completely different shapes: the exponential has a light tail, the Pareto a heavy one, and they differ sharply in *where* the inequality sits.
>
> **A single scalar cannot summarise a curve.** Report the Lorenz curve, or at least several quantile shares ($L(.5)$, $1-L(.9)$, $1-L(.99)$), alongside $G$. **The exponential's $G=.5$ — from the mildest possible right skew — is also a warning against reading Gini values as if the scale were intuitive.**
