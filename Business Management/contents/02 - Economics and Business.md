# Economics and Business

>
> **This note gives the business framing, names the owner, and spends its length on the two things Nickels adds that the owners do not have: a worked supply-and-demand example that does not check out, and a pair of Piketty figures nobody divides.**

**§4 — ⚠️ HIS T-SHIRT EQUILIBRIUM IS INCONSISTENT WITH BOTH OF HIS OWN CURVES.** *(He states two points per curve and then asserts an equilibrium at **\$15 and 25 shirts**.)* **Under the linear reading those two points per curve invite, his supply gives 15 at \$15 and his demand gives 27.5 — and the curves actually cross at \$22.14, 47.6% away.** **⇒ TWO POINTS PER CURVE DO NOT DETERMINE AN EQUILIBRIUM. You need the functional form.**

**§2 — ⚠️ HE PRINTS PIKETTY'S r AND g AND NEVER DIVIDES THEM.** *(Return on capital **5%/yr**, economic growth **1.5%/yr**.)* **⇒ capital's share of the economy grows at 3.4483%/yr and DOUBLES EVERY 20.5 YEARS.** **That single division is Piketty's entire argument, and it is the difference between "inequality is rising" and a rate.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. What economics is, and Adam Smith's move

> **Economics** — the study of how society employs resources to produce goods and services for consumption.
> **Macroeconomics** — the economy as a whole. 
> **Microeconomics** — behavior of people and organizations in particular markets.
> **Resource development** — the study of how to **increase resources** and create conditions that make better use of them.

**Nickels frames the whole subject around one contrast:**

| | |
|---|---|
| the pessimistic view | **fixed resources** must be divided among competing groups *(Malthus: population grows faster than food)* |
| **Adam Smith's view** | **create MORE resources** — the size of the pie is not given |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Smith's actual mechanism, and the sentence that matters
> **"The invisible hand" — people working for their own prosperity and growth are led to help others prosper.** **⇒ self-interest produces social benefit not by intention but by the STRUCTURE of voluntary exchange:** a baker who wants your money must first give you bread you value more.
>
> **The business reading Nickels draws is right: this is why "resource development" belongs in a business course at all.** **Businesses do not divide a fixed pie; a firm that raises productivity has created resources that did not exist.** *That is [[01 - Business, Profit and Risk|ch. 01]]'s farm result — output per farmer rose at least 33× on unchanged land.*

### 2. ⚠️ Piketty — the division he doesn't do

**Nickels reports Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (over 1.5 million copies sold): the return on capital runs about **5% annually** while overall economic growth **"rests at just 1.5 percent per year."** He then says Piketty concludes inequality is ever-increasing — and stops.**

**Divide them. If capital compounds at $r$ and the economy at $g$, capital's share of the economy grows at $(1+r)/(1+g)-1$:**

$$\frac{1.05}{1.015}-1 = \mathbf{3.4483\%\text{ per year}}$$

| | |
|---|---|
| **doubling time** | $\ln 2/\ln(1.034483) = $ **20.45 years** |
| rule of 72 | $72/3.4483 = 20.88$ — the same answer |

| years from now | capital's share, indexed to 100 |
|---|---|
| 10 | 140.4 |
| **21** | **203.8** |
| 50 | 544.7 |
| 100 | **2,967.1** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ CAPITAL'S SHARE OF THE ECONOMY DOUBLES EVERY TWENTY YEARS ON HIS OWN TWO NUMBERS
> **That is the whole of $r > g$, and it is what turns "inequality is rising" — which is unfalsifiable as stated — into a rate you can test against data.**
>
> **⇒ and it is the vault's standing move: the source prints both figures in one sentence and the finding is in the ratio.** *(Honest caveat: this treats capital's return as fully reinvested and both rates as constant. Piketty's own argument is more careful; the point is that the **order of magnitude** — a doubling in a working lifetime — follows from two numbers Nickels already gives.)*

### 3. Free markets, capitalism, and the four degrees of competition

| | |
|---|---|
| **free-market economy** | **the market** largely determines what gets produced, who gets it, and how the economy grows |
| **command economy** | **the government** largely decides |

**Capitalism's four basic rights:** to **own private property**; to **own a business and keep its profits**; to **freedom of competition**; to **freedom of choice**.

> [!note] ⚠️ Prices are the signalling mechanism, and that is the claim to remember
> **"In a free market, prices are not determined by sellers; they are determined by buyers and sellers negotiating in the marketplace."** **⇒ the price tells producers how much to produce**, which is why Nickels can say "there's rarely a long-term shortage of goods in the United States." **A shortage is a price that has not been allowed to move.**

**The four degrees of competition:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **perfect competition** | **many sellers**, none large enough to dictate price; products appear **identical** |
| **monopolistic competition** | many sellers, **differentiated** products |
| **oligopoly** | **a few** sellers dominate |
| **monopoly** | **one** seller |

> [!warning] ⚠️ This is where [[Principles of Marketing/contents/07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|Marketing ch. 07]]'s result lands
> **Monopolistic competition is what a differentiated firm is IN**, and the vault has already priced it: **Kotler's Hearts On Fire premium of 15–20% implies a firm-level demand elasticity of 6.71 against INFINITE under perfect competition.** **⇒ the difference between the first two rows of this table is the difference between a horizontal demand curve and a downward-sloping one, and that difference is worth a measurable premium.**
>
> **The depth is [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro ch. 06]]'s.**

### 4. ⚠️ Supply, demand — and an equilibrium that fails

> **Supply** — the quantity of products sellers are willing to sell at different prices at a specific time. *Rises with price.*
> **Demand** — the quantity buyers are willing to buy at different prices at a specific time. *Falls with price.*

**Nickels's worked example, in three sentences:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **supply** (Figure 2.1) | at **\$5** a vendor supplies **5** shirts; at **\$50**, **50** shirts |
| **demand** (Figure 2.2) | at **\$45** buyers demand **5** shirts; at **\$5**, **35** shirts |
| **equilibrium** (Figure 2.3) | *"At a price of \$15, the quantity of T-shirts demanded and the quantity supplied are equal (25 shirts)"* |

**⚠️ Take the linear reading that two points per curve invite:**

$$Q_s = P \qquad\qquad Q_d = 38.75 - 0.75P$$

| at | $Q_s$ | $Q_d$ |
|---|---|---|
| \$5 | 5.00 | 35.00 ✓ |
| **\$15** | **15.00** | **27.50** |
| \$45 | 45.00 | 5.00 ✓ |

$$P^* = \tfrac{155}{7} = \mathbf{\$22.14}, \qquad Q^* = 22.14$$

> [!warning] ⚠️ HIS STATED EQUILIBRIUM FAILS ON BOTH CURVES AT ONCE
> **His supply at \$15 gives 15, not 25 (off by +10). His demand at \$15 gives 27.5, not 25 (off by −2.5). And the curves cross at \$22.14, not \$15 — 47.6% away.**
>
> **⚠️ RULE 4 — can non-linear curves rescue it?** **Yes, and that is why it is NOT filed as an erratum.** For supply to pass (5,5), (15,25) and (50,50) it must rise **2.00 units/\$** then **0.714 units/\$** — a **concave** supply curve, supply becoming *less* responsive as price rises, which is unusual but not impossible. For demand to pass (5,35), (15,25) and (45,5) it needs slopes of 1.00 then 0.667 — an ordinary **convex** demand curve. **The figures are images and do not extract, so the drawn curves cannot settle it.**
>
> **⇒ THE TEACHING POINT SURVIVES EITHER WAY, AND IT IS THE USEFUL PART: TWO POINTS PER CURVE DO NOT DETERMINE AN EQUILIBRIUM.** **You need the functional form.** **Nickels gives exactly two points per curve and then asserts a third that linearity contradicts** — and a student who does the natural thing gets \$22.14.

**The market price is the equilibrium price**, and in the long run it settles there: **above it, surplus pushes price down; below it, shortage pushes price up.**

### 5. Socialism, communism, and the trend to mixed economies

| | |
|---|---|
| **socialism** | the government owns and operates **major industries**; individuals may own smaller businesses |
| **communism** | the state makes almost **all** economic decisions and owns almost all major factors of production |
| **mixed economy** | **some allocation by the market, some by the government** |

**Nickels's honest presentation of the trade-off:**

| | |
|---|---|
| socialism's benefit | **social equality** — free education, free health care, free child care, generous leave |
| socialism's cost | **the brain drain**, fewer businesses created, slower growth |

**The tax figures he uses:**

| | |
|---|---|
| US top marginal rate | **39.6%** |
| France's top rate reached | **75%** *(many citizens paying over 70%)* — **1.9× the US rate** |
| US sales tax range | **over 10% in Chicago to zero in Delaware** |
| socialist VAT | **15–20% or more** |
| US top earners, all taxes combined | **could exceed 50%** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ His conclusion is the one worth keeping, and it is convergence
> **"The trend toward mixed economies"**: free-market countries have been adding social programmes, and socialist and communist countries have been adding market mechanisms. **⇒ neither pure system is observed; the live question is the MIX, not the label.**
>
> *(The depth is [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/00-Index|Macro/Micro]]'s — including the result this book cannot reach: **efficiency is fixed by fundamentals and distribution by institutional detail, and the two are independent.** That is precisely why "which system" and "how equal" are separable questions.)*

### 6. The three key indicators

|                       |                                                                                                                                  |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **GDP**               | the total value of final goods and services produced in a country in a given year                                                |
| **unemployment rate** | the percentage of civilians **at least 16 years old** who are unemployed **and tried to find a job within the prior four weeks** |
| **price indexes**     | **CPI** — monthly statistics measuring inflation or deflation; **PPI** — price change at the **wholesale** level                 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The unemployment definition contains its own most important limitation
> **You must have "tried to find a job within the prior four weeks" to be counted.** **⇒ someone who stops looking leaves the numerator AND the labour force, so the measured rate can FALL because people gave up.** **The rate is a measure of active search, not of joblessness.**

**Four types of unemployment** *(Figure 2.6)*: **frictional** (between jobs), **structural** (skills or location mismatched to demand), **cyclical** (a recession or downturn — *"the most serious"*), and **seasonal**.

**Unemployment, as he reports it: over 7% in 2013, below 5% in 2017, with 4–5% considered full employment.** ⇒ *the ~2-point fall closed roughly 80% of the gap to full employment.*

**The inflation family:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **inflation** | a general **rise** in prices over time |
| **disinflation** | prices rising **more slowly** |
| **deflation** | prices **declining** |
| **stagflation** | the economy **slowing** while prices **rise** |

> [!note] ⚠️ Core inflation, and why the government uses it
> **Core inflation is the CPI minus food and energy**, because *"the cost of food and energy can have temporary price shocks."* **⇒ it is a signal-extraction choice: strip the volatile components to see the trend.** **The cost is that the excluded items are the ones households buy most often** — which is why the headline and core measures diverge when it matters most.

### 7. ⚠️ Hyperinflation, on one scale

**His definition: hyperinflation is prices rising *"by 50 percent a month."*** ⇒ **that compounds to 12,874.6% a year.**

| his figures | annual | monthly | prices over the year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venezuela | 63% | 4.16% | ×1.6 |
| Venezuela 2016 | **481%** | 15.79% | ×5.8 |
| Venezuela 2017 | **1,600%** | 26.63% | **×17.0** |
| **Zimbabwe 2004–09** | **98% A DAY** | — | — |

> [!warning] ⚠️ At 98% a day, prices DOUBLE EVERY 1.01 DAYS
> **Over a year that is a factor of $10^{108}$.**
>
> **⇒ and this is the same episode [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/11 - Money Demand and the Monetary Policy Framework|Monetary Theories ch. 11]] costed**, where Zimbabwe was found to be **~20,000× past the peak of the inflation-tax revenue curve** — i.e. printing money was by then *reducing* the government's real revenue. **Nickels reports the rate; the owner subject explains why a government does it anyway and why it stops working.**

**Rule of 72** — his own example: *"by just 7 percent a year, they will double in about 10 years."*

| rate | rule of 72 | exact | error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | 24.00 | 23.45 | +0.55 |
| **7%** | **10.29** | **10.24** | **+0.04** |
| 15% | 4.80 | 4.96 | −0.16 |

> [!note] The rule is most accurate near 8% and drifts at both extremes — his 7% example is close to its sweet spot.

### 8. The business cycle, and the two policy levers

**Four phases:** **boom → recession → depression → recovery.** **Recession = two or more consecutive quarters of decline in GDP.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **fiscal policy** | the government's efforts to keep the economy stable through **taxation and spending** |
| **monetary policy** | the **Federal Reserve's** management of the **money supply and interest rates** |

**The two fiscal tools are taxes and government spending — and both have a cost:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **national deficit** | the amount the federal government spends **beyond what it collects in taxes** in a fiscal year |
| **national debt** | the **sum of deficits over time** — **now over \$20 trillion** |

**His deficit series: over \$1 trillion for four straight years (2009–2012), ~\$450 billion in 2015, almost \$600 billion in 2016.**

| | |
|---|---|
| \$20tn debt per person | **\$61,538** |
| per household | **\$158,730** |
| at the 2016 deficit, the debt grows | **3.0%/yr** — doubling in **23 years** |

> [!note] ⚠️ Debt and deficit are the stock and the flow, and confusing them is the classic error
> **A deficit is one year's shortfall; the debt is every past shortfall added up.** **⇒ so a *falling* deficit still *raises* the debt** — which is exactly what his own series shows between 2015 and 2016, where the deficit rose from \$450bn to \$600bn and the debt rose in both years regardless.
>
> **After the 2008 crisis the Fed kept interest rates near zero for years** — the monetary lever at its limit, which is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/09 - Tools and Conduct of Monetary Policy|Monetary Theories ch. 09]]'s corridor system and zero lower bound.
