## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. What accounting is, and who it is for

> **Accounting** — the recording, classifying, summarizing and interpreting of financial events and transactions to provide management and other interested parties the information they need to make good decisions.

**Five accounting disciplines:** **financial** *(for people outside the firm)* · **managerial** *(for people inside)* · **auditing** · **tax** · **governmental and not-for-profit**.

> [!warning] ⚠️ The financial/managerial split is a split in the AUDIENCE, and everything follows from it
> **Financial accounting serves owners, creditors, lenders, unions, customers, suppliers, government and the public** — external users asking *"is it profitable? can it pay its bills? how much does it owe?"*
>
> **Managerial accounting serves managers inside.** **⇒ that is why financial accounting is standardised (GAAP) and managerial accounting is not: an outsider needs COMPARABILITY, an insider needs RELEVANCE.**
>
> *(**GAAP** — generally accepted accounting principles. Publicly traded firms are required by the **SEC** to follow them; the annual report is where external users find the answers.)*

**Bookkeeping vs accounting:** **bookkeeping is the RECORDING** of transactions; **accounting also classifies, summarizes, interprets and reports.** **Double-entry bookkeeping** records every transaction in two places *"so they can check one recording against the other"* — his example of the error it catches is transposing **\$10.98 as \$10.89.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Double entry is an error-detecting code, not a bureaucratic habit
> **A transposition changes one total and not the other, so the two disagree and the error announces itself.** **⇒ it catches exactly the class of mistake a human is most likely to make** — and it is the same principle as a checksum. *(It does not catch an error made identically in both places, which is why audits exist.)*

### 2. The accounting cycle

| | |
|---|---|
| **1** | analyze source documents |
| **2** | record transactions in **journals** |
| **3** | transfer (post) journal entries to **ledgers** |
| **4** | take a **trial balance** |
| **5** | prepare the **financial statements** — balance sheet, income statement, statement of cash flows |

> [!note] Steps 1–3 are bookkeeping; steps 4–5 are accounting
> **And the trial balance is the point where double entry pays: it is a summary showing whether the ledger totals agree.**

### 3. ⚠️ The fundamental accounting equation

$$\textbf{Assets} = \textbf{Liabilities} + \textbf{Owners' equity}$$

**Nickels's coffee-shop walkthrough, which is better than it looks:**

| | assets | = | liabilities | + | owners' equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| you invest \$50,000 | **\$50,000** | = | \$0 | + | **\$50,000** |
| you borrow \$30,000 | **\$80,000** | = | **\$30,000** | + | \$50,000 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ BORROWING ADDS ASSETS AND LIABILITIES AND LEAVES EQUITY UNTOUCHED — WHICH IS EXACTLY WHAT LEVERAGE IS
> **Debt-to-equity goes from 0% to 60% in one transaction, and the owner's stake did not change.**
>
> **⇒ that is [[05 - How to Form a Business|ch. 05]]'s Dell LBO in miniature: \$19.4bn of debt against \$5.6bn of equity is the same move at 346%.** **The accounting equation is not just bookkeeping — it is the balance sheet, and the balance sheet is the capital structure.**

### 4. The balance sheet

> **Balance sheet** — the financial statement that reports a firm's financial condition at a **specific time**.

| assets, by liquidity | |
|---|---|
| **current assets** | convertible to cash **within one year** — cash, accounts receivable, inventory |
| **fixed assets** | relatively permanent — land, buildings, equipment *(property, plant and equipment)* |
| **intangible assets** | **no physical form but real value** — patents, trademarks, copyrights, **goodwill** |

| liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|
| **current liabilities** | due in **one year or less** — accounts payable, notes payable, accrued expenses |
| **long-term liabilities** | not due for a year or more |
| **owners' (stockholders') equity** | what the owners own — capital stock and **retained earnings** |

> [!note] ⚠️ Assets are ordered by LIQUIDITY and liabilities by MATURITY — the same idea twice
> **Both lists are sorted by how soon they become cash.** **⇒ which is what makes the current ratio meaningful: it compares the top of one list with the top of the other**, i.e. what will become cash soon against what must be paid soon.

### 5. The income statement

> **Income statement** — the financial statement showing revenues and expenses **over a period of time**, ending with **net income or loss**.

$$\text{Revenue} - \text{COGS} = \text{Gross profit}; \quad \text{Gross profit} - \text{Operating expenses} = \text{Net income before taxes}$$

**Cost of goods sold** includes the purchase price **plus freight** and **plus storage costs.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Balance sheet = a POINT IN TIME; income statement = a PERIOD. The distinction is the exam's favourite.
> **"At a specific time" against "over a period of time."** **⇒ a balance sheet is a photograph and an income statement is a film**, and the statement of cash flows is a third thing again — *the cash consequences of operating, investing and financing activities.*

**LIFO vs FIFO** — Nickels notes that *when* an item entered inventory does not matter for reporting, but **how the accountant records its cost when it sells** does.

> [!note] ⚠️ That is the whole of inventory valuation in one sentence, and the depth is [[Principle of Accounting/contents/00-Index|Principle of Accounting]]'s
> **In a period of rising prices, LIFO charges the newest (highest) cost to COGS, lowering reported profit and tax; FIFO charges the oldest (lowest), raising both.** **⇒ two firms with identical physical operations can report different profits, legally, from this choice alone.**

### 6. ⚠️ Harvest Gold — every figure checked

**Balance sheet:**

| | |
|---|---|
| total current assets | **\$600,000** |
| building and improvements | \$200,000 · equipment and vehicles \$120,000 · furniture and fixtures \$26,000 |
| **total current liabilities** | **\$288,000** |
| notes payable (due Mar. 2023) | \$35,000 |
| common stock (**1,000,000 shares**) | \$100,000 |
| **total liabilities** | **\$613,000** |
| **owners' equity** | **\$213,000** |
| **total liabilities & owners' equity** | **\$826,000** |

**Income statement:** net sales **\$700,000** *(after \$12,000 of returns and allowances)*; beginning inventory **\$200,000**; merchandise purchases **\$400,000**; cost of goods available for sale **\$640,000**; **COGS \$410,000**; total selling expenses \$110,000; **net income before taxes \$68,000**; **net income after taxes \$49,000.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE CROSS-STATEMENT CHECK — AND IT PASSES EXACTLY
> **Goods available for sale \$640,000 − COGS \$410,000 ⇒ ENDING INVENTORY \$230,000.**
> **With a beginning inventory of \$200,000, average inventory = \$215,000.**
> **And \$215,000 is exactly the figure he uses in the inventory-turnover ratio.**
>
> **⇒ TWO SEPARATE STATEMENTS, ONE CONSISTENT NUMBER.** *The vault's rule — always test a table, and verify its internal subtotals before trusting it — applied and passed.*

**⚠️ Three figures he never states, recovered from the ones he does:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **gross profit** | \$700,000 − \$410,000 = **\$290,000** *(41.43% gross margin)* |
| **operating expenses** | \$290,000 − \$68,000 = **\$222,000** *(31.71% of sales)* |
| **tax paid** | \$68,000 − \$49,000 = **\$19,000** ⇒ **an effective rate of 27.94%** |

### 7. ⚠️ Ratio analysis — all six verified, and two that say more than he notices

| ratio | formula | **his value** | **recomputed** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **current ratio** | current assets / current liabilities | 2.08 | **2.0833** ✓ |
| **debt to owners' equity** | total liabilities / owners' equity | 288% | **287.79%** ✓ |
| **basic EPS** | net income / shares outstanding | \$0.049 | **\$0.049** ✓ |
| **return on sales** | net income / net sales | 7% | **7.0000%** ✓ |
| **inventory turnover** | COGS / average inventory | 1.9 times | **1.9070** ✓ |
| **return on equity** | net income / owners' equity | *(stated)* | **23.00%** |

*(Consistency check: book value per share is \$0.213, and EPS ÷ BVPS = 23.00% = ROE ✓)*

**Liquidity ratios** are the current ratio and the **acid-test (quick) ratio** *(cash + receivables + marketable securities, over current liabilities)* — which **excludes inventory**, because inventory is the current asset least certain to become cash.

> [!warning] ⚠️ FINDING 1 — [[05 - How to Form a Business|CH. 05]]'S LEVERAGE RULE, APPLIED HERE
> **Nickels observes that 288% is "a rather high degree of debt" and that lenders "may perceive the firm to be quite risky." He does not say how risky.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | debt-to-equity | **287.79% ⇒ levered 2.88×** |
> | equity as a share of assets | **\$213,000 / \$826,000 = 25.79%** |
> | **⇒ the wipeout threshold** | **A 25.8% FALL IN ASSET VALUE DESTROYS THE OWNERS' STAKE ENTIRELY** |
>
> **⇒ [[05 - How to Form a Business|Ch. 05]] established that the equity share IS the wipeout threshold, using Dell's LBO (77.6% debt ⇒ a 22.4% fall wipes out the equity).** **Harvest Gold's 25.79% is only three points better than a leveraged buyout.**
>
> **A neighbourhood restaurant is capitalised nearer to an LBO than to an ordinary business** — which is precisely what "quite risky" means, stated as a number.

> [!warning] ⚠️ FINDING 2 — ONE RATIO CANNOT BE TRUE OF A RESTAURANT
> **Inventory turnover of 1.907 times a year is $365/1.907 = \mathbf{191 \text{ days}}$ of stock on hand.**
>
> **Food does not keep 191 days.**
>
> **⇒ "HARVEST GOLD" IS A MERCHANDISING ILLUSTRATION WEARING A RESTAURANT'S NAME.** **And Nickels himself reaches for exactly the right analogy in the same paragraph — *"how many times did your employer expect you to turn over a table in an evening?"* — which is a turnover of several per DAY, not 1.9 per YEAR.**
>
> **Not an erratum: it is an avowedly hypothetical example, and every number in it is internally consistent.** **But a student who reads 1.9 turns as typical of food service is badly misled** — *and the lesson is the vault's: a ratio is only interpretable against the industry it came from, which is exactly what Nickels says three times and his own example violates.*

> [!note] ⚠️ FINDING 3 — the 7% return on sales, through [[Principles of Marketing/contents/10 - Pricing|Marketing ch. 10]]'s law
> **Profit change from a price rise is $d/m$. At a 7% net margin:**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | a **1%** price rise, no volume loss | **+14.3% of net profit** |
> | a 5% price rise | +71.4% |
> | a **1% price CUT**, no volume gain | **−14.3%** |
>
> **⇒ so the single most powerful lever on Harvest Gold's bottom line is a price change of one percent** — and the statements this chapter teaches you to build are what make that computable.

**Nickels's own caution, and it is the right one, stated three times:** every ratio must be compared **against competitors in the same industry** and **against the firm's own prior years.** *"A lower-than-average inventory turnover ratio often indicates obsolete merchandise on hand or poor buying practices"* — **average of what, is the question the ratio cannot answer by itself.**
