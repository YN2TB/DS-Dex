# Doing Business in Global Markets

> [!note] ⚠️ Another INTEGRATION chapter — two owners, both deeper
> **[[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/01 - Ten Principles, Thinking Like an Economist, and Comparative Advantage|Macro/Micro ch. 01]] owns comparative advantage** — including the result this book cannot reach: *the allocation is fixed by the comparative advantage and only the SPLIT depends on the price, with the total gain constant at 10 oz across the whole feasible range.* **[[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/10 - Foreign Exchange and the International Financial System|Monetary Theories ch. 10]] owns exchange rates**, including the trilemma derived from interest parity.
>
> **This note gives the business framing, names the owners, and spends its length on what Nickels adds: a market-opportunity argument whose two halves he never joins, and an anti-dumping rule that is not what he says it is.**

**§1 — ⚠️ HIS CHAPTER'S THESIS IS COMPUTABLE, AND ITS TWO HALVES SIT THREE PAGES APART.** *(325 million Americans against **7.4 billion** potential customers; and *"only 1 percent of the 30 million small businesses export."*)* **⇒ 95.61% OF POTENTIAL CUSTOMERS ARE OUTSIDE THE US, AND 99% OF US SMALL BUSINESSES SELL TO NONE OF THEM** — **21.8 customers abroad for every one at home.** **And the leverage follows: since that 1% already generates 30% of ALL US exports, doubling the exporting share from 1% to 2% would add 30% to total US exports.**

**§6 — ⚠️ THE ANTI-DUMPING RULE HE QUOTES IS A MINIMUM-MARKUP RULE, AND IT IS NOT ABOUT DUMPING.** *(Foreign firms must price to include **10% overhead plus an 8% profit margin**.)* **⇒ a price floor 18.8% above cost — which binds on any efficient exporter whose real markup is under 18.8%, regardless of what it charges at home.** **His own definition of dumping is about the home price; the rule never mentions it.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. ⚠️ Why go global — the argument, completed

**Nickels's two figures:**

| | |
|---|---|
| the US market | **over 325 million people** |
| the global market | **over 7.4 billion** in **196 countries** |

| | |
|---|---|
| US share of world population | **4.39%** |
| **⇒ potential customers outside the US** | **95.61%** |
| customers abroad per American | **21.8** |

**And three pages later:** *"In the United States, only 1 percent of the 30 million small businesses export, yet they account for about 30 percent of the total U.S. exports."*

| | |
|---|---|
| exporting small businesses | **300,000** |
| **NOT exporting** | **29,700,000 (99%)** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ 95.6% OF CUSTOMERS ARE ABROAD AND 99% OF US SMALL BUSINESSES SELL TO NONE OF THEM
> **That is the whole chapter, and Nickels never puts the two numbers side by side.**
>
> **⚠️ And the leverage follows immediately.** **300,000 firms — 1% of small businesses — already generate 30% of ALL US exports**, large firms included. If the exporting share rose and new exporters performed like existing ones:
>
> | small businesses exporting | **total US exports** |
> |---|---|
> | 1.5% | **+15%** |
> | **2.0%** | **+30%** |
> | 3.0% | +60% |
>
> **⇒ DOUBLING THE EXPORTING SHARE FROM 1% TO 2% WOULD ADD 30% TO TOTAL US EXPORTS.** *That is the size of the prize his "real global job potential may be with small businesses" is gesturing at.*

**And Bergsten's two figures make it concrete:** the US economy is *"\$1 trillion a year richer because of its integration with the world economy"* — **\$3,077 per American per year** — and *"every \$1 billion in additional U.S. exports generates over 7,000 jobs at home."*

> [!note] ⚠️ Invert the jobs figure to check it
> **\$1bn ÷ 7,000 jobs = \$142,857 of exports per job supported.** **That is revenue per worker in export industries, and it is the right order of magnitude — so the claim is at least internally sane**, which is more than can be said for many jobs-created numbers.

### 2. Comparative and absolute advantage

> **Comparative advantage theory** — a country should sell to other countries the products it produces **most efficiently**, and buy from others what it cannot produce as efficiently.
> **Absolute advantage** — a country produces a specific product **more efficiently than any other country**. *(Nickels notes absolute advantage is rare, and that few countries have it for long.)*

> [!warning] ⚠️ The whole result is [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/01 - Ten Principles, Thinking Like an Economist, and Comparative Advantage|Macro/Micro ch. 01]]'s, and it goes further than this chapter can
> **Nickels states the principle. The owner subject proves the sharp version: *comparative advantage fixes the ALLOCATION, and the price fixes only the SPLIT* — the total gain stays constant at 10 oz across the whole feasible price range.**
>
> **⇒ so "who gains from trade" and "how much trade there should be" are separate questions**, and conflating them is what most trade arguments do. **That is Macro/Micro's organising result — efficiency is fixed by fundamentals, distribution by institutional detail, and the two are independent — appearing here in its first and least developed form.**
>
> **Do not re-derive it here.**

### 3. Importing, exporting, and the two balances

| | |
|---|---|
| **balance of trade** | the total value of a nation's **exports** compared with its **imports** |
| **trade surplus / deficit** | exports exceed imports / fall short of imports |
| **balance of payments** | the difference between money **coming in** (exports) and **leaving** (imports), **plus** money flows from other factors — tourism, foreign aid, military spending, foreign investment |

**His intuition pump:** *"If I sell you \$200 worth of goods and buy only \$100 worth, I have an extra \$100 available to buy other things."*

> [!warning] ⚠️ That intuition is exactly what [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/10 - Foreign Exchange and the International Financial System|Monetary Theories ch. 10]] corrects
> **A trade deficit is not a debt and a surplus is not profit.** **The balance of payments must balance: a current-account deficit is matched by a capital-account surplus — foreigners acquiring domestic assets.**
>
> **⇒ so "we buy more than we sell" is simultaneously "the rest of the world is investing here", and which framing you use is a choice, not a fact.** **Nickels's household analogy makes a deficit look unambiguously bad; the owner subject shows it is an accounting identity with two readings.**

**Dumping** — selling products in a foreign country at **lower prices than those charged in the producing country**. *A predatory tactic, sometimes used to make a quick sale of surplus or to gain a foothold.*

### 4. Seven strategies for reaching global markets

*(Figure 3.4 places them on a continuum of **commitment, control, risk and profit potential** — lowest to highest:)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **licensing** | grant the right to manufacture or use a trademark for a fee (royalty) |
| **exporting** | sell abroad directly, or through an **export-trading company** |
| **franchising** | the franchisor supplies the format; **must adapt to local tastes** |
| **contract manufacturing** | a foreign firm makes goods to specification, under your label *(**"outsourcing"**)* — **worldwide, an over \$300 billion industry** |
| **international joint venture** | two firms form **one new company** to undertake a project *(Shanghai Disneyland: a **\$5.5 billion** venture with the state-owned Shanghai Shendi Group)* |
| **strategic alliance** | a long-term partnership **without** forming a new entity — no shared costs, risks or management |
| **foreign direct investment / subsidiary** | a company **owned in a foreign country by a parent**; the subsidiary operates as a domestic firm and retains **complete control** |

> [!note] ⚠️ The continuum is the point, not the list
> **As you move down, commitment, control, risk and profit potential all rise together.** **⇒ so the choice is not "which strategy is best" but "how much risk am I buying control with"** — the same structure as [[Principles of Marketing/contents/11 - Marketing Channels, Retailing and Wholesaling|Marketing ch. 11]]'s distribution intensity and [[Principles of Marketing/contents/07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s targeting spectrum. **A spectrum in two variables that move together has no dominant point.**
>
> *(Joint ventures have a specific drawback he names: **one partner can learn the other's technology and processes and then compete with them.** That is the cost of the shared-control middle of the continuum.)*

**Multinational corporation** — an organization that manufactures and markets products in **many different countries** and has **multinational stock ownership and management.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ His clarifying sentence is the exam trap
> **"A firm can export everything it produces, deriving 100 percent of its sales and profits globally, and still not be a multinational."** **⇒ what makes a firm multinational is where it OPERATES and who OWNS it, not where it sells.**

**Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs)** — investment funds controlled by governments holding stakes in foreign companies. **Globally about \$7 trillion.**

| Norway's SWF | |
|---|---|
| assets | **\$905 billion** |
| population | 5 million |
| **per citizen** | **\$181,000** |
| share of all SWF assets | **12.93%** |
| share of world population | 0.0676% |
| **⇒ concentration** | **191×** |

### 5. Four forces that affect global trading

| | |
|---|---|
| **sociocultural** | language, religion, values, customs — *and the failures are the memorable part* |
| **economic and financial** | income levels, exchange rates, currency stability |
| **legal and regulatory** | conflicting laws, no unified body of international law, **corruption** |
| **physical and environmental** | distribution, transport, technology infrastructure |

**Exchange rate** — the value of one nation's currency relative to others.

| | |
|---|---|
| **a HIGH value of the dollar** | a dollar is traded for **more** foreign currency ⇒ foreign goods are **cheaper** for Americans, and US goods are **dearer** abroad |
| **a LOW value of the dollar** | the reverse |
| **devaluation** | deliberately **lowering** a currency's value *(Venezuela, 2014 — and Nickels notes it did not work)* |
| **countertrading** | a complex **barter** in which several countries trade goods for goods |

> [!note] ⚠️ Countertrading exists because of a specific failure, and Nickels almost says it
> **It arises when a country's currency is not readily convertible or it lacks foreign exchange.** **⇒ barter is what trade falls back on when the monetary system fails**, which is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/01 - The Financial System and What Money Is|Monetary Theories ch. 01]]'s double-coincidence-of-wants problem arriving from the other direction.
>
> **The exchange-rate depth — including why a peg forces $i_D = i_F$ exactly, and the trilemma — is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/10 - Foreign Exchange and the International Financial System|Monetary Theories ch. 10]]'s.**

### 6. ⚠️ Trade protectionism — and a rule that isn't what it says

> **Trade protectionism** — the use of government regulations to limit imports, on the theory that it helps domestic producers survive and grow.

| barrier | |
|---|---|
| **protective tariff** | an import tax that **raises the retail price** of imports so domestic goods are competitively priced — *"meant to save jobs for domestic workers"* |
| **revenue tariff** | designed to **raise money** for the government |
| **import quota** | limits the **number** of products in a category a nation may import *(the US has quotas on sugar and peanuts)* |
| **embargo** | a **complete ban** on importing or exporting a product, or all trade with a country |
| **nontariff barriers** | not formal, but as damaging — restrictive standards, testing, licensing |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The two tariffs have OPPOSITE success conditions, and that is the point to hold
> **A protective tariff succeeds when imports STOP — and then it raises no revenue.** **A revenue tariff succeeds when imports CONTINUE and pay the tax.**
>
> **⇒ so a government cannot claim both purposes for the same tariff.** **The more it protects, the less it collects.** *Nickels defines both and never notes that they are in tension.*

**⚠️ And now the anti-dumping rule.** He defines **dumping** as selling abroad below the price charged at home, then reports that some rules *"are specific and require foreign firms to price their products to include 10 percent overhead costs plus an 8 percent profit margin."*

| reading | price floor | **minimum markup** |
|---|---|---|
| multiplicative | $C \times 1.10 \times 1.08$ | **18.8%** |
| additive | $C \times 1.18$ | 18.0% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THAT RULE IS NOT ABOUT DUMPING. IT IS A COST-PLUS PRICE FLOOR OF ABOUT 18.8%.
> **His own definition compares the foreign price with the HOME price. The rule never mentions the home price at all** — it compares the foreign price with **cost**.
>
> **⇒ so the rule binds on any efficient exporter whose genuine markup is under 18.8%, even one charging exactly the same price at home and abroad — which by his own definition is not dumping.** **A low-margin, high-volume exporter is caught; a high-margin one selling below its home price is not.**
>
> **This is [[Principles of Marketing/contents/10 - Pricing|Marketing ch. 10]]'s cost-plus pricing written into law** — with the same defect the vault found there: *it ignores demand entirely.*

**The GATT and the WTO:** in **1948**, leaders from **23 nations** formed the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade**, a forum for reducing trade restrictions; the **World Trade Organization** succeeded it as the mediator of trade disputes.

**Common markets** *(also called trading blocs)* — the **EU**, **Mercosur**, the **ASEAN Economic Community**, **COMESA**. **NAFTA** created a free-trade area among the US, Canada and Mexico:

| NAFTA | |
|---|---|
| combined population | **over 475 million** — **6.42% of the world** |
| combined GDP | **over \$22 trillion** |
| **⇒ GDP per capita** | **\$46,316** |

> [!note] ⚠️ The unresolved question Nickels names, and it is the right one
> **"Whether common markets create regional alliances at the EXPENSE of global expansion."** **⇒ a trading bloc lowers barriers inside and leaves them outside, so it is simultaneously free trade and protectionism — depending on which side of the line you are.**

**2. (Advantage and balances.)** (a) Distinguish comparative from absolute advantage. (b) Distinguish the two balances. (c) What is wrong with his trade-deficit intuition?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Comparative advantage**: sell what you produce *most efficiently*, buy what you cannot. **Absolute advantage**: produce a specific product more efficiently than *any* other country — **rare, and rarely durable.**
>
> **⚠️ The depth is [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/01 - Ten Principles, Thinking Like an Economist, and Comparative Advantage|Macro/Micro ch. 01]]'s**, and it proves the sharp version: **comparative advantage fixes the allocation and the price fixes only the split**, with the total gain constant at 10 oz across the whole feasible range. **⇒ "who gains" and "how much trade" are separate questions.**
>
> **(b)** **Balance of trade** = exports vs imports (goods and services only). **Balance of payments** = that, **plus** tourism, foreign aid, military spending and investment flows.
>
> **(c) ⚠️ It makes a deficit look unambiguously bad, and a deficit is an accounting identity with two readings.**
>
> His analogy — *"if I sell you \$200 worth of goods and buy only \$100 worth, I have an extra \$100"* — treats a country like a household.
>
> **But the balance of payments must balance.** **A current-account deficit is matched by a capital-account surplus: foreigners acquiring domestic assets.** **⇒ "we buy more than we sell" and "the rest of the world is investing here" are the SAME FACT stated twice**, and choosing between them is rhetoric, not measurement.
>
> *The correction is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/10 - Foreign Exchange and the International Financial System|Monetary Theories ch. 10]]'s. Nickels's framing is the standard business-text simplification, and it is the kind of thing this integration layer exists to flag.*

**3. (Market-entry strategies.)** (a) Name the seven. (b) What orders them? (c) What makes a firm multinational?

> [!example]- Solution

>
> *(And the middle of the continuum has its own specific cost: in a joint venture, **one partner can learn the other's technology and then compete with it.** Shared control is shared exposure.)*
>
> **(c) Where it OPERATES and who OWNS it — not where it sells.**
>
> **Nickels's own trap sentence: "a firm can export everything it produces, deriving 100 percent of its sales and profits globally, and still not be a multinational."** A multinational **manufactures and markets in many countries** and has **multinational stock ownership and management.**

**4. (Hard — protectionism.)** (a) Name the barriers. (b) What is the tension between the two tariffs? (c) Analyse the anti-dumping rule he quotes.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Protective tariff** (raise import prices to shield domestic producers) · **revenue tariff** (raise money) · **import quota** (limit the number) · **embargo** (complete ban) · **nontariff barriers** (standards, testing, licensing).
>
> **(b) ⚠️ They succeed under opposite conditions.**
>
> **A protective tariff succeeds when imports STOP — and then collects nothing.** **A revenue tariff succeeds when imports CONTINUE and pay.**
>
> **⇒ THE MORE A TARIFF PROTECTS, THE LESS IT COLLECTS.** A government cannot honestly claim both purposes for the same measure. **Nickels defines both and never notes that they are in tension.**
>
> **(c) ⚠️ It is a cost-plus price floor of about 18.8%, and it is not about dumping.**
>
> He defines dumping as selling abroad **below the home price**. But the rule he quotes requires foreign firms to price to include **10% overhead plus an 8% profit margin**:
>
> | reading | floor | markup |
> |---|---|---|
> | multiplicative | $1.10 \times 1.08 = 1.188C$ | **18.8%** |
> | additive | $1.18C$ | 18.0% |
>
> **⇒ THE RULE NEVER MENTIONS THE HOME PRICE.** It compares the export price with **cost**.
>
> **So it catches an efficient, low-margin exporter charging identical prices at home and abroad — which is not dumping by his own definition — and misses a high-margin firm genuinely undercutting its home price.**
>
> **⚠️ It is [[Principles of Marketing/contents/10 - Pricing|Marketing ch. 10]]'s cost-plus pricing written into law, with the same defect the vault found there: it ignores demand entirely.** *(There, cost-plus derived \$20 for a toaster while the profit-maximising price was \$18 — 33% of achievable profit discarded. A cost-plus rule cannot recognise that a low price may be efficient rather than predatory.)*

**5. (Forces and blocs.)** (a) Name the four forces. (b) Explain exchange-rate direction. (c) What does NAFTA's data show, and what is the unresolved question?

> [!example]- Solution

> **(b) A HIGH dollar buys more foreign currency ⇒ imports get cheaper for Americans and US exports get dearer abroad.** A low dollar reverses it.
>

>
> *(**Devaluation** deliberately lowers a currency's value — Venezuela in 2014, which Nickels notes did not work. **Countertrading** is multi-country barter, and it exists precisely when a currency is not convertible: **barter is what trade falls back on when the monetary system fails**, which is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/01 - The Financial System and What Money Is|Monetary Theories ch. 01]]'s double-coincidence problem from the other side.)*

1. **⚠️ Put a market-size figure next to a participation figure before believing either.** 95.6% vs 99%.

5. **Absolute advantage is rare and rarely durable.**
6. **⚠️ Market-entry strategies form a spectrum with no dominant point.**
7. **A joint-venture partner can learn your technology and become your competitor.**
8. **⚠️ Exporting everything does not make you multinational.**
