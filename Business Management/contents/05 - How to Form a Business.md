# How to Form a Business

> [!note] ⚠️ Also new to the vault
> **No other subject covers legal forms of business ownership.** *(The nearest neighbour is [[Commercial Banking/contents/02 - Organization, Structure and Market Entry|Commercial Banking ch. 02]], which owns merger **risk arithmetic** — $\sigma_{comb}=\sigma\sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}$ — but not the legal machinery.)* **Full depth here.**

**§7 — ⚠️ THE DELL LBO SHOWS WHAT "LEVERAGED" ACTUALLY MEANS, AND HE GIVES THE TWO NUMBERS WITHOUT IT.** *(A **\$25 billion** deal with **\$19.4 billion** from lenders.)* **⇒ \$5.6bn of equity controls a \$25bn company — leverage of 4.46× — so equity moves 4.46× the enterprise value in BOTH directions, and A 22.4% FALL IN ENTERPRISE VALUE WIPES OUT THE EQUITY ENTIRELY.**

**§1 — ⚠️ THE CASE FOR INCORPORATING IS IN ONE SENTENCE HE NEVER DIVIDES.** *(Corporations are **20% of businesses** and earn **81% of receipts**.)* **⇒ A CORPORATION EARNS 17.05× THE RECEIPTS OF A NON-CORPORATION.**

**§4 — ⚠️ DOUBLE TAXATION HAS A DROPPED CROSS TERM.** **Effective rate $= t_c + t_d - t_c t_d$, not $t_c + t_d$** — because the second tax falls on what *survives* the first. **At 35%/15% that is 44.75%, not 50% — the cross term saves 5.25 points.**

**§5 — ⚠️ THE C CORPORATION IS THE MINORITY FORM, OUTNUMBERED 11.5 TO 1.** *(4.6 million S corporations out of 5 million corporations = **92%**.)* **The chapter spends most of its length on the form 8% of corporations actually use** — though those 8% are the large ones, which is the honest qualification.

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. The three basic forms, and the number that decides

| | |
|---|---|
| **sole proprietorship** | a business **owned, and usually managed, by one person** |
| **partnership** | a legal form of business with **two or more owners** |
| **corporation** | a **state-chartered legal entity** with authority to act and have **liability separate from its owners** |

**Nickels's one sentence with the whole argument in it:** *"5 million corporations in the United States make up only 20 percent of all businesses, but they earn 81 percent of total U.S. business receipts."*

| | share of firms | share of receipts | **receipts per firm** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **corporations** | 20% | **81%** | **4.05** |
| everything else | 80% | 19% | 0.2375 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A CORPORATION EARNS 17.05× THE RECEIPTS OF A NON-CORPORATION
> **and 4.05× the average business.** **⇒ that is the empirical case for the corporate form, and it sits in a sentence he uses only to introduce Figure 5.1.**
>
> *(Causation runs both ways, and honestly: **large businesses incorporate because they are large**, as much as they are large because they incorporated. What the ratio establishes is that the corporate form is where the economic activity is — not that filing papers creates revenue.)*

> [!note] ⚠️ A cross-chapter tension worth recording
> **His 5 million corporations at 20% implies 25 million US businesses in total.** **But [[03 - Doing Business in Global Markets|ch. 03]] said "30 million small businesses" alone.**
>
> **⇒ the two chapters use different universes** — tax-filing entities here, SBA small-business counts (which include non-employer sole proprietors) there. **Not an error, and neither figure is ever used with the other — but a reader who combines them gets nonsense.** *Recorded in the errata table.*

### 2. Sole proprietorships

| advantages | disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **ease of starting and ending** | **⚠️ UNLIMITED LIABILITY — the risk of personal losses** |
| **being your own boss** | **limited financial resources** |
| **pride of ownership** | **management difficulties** |
| **leaving a legacy** | **overwhelming time commitment** |
| **retention of company profits** | **few fringe benefits** |
| **no special taxes** — profits taxed as the owner's personal income | **limited growth** and **limited life span** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Unlimited liability is the one that matters, and Nickels states it precisely
> **"When you own your own business, you and the business are one."** **⇒ business debts are personal debts, and personal assets — house, car, savings — are available to creditors.**
>
> **That single fact explains every other form in this chapter.** **Limited partnerships, corporations, S corporations and LLCs are all devices for keeping the second sentence from being true**, and each pays a different price for it.

> [!note] The fringe-benefit point is quantifiable
> **He notes elsewhere that benefits "may add up to 30 percent or more of a worker's compensation."** **⇒ a sole proprietor forgoing benefits is forgoing about 30% of a comparable employee's package** — and paying for health insurance and retirement out of the profits that look like a higher income.

### 3. Partnerships

| type | |
|---|---|
| **general partnership** | **all owners share in operating** the business and **in assuming liability** for its debts |
| **limited partnership** | has **one or more general partners AND one or more limited partners** |
| **master limited partnership (MLP)** | looks like a corporation and **trades on a stock exchange**, but **is taxed like a partnership** — it avoids the corporate income tax |
| **limited liability partnership (LLP)** | limits partners' risk of losing personal assets **to their own acts and those they supervise** |

| | |
|---|---|
| **general partner** | an owner who has **unlimited liability** and **is active in managing** the firm |
| **limited partner** | an owner who invests money but **does not have management responsibility or liability beyond the investment** |

**Advantages:** more financial resources · shared management and pooled knowledge · longer survival · **no special taxes** (profits taxed as personal income).
**Disadvantages:** **⚠️ unlimited liability** · division of profits · disagreements among partners · **difficulty of termination**.

> [!warning] ⚠️ The general partner's liability is joint AND several, and Nickels says so
> **"Each general partner is liable for the debts of the firm, no matter which partner incurred them."** **⇒ you are exposed to your partner's judgement, not merely your own** — which is why he recommends choosing partners "as carefully as you would choose a spouse."
>
> **The LLP is the modern fix: it limits your exposure to your OWN acts and those you supervise** — which is exactly why professional firms (law, accounting) use it.

> [!note] ⚠️ The MLP is the interesting one, and it shows what the forms really are
> **It trades like a corporation and is taxed like a partnership.** **⇒ so "corporation" and "partnership" are not natural kinds — they are bundles of three separable attributes: liability, taxation, and transferability of ownership.** **Every form in this chapter is a different combination of those three**, and reading the chapter that way turns a list into a design space.

### 4. ⚠️ Corporations, and the cross term in double taxation

> **Conventional (C) corporation** — a **state-chartered legal entity** with authority to act and have **liability separate from its owners**, the stockholders.

| advantages | disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **⚠️ LIMITED LIABILITY** | **initial cost** and extensive paperwork |
| **ability to raise more money for investment** | **⚠️ DOUBLE TAXATION** |
| **size** | **two tax returns** |
| **perpetual life** | **size** *(can become inflexible)* |
| **ease of ownership change** | **difficulty of termination** |
| **ease of attracting talented employees** | **possible conflict with stockholders and the board** |
| **separation of ownership from management** | |

*(His financing illustration: **10 million shares at \$50 = \$500 million** available to build plants, buy materials and hire people.)*

**⚠️ Now the double taxation, done properly.** Corporate income is taxed at the corporate level, and the dividend is taxed again in the stockholder's hands:

$$\text{effective rate} = 1-(1-t_c)(1-t_d) = t_c + t_d - \mathbf{t_c t_d}$$

| corporate rate | dividend rate | **naive sum** | **actual** | the cross term saves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21% | 15% | 36.00% | **32.85%** | 3.15 pts |
| **35%** | **15%** | **50.00%** | **44.75%** | **5.25 pts** |
| 35% | 20% | 55.00% | 48.00% | **7.00 pts** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE SECOND TAX FALLS ON WHAT SURVIVES THE FIRST, NOT ON THE ORIGINAL AMOUNT
> **\$100 of corporate profit at 35%/15%: \$65.00 after corporate tax, \$55.25 after the dividend tax — a total burden of 44.75%, not 50%.**
>
> **⇒ this is the vault's standing rule again: ALWAYS ASK WHAT THE NEGLECTED TERM IS PROPORTIONAL TO.** **[[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/00-Index|Monetary Theories]] found SIX dropped cross terms in one subject** — Fisher's $r\pi^e$, duration/convexity, debt deflation, interest parity — **and this is another instance of the identical mistake: adding two rates that are applied sequentially.**
>
> *(The cross term grows with both rates, so the error is largest exactly where the stakes are highest — 7.00 points at 35%/20%.)*

**Incorporation costs vary absurdly:** the secretary of state's fee runs **from \$50** (Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Iowa, West Virginia) **to \$775** (Nevada) — **a 15.5× spread for an identical legal product.** *(LegalZoom will file the paperwork "for as little as \$149 plus the state filing fee" — so in Nevada **the state costs 5.2× the service**.)*

### 5. ⚠️ S corporations and LLCs — and who actually uses what

> **S corporation** — a **government creation that looks like a corporation but is taxed like a sole proprietorship or partnership.**

**Its eligibility rules are strict:** no more than **100 shareholders**, all of whom must be **individuals or estates** and **citizens or permanent residents**; **only one class of stock**; and **no more than 25% of income from passive sources** (rents, royalties, interest).

> [!warning] ⚠️ 4.6 MILLION OF 5 MILLION US CORPORATIONS ARE S CORPORATIONS — 92%
> **C corporations are outnumbered 11.5 to 1.**
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | S corporations | **4,600,000** | **92.0%** |
> | C corporations | 400,000 | **8.0%** |
>
> **⇒ the chapter — like every business textbook — spends most of its length on the C corporation, which is the form 8% of corporations actually use.**
>
> **⚠️ HONEST QUALIFICATION, and it matters: C corps are the LARGE ones.** Every public company is a C corp. **So §1's 81% of receipts is earned overwhelmingly by that 8%.** **Counting entities and counting receipts give opposite pictures of the same set** — which is the same warning [[Principles of Marketing/contents/09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|Marketing ch. 09]] gave about never dividing two shares with different bases.

> **Limited liability company (LLC)** — similar to an S corporation **but without the special eligibility requirements.** *(Introduced in **Wyoming in 1977**; recognised as a partnership for federal income tax purposes in **1988**.)*

| LLC advantages | LLC disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **limited liability** — personal assets protected | **no perpetual life** |
| **⚠️ CHOICE OF TAXATION** — may be taxed as a partnership or a corporation | **fewer incentives** (no stock, so no stock options) |
| **flexible ownership rules** | **taxes** — members pay **self-employment tax** on profits |
| **flexible distribution of profits and losses** | **paperwork** — more than a sole proprietorship |
| operating flexibility | |

> [!note] ⚠️ "Choice of taxation" is the whole point of the LLC
> **Every other form has its tax treatment fixed by its legal form.** **The LLC unbundles them** — you get limited liability *and* you choose whether to be taxed once or twice. **⇒ which is why it has largely displaced the S corporation for new small businesses**, whose eligibility rules it does not impose.

### 6. Franchising and cooperatives

> **Franchise agreement** — an arrangement whereby someone with a good idea for a business (**the franchisor**) sells the rights to use the business name and sell its products (**a franchise**) to others (**franchisees**) in a given territory.

| advantages | disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **management and marketing assistance** | **large start-up costs** |
| **personal ownership** | **shared profit** |
| **a nationally recognised name** | **management regulation** |
| **financial advice and assistance** | **coattail effects** — *other franchisees' failures hurt you* |
| **lower failure rate** | **restrictions on selling** and **fraudulent franchisors** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The "coattail effect" is the one that has no equivalent in any other form
> **A franchisee's fortunes depend on the conduct of franchisees they neither chose nor control.** **⇒ you have bought a share in a brand's reputation, which means you have bought a share in everyone else's mistakes.**
>
> **That is [[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|Marketing ch. 08]]'s brand-equity result seen from the inside**: brand equity is an asset the whole network holds jointly, and joint assets have a free-rider problem.

> **Cooperative** — a business **owned and controlled by the people who use it** — producers, consumers or workers with similar needs who pool resources for mutual gain. *(Members democratically control the business by electing a board; many pay no corporate income tax.)*

### 7. ⚠️ Mergers, acquisitions and the leverage arithmetic

| | |
|---|---|
| **merger** | the result of **two firms joining to form one company** |
| **acquisition** | **one company buying the property and obligations of another** |

**Three types of merger** *(Figure 5.8, using his own soft-drink example):*

| | | his illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **vertical** | joins two firms at **different stages of related businesses** | a soft-drink company + an artificial-sweetener maker — *"ensures a constant supply"* |
| **horizontal** | joins two firms **in the same industry** | a soft-drink company + a mineral-water company |
| **conglomerate** | unites firms in **completely unrelated industries**, to diversify | a soft-drink company + a snack-food company |

> [!note] ⚠️ Vertical and horizontal buy different things
> **A vertical merger buys CERTAINTY of supply or distribution; a horizontal one buys SCALE and product range; a conglomerate buys DIVERSIFICATION.**
>
> **⚠️ And [[Commercial Banking/contents/02 - Organization, Structure and Market Entry|Commercial Banking ch. 02]] priced the third one and found it mostly illusory:** combining two firms gives $\sigma_{comb} = \sigma\sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}$, which is **a 29.3% risk reduction at $\rho=0$ but only 2.5% at $\rho=0.9$.** **⇒ conglomerate diversification works only to the extent the businesses are genuinely uncorrelated** — and shareholders can diversify more cheaply by holding both stocks.

> **Leveraged buyout (LBO)** — an attempt by employees, management or a group of investors to buy out the stockholders in a company, **primarily by borrowing.** *(His stated range: **\$50 million to \$34 billion** — a factor of 680.)*

**⚠️ Now the Dell deal, which he describes and never computes.** *"After closing the \$25 billion deal (with \$19.4 billion of the total coming from a group of lenders), Michael Dell now controls a 70 percent stake."*

| | |
|---|---|
| deal value | **\$25,000,000,000** |
| debt | **\$19,400,000,000 — 77.6%** |
| **equity** | **\$5,600,000,000 — 22.4%** |
| **leverage** | **4.46×** |

| enterprise value change | equity value | **change in equity** |
|---|---|---|
| **+20%** | \$10.6bn | **+89.3%** |
| +10% | \$8.1bn | +44.6% |
| 0% | \$5.6bn | — |
| −10% | \$3.1bn | **−44.6%** |
| **−22.4%** | **\$0** | **−100% — WIPEOUT** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ \$5.6 BILLION OF EQUITY CONTROLS A \$25 BILLION COMPANY, AND A 22.4% FALL DESTROYS ALL OF IT
> **Equity moves 4.46× the enterprise value in BOTH directions. That is the entire meaning of the word "leveraged", and Nickels supplies both numbers without performing the division.**
>
> **⇒ and it is the same mathematics [[Commercial Banking/contents/00-Index|Commercial Banking]] runs on: a bank with 9.82% equity is levered about 10×, so a 9.82% fall in asset value is insolvency.** **An LBO is a bank's capital structure applied to an operating company** — which is why LBO'd firms are fragile in recessions and why lenders, not owners, set the covenants.

**Taking a firm private** — management or a group of stockholders **buys all the stock**, so the shares no longer trade publicly. *(Nickels notes Dell as the largest company by revenue to go private via an LBO.)*

*(For scale, the deals he cites: **AB InBev/SABMiller \$52 billion**; **Bayer/Monsanto \$57 billion**; and **over \$223 billion** of foreign direct investment into US companies in 2016.)*

2. **⚠️ Read the forms as combinations of liability, taxation and transferability** — not as a list.
3. **Unlimited liability makes business debts personal debts.**
4. **⚠️ A general partner is liable for a partner's decisions.** Choose accordingly, or use an LLP.
5. **A limited partner who starts managing can lose limited status.**
6. **⚠️ NEVER ADD TWO SEQUENTIALLY APPLIED RATES.** Multiply the survival factors.
7. **The double-taxation cross term grows with both rates** — largest where it matters most.
8. **⚠️ Perpetual life and ease of ownership transfer are the corporation's underrated advantages** — they are why capital is willing to come.
9. **Incorporation fees vary 15.5× for an identical entity.**
10. **⚠️ 92% of corporations are S corps; 8% earn most of the receipts.** Both facts, one set.
11. **The LLC won by unbundling tax treatment from liability.**
12. **⚠️ A franchisee buys a share of everyone else's mistakes** — the coattail effect.
13. **Conglomerate diversification is worth little when the businesses are correlated.**
14. **A shareholder can diversify more cheaply than a conglomerate can.**
15. **⚠️ COMPUTE THE LEVERAGE ON ANY DEBT-FINANCED DEAL.** Equity/deal value gives the wipeout threshold directly.
16. **⚠️ The wipeout threshold IS the equity share.** 22.4% equity ⇒ a 22.4% fall.
17. **Leverage is symmetric — 4.46× up and 4.46× down.**
18. **An LBO is a bank's balance sheet on an operating company.** Expect covenants and fragility.
