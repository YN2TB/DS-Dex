# Ethics and Corporate Social Responsibility

> [!note] ⚠️ THE FIRST CHAPTER IN THIS SUBJECT WITH NO OWNER ELSEWHERE IN THE VAULT
> **Nothing in the other twenty subjects covers business ethics or CSR at all.** **⇒ full depth here, and the cross-links run outward rather than inward — this chapter *supplies* the ethical frame that [[Principles of Marketing/contents/00-Index|Marketing]], [[Commercial Banking/contents/00-Index|Commercial Banking]] and [[Database Management Systems/contents/00-Index|DBMS]] each assume without stating.**

**§4 — ⚠️ THE WHISTLEBLOWER RULE HAS A STRUCTURAL BLIND SPOT, AND IT IS WIDER THAN THE THRESHOLD.** *(Awards are **10–30% of the penalty**, but only for violations **exceeding \$1 million**.)* **⇒ a \$999,999 fraud carries no incentive whatsoever — and worse, since the 10% floor breaks even against a whistleblower's realistic cost at about \$600,000, there is a band between \$600k and \$1m where blowing the whistle is RATIONAL but UNREWARDED.**

**§7 — ⚠️ HE INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMS A FINDING FROM ANOTHER SUBJECT.** *(Replacing an employee costs **6–213% of salary**.)* **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|Marketing ch. 08]] computed that the Four Seasons' six free room-nights break even at a \$2,500 replacement cost.** **⇒ even Nickels's LOWEST figure — 6% of a \$50,000 salary, \$3,000 — clears it, and his midpoint clears it 22×.** ***Two sources, two industries, same conclusion.***

**§8 — ⚠️ "50% OF CONSUMERS ARE WILLING TO PAY MORE" IS A STATED INTENTION, AND THE VAULT HAS PRICED THE GAP.** **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|Marketing ch. 09]] measured stated-versus-actual at 2.41× ⇒ ~20.7% actual.** **And "more" has no magnitude, so it is not a pricing input at all.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Ethics is not legality

> **Ethics** — society's accepted standards of moral behaviour; **what is right and wrong**.

**Nickels's opening move is the one the whole chapter rests on:** *"A society gets into trouble when people consider only what is illegal and not also what is unethical. Ethics and legality are two very different things."*

> [!warning] ⚠️ Legality is a FLOOR, and the chapter's first learning objective says so
> **"Explain why obeying the law is only the first step in behaving ethically."**
>
> **⇒ the two categories cross:** something can be **legal and unethical** *(a payday loan at a lawful rate; a technically-true advertisement)*, and **illegal but widely thought ethical** *(civil disobedience)*. **The set of legal actions is not a subset of the ethical ones, and treating compliance as the objective is the error the rest of the chapter attacks.**

**His evidence that the problem is cultural, not corporate:** **51% of high school students admit cheating on tests**, and plagiarism detection now checks against **more than 40 billion online sources.** *The point being that people arrive at work with habits already formed.*

### 2. ⚠️ The three questions

**When facing a potentially unethical action, ask:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **1. Is it LEGAL?** | *"A society gets into trouble when people consider only what is illegal and not also what is unethical."* |
| **2. Is it BALANCED?** | *"Am I acting fairly? Would I want to be treated this way? Will I win everything at the expense of another?"* |
| **3. How will it make me FEEL ABOUT MYSELF?** | *"Would I feel proud if my family learned of my decision? Could I discuss it with my supervisor? The company's clients?"* |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The three questions are three DIFFERENT ethical traditions, and that is why they work as a set
> **Question 1 is rule-based** — does it satisfy an external standard? **Question 2 is consequentialist and reciprocal** — the golden rule and a check on win–lose outcomes. **Question 3 is virtue-based and publicity-based** — would this survive disclosure to people whose judgement you respect?
>
> **⇒ so an action must pass three independent tests, and each catches what the others miss.** **A legal action can be unbalanced; a balanced one can still be something you would hide.**
>
> **⚠️ And question 2 contains a genuine business claim, not a platitude: "win–lose situations often become lose–lose situations and generate retaliation."** **That is a repeated-game argument** — the counterparty you exploit is the counterparty you must deal with again — **and it is why the ethical answer and the long-run profitable answer coincide more often than the cynical view allows.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Question 3 is the practical one, and it has a name
> **The "publicity test": would you be content for this to be reported?** **⇒ it works because it forces you to adopt an outsider's view of your own reasoning**, which is precisely what motivated reasoning prevents. *Nickels's version — "would I feel proud if my family learned of my decision?" — is the same test in domestic clothing.*

### 3. Ethics begins at the top

**Nickels is blunt: *"Employees must understand that expectations for ethical behavior begin at the top."*** **Management's role is not to publish a code but to be the evidence for it.**

**His scandal roll-call, and it is worth keeping for the scale:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Enron** | made the company look almost **\$11 billion** more profitable; **investors lost \$74 billion**; Lay's estate gave **\$42 million** to victims |
| **WorldCom** | intentionally misstated and understated **\$5 billion** |
| **Tyco** | executives stole **\$150 million** through unapproved loans; Tyco paid **\$2.9 billion** to investors |
| a global financial services firm | hid over **\$50 billion** |
| **Madoff** | defrauded investors of **\$64.8 billion**; sentenced to **150 years** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Enron's restitution covered 0.057% of the loss — 0.6 cents on every \$1,000
> **\$42 million against \$74 billion.** **⇒ criminal penalties in large frauds are not compensation; they are punishment and deterrence.** **Anyone treating enforcement as a recovery mechanism has misunderstood what it does.** *(Madoff's 150 years works out at \$432 million per year of sentence — a number that means nothing except as a measure of how far the loss outruns any punishment.)*

**Insider trading** — using private company information to further insiders' own fortunes or those of family and friends.

*(His Galleon example: Lotus stock **soared 89%** on the announcement, and one trade made **\$468,000** — **implying a position of about \$525,843**. The ring made **\$64 million** overall.)*

### 4. ⚠️ Whistleblowing — and the gap in the incentive

> **Whistleblowers** — insiders who report illegal or unethical behaviour. **They "must feel protected from retaliation."**

**Two protections he names:** the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** requires all public corporations to allow employee concerns to be raised anonymously; and financial incentives let a whistleblower **collect 10–30% of the total penalty for violations that exceed \$1 million.** *(The Enron whistleblower received **\$1 million**.)*

| penalty | award at 10% | award at 30% |
|---|---|---|
| **\$500,000** | **nothing** | **nothing** |
| \$1,000,000 | \$100,000 | \$300,000 |
| \$50,000,000 | \$5,000,000 | \$15,000,000 |
| \$180,000,000 | \$18,000,000 | \$54,000,000 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A \$999,999 FRAUD CARRIES NO WHISTLEBLOWER INCENTIVE AT ALL
> **And the gap is wider than the threshold suggests.** **A whistleblower's realistic cost is roughly a year out of work plus legal fees and industry blacklisting — call it \$60,000.** **The 10% floor covers that once the penalty exceeds \$600,000.**
>
> **⇒ SO BETWEEN \$600,000 AND \$1,000,000 THERE IS A BAND IN WHICH BLOWING THE WHISTLE IS RATIONAL BUT UNREWARDED**, and below \$600,000 it is neither. **The incentive is calibrated for large public frauds and leaves small-firm misconduct entirely unpoliced.**
>
> *(The \$60,000 cost figure is mine and is illustrative — but the structure does not depend on it. Any positive cost creates a band, and the band sits below the statutory threshold for any cost under \$100,000.)*

> [!note] ⚠️ And the incentive alone is not enough, which is why Sarbanes-Oxley exists
> **Money compensates for a cost already incurred; anonymity prevents the cost.** **⇒ the two instruments do different jobs, and a firm that offers a hotline without protecting the people who use it has built the expensive half.**

### 5. ⚠️ Compliance-based versus integrity-based codes

| | |
|---|---|
| **compliance-based ethics codes** | ethical standards that emphasize **preventing unlawful behaviour** by **increasing control** and **penalizing wrongdoers** |
| **integrity-based ethics codes** | define the organization's **guiding values**, create an environment that **supports ethically sound behaviour**, and stress **shared accountability** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The distinction is about what is being managed, and it decides everything downstream
> **A compliance code manages ACTIONS: it enumerates what is forbidden and attaches penalties.** **An integrity code manages JUDGEMENT: it states values and expects people to apply them to cases nobody anticipated.**
>
> **⇒ compliance is enforceable and enumerable, and therefore blind to anything not on the list.** **Integrity covers the unenumerated case and is unenforceable when it fails.**
>
> **⚠️ AND THE VAULT HAS SEEN THIS EXACT TRADE-OFF TWICE, BOTH TIMES IN TECHNICAL SUBJECTS:**
> - **[[Database Management Systems/contents/00-Index|DBMS]] found that SQLite enforces neither integrity rule by default** — foreign keys off, `PRIMARY KEY` accepting NULLs — so **the DDL states a rule the engine does not enforce.** *That is an integrity-based code with no compliance layer.*
> - **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|Marketing ch. 09]]'s R-W-W screen** is a three-question compliance test, and the vault showed it **kills 27.1% of good ideas at 90% accuracy** — the cost of an enumerable rule applied to unenumerable cases.
>
> **⇒ neither instrument dominates. The answer is both, which is what the six steps below build.**

**The six steps in setting up a corporate ethics code:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **1** | **Top management must adopt and unconditionally support** an explicit code of conduct |
| **2** | **Employees must understand that expectations for ethical behaviour begin at the top** and that senior management expects all employees to act accordingly |
| **3** | **Managers and others must be trained** to consider the ethical implications of all business decisions |
| **4** | **An ethics office must be set up** with which employees can communicate anonymously |
| **5** | **Outsiders — suppliers, subcontractors, distributors, customers — must be told about the ethics programme** |
| **6** | **The ethics code must be enforced.** *"It is important to back any ethics program with timely action if any rules are broken."* |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Steps 1, 2 and 6 are the same requirement stated three times, and that is deliberate
> **Adopt it, be seen to mean it, and act when it is broken.** **⇒ a code that is published but not enforced is worse than no code**, because it documents that the organization knew the standard and chose not to apply it.
>
> **And step 5 is the one firms skip: telling suppliers and distributors.** **A supply chain is where most ethical exposure actually sits** — [[Principles of Marketing/contents/11 - Marketing Channels, Retailing and Wholesaling|Marketing ch. 11]] showed a channel commits a firm to other firms for years, **so a code that stops at the company's own payroll covers the minority of the risk.**

### 6. Corporate social responsibility

> **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** — the concern businesses have for the welfare of society.

**Four dimensions, and they are genuinely different things:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **corporate philanthropy** | **charitable donations** to nonprofits — *80% of business leaders surveyed said their companies participate* |
| **corporate social initiatives** | enhanced philanthropy **directly related to the company's competencies** *(GE's **\$17 billion** in clean-technology R&D; Coca-Cola helping **1.2 million women in 60 countries** — 20,000 per country — become entrepreneurs)* |
| **corporate responsibility** | **acting responsibly within society** — treating employees fairly, environmental practice |
| **corporate policy** | the **position a firm takes on social and political issues** *(Patagonia: "A love of wild and beautiful places demands participation")* |

> [!note] ⚠️ Social initiatives beat philanthropy for a structural reason Nickels only gestures at
> **Philanthropy transfers money; a social initiative deploys a *competence*.** **⇒ GE's \$17bn in clean-tech R&D produces something GE is uniquely able to produce, and produces a return** *(the Digital Wind Farm boosts a farm's energy production by 20%)*. **A cash donation of the same size produces neither.**
>
> **That is [[01 - Business, Profit and Risk|ch. 01]]'s five-factors argument again: what a firm has that is scarce is its knowledge, not its money.**

### 7. ⚠️ Responsibility to four stakeholder groups

*(Figure 4.4:)*

| to… | means |
|---|---|
| **customers** | satisfying with goods and services of real value; **honest communication** |
| **investors** | ethical behaviour that protects their money — **no insider trading, no misrepresentation** |
| **employees** | creating jobs, fair treatment, **a workplace worth staying in** |
| **society in general** | environment, community, taxes, employment |

**⚠️ And on employees he supplies a number the vault can use.** *"Replacing employees costs between 6 and 213 percent of their [salary]."*

| salary | replacement cost | range |
|---|---|---|
| \$30,000 | \$1,800 – \$63,900 | **35.5×** |
| **\$50,000** | **\$3,000 – \$106,500** | 35.5× |
| \$80,000 | \$4,800 – \$170,400 | 35.5× |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THIS INDEPENDENTLY CONFIRMS A RESULT COMPUTED IN ANOTHER SUBJECT
> **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|Marketing ch. 08]] found that the Four Seasons' six free room-nights per employee per year — costed at the marginal cost of an empty room — break even at a \$2,500 replacement cost**, given turnover of 18% against an industry 36%.
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | Marketing ch. 08's break-even | **\$2,500** |
> | Nickels's LOWEST figure (6% of \$50,000) | **\$3,000** |
> | at his midpoint (110% of salary) | **\$55,000 — 22× the break-even** |
>
> **⇒ THE PERK PAYS EVEN AT THE VERY BOTTOM OF HIS RANGE.** **Two independent sources, two industries, the same conclusion — which is worth far more than either alone.**
>
> *(And the 35.5× spread is itself the finding: replacement cost depends enormously on the role. A 6% replacement is a queue-worker; a 213% replacement is someone whose knowledge walked out — [[01 - Business, Profit and Risk|ch. 01]]'s fifth factor of production leaving the building.)*

**His growth claim, and it needs a second look:** *"contented employees outgrew their counterparts by four to one for more than 10 years,"* outearning them by **nearly \$40 billion** and generating **800,000 jobs**.

> [!warning] ⚠️ 4× over 10 years is 14.87% COMPOUND OUTPERFORMANCE PER YEAR — and the causation runs both ways
> **For scale: [[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|Marketing ch. 08]] found licensing's fastest-ever decade grew at 29.97%/yr.** **A sustained 14.9-point annual *advantage* over a decade is extraordinary.**
>
> **⇒ AND GROWING COMPANIES CAN AFFORD TO TREAT EMPLOYEES WELL.** **Selection and reverse causation fit these data exactly as well as the causal story, and a raw growth comparison cannot separate them** — which is [[Principles of Marketing/contents/04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|Marketing ch. 04]]'s point that only an experiment establishes causation.
>
> **The claim may well be true. It is not evidenced by this statistic.**

### 8. ⚠️ Does it pay? — and a survey that cannot answer

**Nickels's argument that ethics pays:** ethical firms **attract better employees, draw more customers, and enjoy greater employee loyalty.** *(He cites a fund with assets of approximately **\$40 billion** built on social-responsibility screening.)*

**And the consumer evidence:** *"a recent GT Nexus survey showed that 50 percent of the consumers surveyed were willing to pay more for goods from socially responsible companies."*

> [!warning] ⚠️ THAT IS A STATED INTENTION, AND THE VAULT HAS MEASURED THE GAP
> **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|Marketing ch. 09]] found that a naive top-two-box reading of purchase intent (7.00%) overstates the properly weighted figure (2.90%) by 2.41×.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | stated willingness | **50.0%** |
> | deflated by the measured 2.41× gap | **~20.7%** |
>
> **⚠️ AND THE LARGER PROBLEM: "willing to pay MORE" HAS NO MAGNITUDE.**
>
> **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|Marketing ch. 07]] showed a 17.5% premium is worth +43.8% of profit per unit and lets a firm lose 30.4% of volume before breaking even.** **"More" could be 1% or 50%** — and the survey cannot distinguish a company that would prosper from one that would be destroyed. **A stated willingness without a magnitude is not a pricing input.**

**The Wells Fargo case, costed.** *(Employees opened accounts without customers' knowledge; the company was **fined \$180 million** and **5,300 employees were fired**; trust **fell from 60% before the scandal to 24%**.)*

| | |
|---|---|
| fine per dismissed employee | **\$33,962** |
| trust collapse | **−36 points = a 60% RELATIVE fall** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE FINE IS PRICED AND THE TRUST IS NOT — AND THE UNPRICED HALF IS LARGER
> **[[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|Marketing ch. 08]] measured brand equity directly: the Joshua Bell experiment put the branded-versus-unbranded ratio at 3,437×.** **⇒ so a 60% fall in trust is a balance-sheet event with no line on the balance sheet**, and the \$180 million is the visible number and almost certainly the smaller one.
>
> **That is the honest answer to "does ethics pay?"** **The costs of misconduct are split between a small measured penalty and a large unmeasured one, which is exactly why the measured one is a poor deterrent.**

**Social auditing** — a systematic evaluation of an organization's progress toward implementing socially responsible and responsive programmes.

> [!note] ⚠️ Who audits matters more than what is audited
> **Nickels lists the watchdogs: socially conscious investors, socially conscious research organizations, environmentalists, union officials, and customers.** **⇒ a self-conducted social audit has the same problem as a self-graded exam**, and the value of the external groups is that they are not paid by the firm.
>
> **Compare [[Principle of Accounting/contents/00-Index|Principle of Accounting]]'s treatment of the financial audit, where independence is a legal requirement — social auditing has the same need and none of the enforcement.**

**Internationally**, Nickels notes the difficulty plainly: **standards differ across countries, and multinationals must decide whose standards apply** — and that firms are increasingly held responsible for their **suppliers'** conduct, not merely their own.

> **(c) Its value is entirely in who conducts it.**
>
> **A social audit measures progress toward socially responsible programmes** — but **a self-conducted audit has the problem of a self-graded exam.** Nickels's list of watchdogs is the answer: **socially conscious investors, research organizations, environmentalists, union officials and customers** — valuable precisely because **the firm does not pay them.**
>
> **⇒ compare [[Principle of Accounting/contents/00-Index|Principle of Accounting]], where auditor independence is a LEGAL requirement.** **Social auditing has the same need for independence and none of the enforcement**, which is why the external watchdogs do the real work.

## 📝 Summary

- **Ethics = society's accepted standards of right and wrong. ⚠️ LEGALITY IS A FLOOR, NOT THE OBJECTIVE** — the two categories cross.
- **⚠️ The three questions — is it LEGAL, is it BALANCED, how will it make me FEEL — are three different ethical traditions**, so each catches what the others miss.
- **⚠️ "Win–lose becomes lose–lose" is a repeated-game claim**, not a platitude.
- **Ethics begins at the top; expectations are set by conduct, not by publication.**
- **⚠️ Enron's restitution covered 0.057% of the loss — 0.6 cents per \$1,000.** Enforcement punishes; it does not compensate.
- **Whistleblowers get 10–30% of penalties over \$1 million, and Sarbanes-Oxley requires anonymous channels.**
- **⚠️ A \$999,999 FRAUD CARRIES NO INCENTIVE — AND THERE IS A RATIONAL-BUT-UNREWARDED BAND BETWEEN ~\$600k AND \$1m.**
- **⚠️ Money compensates a cost; anonymity prevents it.** They are not substitutes.
- **Compliance codes manage ACTIONS; integrity codes manage JUDGEMENT.**
- **⚠️ Compliance is enforceable and blind to the unlisted; integrity covers the unlisted and is unenforceable.** Neither dominates.
- **The six steps: adopt, signal from the top, train, provide an anonymous office, tell outsiders, ENFORCE.**
- **⚠️ Step 5 (outsiders) is skipped and covers most of the exposure.** **A published-but-unenforced code is worse than none.**
- **Four CSR dimensions: philanthropy, social initiatives, corporate responsibility, corporate policy.**
- **⚠️ Social initiatives beat philanthropy because they deploy a COMPETENCE, not money** — [[01 - Business, Profit and Risk|ch. 01]]'s fifth factor.
- **Responsibility runs to customers, investors, employees and society.**
- **⚠️ REPLACING AN EMPLOYEE COSTS 6–213% OF SALARY — a 35.5× spread — AND THIS CONFIRMS [[Principles of Marketing/contents/08 - Products, Services and Brands|MARKETING CH. 08]]'S \$2,500 BREAK-EVEN even at the 6% floor.**
- **⚠️ His 4:1 growth claim is 14.87%/yr compound — extraordinary, and reverse causation fits it equally well.**
- **⚠️ "50% WILL PAY MORE" DEFLATES TO ~20.7% BY [[Principles of Marketing/contents/09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|MARKETING CH. 09]]'S 2.41× GAP — and "more" has no magnitude, so it is not a pricing input.**
- **⚠️ Wells Fargo: \$180m fine, \$33,962 per dismissed employee, and a 60% relative collapse in trust that nothing prices.**
- **Social auditing works only when the auditor is not paid by the firm.**
- **Internationally, standards differ — and firms are now held responsible for their SUPPLIERS' conduct.**

## ⚠️ Important Notes

1. **⚠️ Legal ≠ ethical, in both directions.**
2. **⚠️ Apply all three questions; they test different things.**
3. **The publicity test works because it forces an outsider's view of your own reasoning.**
4. **⚠️ Exploiting a counterparty you will meet again is a strategy error, not only a moral one.**
5. **Restitution in large frauds is negligible against the loss.** Do not treat enforcement as recovery.
6. **⚠️ Check the THRESHOLD of any incentive scheme** — the gap below it is where misconduct concentrates.
7. **⚠️ An award compensates; anonymity prevents.** Build both.
8. **Compliance codes are blind to whatever is not enumerated.**
9. **Integrity codes are unenforceable at the moment they are needed most.**
10. **⚠️ A code that is published and not enforced documents your knowledge of the standard.**
11. **⚠️ Extend the code to suppliers and distributors** — that is where the exposure is.
12. **Social initiatives deploy competence; philanthropy deploys cash.** The first is scarcer.
13. **⚠️ Replacement cost varies 35.5× across roles.** Compute it before assuming turnover is cheap.
14. **⚠️ A retention perk costed at MARGINAL cost usually pays** — confirmed now by two independent sources.
15. **⚠️ Discount every stated willingness to pay.** The measured gap is 2.41×.
16. **⚠️ "Willing to pay more" without a magnitude is unusable.**
17. **⚠️ A growth comparison cannot establish causation.** Growing firms can afford good treatment.
18. **The unmeasured cost of misconduct exceeds the measured one** — which is why fines deter poorly.
19. **⚠️ A self-conducted social audit is a self-graded exam.**
20. **A share of enforcement is not a count of frauds.** *(SEC financial-fraud cases: >25% pre-recession → 11% in 2013 → 17% in 2015 — 44% then 68% of the earlier level, but of a share, not a rate.)*
