# Pricing

**⚠️ THE ARITHMETIC CHAPTER OF THE SUBJECT — three source units, and every number in all three verified.** *Kotler ch. 10 (setting price), ch. 11 (pricing strategies), and **Appendix 2**, which is a whole appendix of worked computation.*

**§4 — ⚠️ THE APPENDIX ENDS IN AN ABSURDITY AND CONTAINS ITS OWN EXPLANATION.** Its last calculation demands **20,000,000 units — \$3.36 billion in sales** — for a 25% return on sales, and Kotler asks *"Could it possibly achieve this level of sales?"* without answering. **The denominator is $0.75\times168-125 = $ EXACTLY \$1.00, a knife-edge 0.80% above a vertical asymptote at \$166.67.** **⇒ and at the markup price of \$193.33 the required volume is EXACTLY 1,000,000 units — the very figure that price was defined from. A 13.10% price cut multiplies the required volume by 20×.** ***He computes both numbers fifteen pages apart and connects neither.***

**§8 — ⚠️ THE REQUIRED ELASTICITY OF A PRICE CUT IS $1/(m-d)$.** His 10% cut at a 21% contribution margin needs **+90.91% volume, an elasticity of 9.09**, and he justifies it with *"demand… is elastic"* — **which means only $e>1$.** **⇒ AND THE HARD BOUND: a firm can never break even on a price cut larger than its contribution margin, at any volume whatever.**

**§11 — ⚠️ PROFIT CHANGE FROM A PRICE RISE $= d/m$.** His claim that a 3%-margin firm gains **33%** of profit from a **1%** price rise **verifies exactly** — and it is the mirror of §8. **⇒ pricing is radically asymmetric: +1% earns 33%, −1% destroys 33%, and −10% needs to nearly double volume.**

**§2 — ⚠️ HIS OWN TABLE REFUTES HIS OWN COST-PLUS PRICE.** Cost-plus derives **\$20** for the toaster; **Table 10.1 (all 25 cells verified) shows \$18 earns \$180,000 against \$120,000 — 50% more profit at a LOWER price.** **⇒ cost-plus discarded 33.3% of the achievable profit, and Kotler says "generally, no" without ever showing it.** **The table finds $MR=MC$ by enumeration and never names the condition.**

**§6 — ⚠️ THE VARIANCE BRIDGE HE DOESN'T BUILD: 6.5 + 5.0 + 2.0 = 13.5, exactly the profit miss.** **"Net sales fell \$25 million short" explains only 48.1% of it.**

**§7 — ⚠️ A PRODUCT REPORTING A \$1m LOSS CONTRIBUTES +\$4m**, and \$2m of *pure allocation* flipped the sign.

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. What price is, and the three roads to it

> **Price** — the amount charged for a product or service; **the only marketing mix element that produces revenue**, and the most flexible.

**Kotler's opening claim is quantitative and §11 verifies it:** *"A small percentage improvement in price can generate a large percentage increase in profitability."*

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **customer perceptions of value** | **the price CEILING** | above it, no demand |
| competitors' prices, other external and internal factors | | |
| **product costs** | **the price FLOOR** | below it, no profit |

**Three approaches, and the order of operations is the whole point:**

| | sequence |
|---|---|
| **cost-based** | product → cost → **price** → value → customers |
| **value-based** | customers → **value** → price → cost → product |
| **competition-based** | set relative to competitors' **value delivered**, not their prices |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Value-based pricing reverses the arrow, and that is what makes it different
> **Cost-based pricing ends at the customer; value-based pricing begins there.** *"The targeted value and price drive decisions about what costs can be incurred."* **⇒ that is **target costing** — start from the ideal selling price and work backwards to a cost that permits it.**
>
> **⇒ and it is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s result operationalised: positioning determines what you can charge, and what you can charge determines what you may spend.** **Kotler's *value-added pricing* — "attaching value-added features and services… and charging higher prices" — is the same premium that gave Hearts On Fire an implied firm elasticity of 6.71.**

### 2. ⚠️ Cost-based pricing, and the table that refutes it

**Cost types:** **fixed costs (overhead)** do not vary with output — *"regardless of the level of output, the company must pay these costs"*; **variable costs** vary directly with production; **total costs** are their sum. **Average fixed cost falls as output rises; average variable cost does not.**

**Two cost curves worth distinguishing:**

- **Economies of scale** — the short-run average cost curve is U-shaped in *plant size*. *(Kotler's illustration: a 3,000-a-day plant beats a 1,000-a-day plant, but a 4,000-a-day plant runs into "diseconomies of scale — too many workers to manage, paperwork slowing things down.")*
- **The experience (learning) curve** — average cost falls with **accumulated** production. *(Figure 10.4: **\$10 at 100,000 units → \$8.50 at 200,000 → \$7.00 at 400,000**.)*

> [!note] The implied learning rates are 85.0% then 82.4% per doubling
> **A strict experience curve is constant per doubling; a true 85% curve would give \$7.225 at 400,000, not \$7.00.** *The figures are read off a chart, so this is rounding, not error — but the standard model is $C(Q) = C_0(Q/Q_0)^{\log_2 L}$ and it is worth knowing that Kotler's numbers are approximately, not exactly, one.*
>
> **⚠️ And Kotler names the three risks of experience-curve pricing honestly:** it can give the product a **cheap image**; it **assumes competitors are weak** and will not match cuts; and **a competitor may find a lower-cost technology and start below your curve entirely.**

**Cost-plus (markup) pricing** — the toaster, with variable cost \$10, fixed costs \$300,000 and expected sales 50,000:

$$\text{unit cost} = 10 + \frac{300{,}000}{50{,}000} = \$16 \qquad \text{markup price} = \frac{16}{1-0.20} = \$20$$

**The dealer then takes 50% *on the selling price*, giving a \$40 retail price — which is a 100% markup *on cost*.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Margin on price and markup on cost are different numbers for the same decision
> $$\text{markup on cost} = \frac{m}{1-m}$$
>
> | margin on **selling price** | markup on **cost** |
> |---|---|
> | 20% | 25.00% |
> | 30% | 42.86% |
> | 40% | 66.67% |
> | **50%** | **100.00%** |
> | 60% | 150.00% |
>
> **⇒ "a 50% markup" is ambiguous and the two readings differ by a factor of two at that level.** **Always ask: on cost, or on price?**

**Break-even pricing / target-return pricing:** $\text{BE volume} = F/(P-v) = 300{,}000/10 = 30{,}000$ units. **For a 20% return on a \$1,000,000 investment (\$200,000), 50,000 units.**

**⚠️ Now Table 10.1, which extracted whole. All 25 cells verified.**

| price | BE volume | expected demand | revenue | total cost | **profit** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| \$14 | 75,000 | 71,000 | \$994,000 | \$1,010,000 | **−\$16,000** |
| \$16 | 50,000 | 67,000 | \$1,072,000 | \$970,000 | \$102,000 |
| **\$18** | 37,500 | 60,000 | \$1,080,000 | \$900,000 | **\$180,000** |
| **\$20** | 30,000 | 42,000 | \$840,000 | \$720,000 | **\$120,000** |
| \$22 | 25,000 | 23,000 | \$506,000 | \$530,000 | −\$24,000 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE COST-PLUS SECTION DERIVED \$20. THIS TABLE, TWO PAGES LATER, SHOWS \$18 EARNS 50% MORE.
> **\$180,000 against \$120,000 — cost-plus discarded \$60,000, which is 33.3% of the achievable profit — and the better price is LOWER, not higher.**
>
> **Kotler asks "does using standard markups to set prices make sense?" and answers "generally, no", listing three reasons why it survives anyway** *(sellers know costs better than demand; industry-wide use suppresses price competition; it feels fair)*. **He never points at his own table.**
>
> *(And none of the five prices reaches the \$200,000 target return — the best is \$180,000, 10.0% short. His conclusion is right: the firm must lower costs, not raise price.)*

**⚠️ And the table contains a demand curve, so it can be differentiated.** *(Arc elasticities from his own demand column:)*

| | **implied elasticity** |
|---|---|
| \$14 → \$16 | **0.4348** |
| \$16 → \$18 | 0.9370 |
| \$18 → \$20 | 3.3529 |
| \$20 → \$22 | **6.1385** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Elasticity runs from 0.43 to 6.14 across an \$8 range — a factor of 14
> **⇒ "is demand elastic?" has no answer without saying *at what price*.** **This one product is inelastic below \$16 and violently elastic above \$20.**
>
> **And the optimum satisfies the Lerner condition exactly.** At \$18 with marginal cost \$10: $(P-MC)/P = 8/18 = 0.4444$, so the optimum requires $e = 1/0.4444 = 2.25$ — **and the two arcs either side of \$18 are 0.937 and 3.353, which bracket 2.25.**
>
> **⇒ TABLE 10.1 IS $MR = MC$ SOLVED BY ENUMERATION.** **[[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro]] owns the condition; Kotler owns a numerical instance of it and never names it.** *This is the cash-in [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] promised.*

### 3. Value-based and competition-based pricing

**Good-value pricing** offers the right combination of quality and service at a fair price *(everyday low pricing; value menus)*. **Value-added pricing** goes the other way — **differentiate and charge more rather than cut.**

**Competition-based pricing:** *"the goal is not to match or beat competitors' prices. Rather, the goal is to set prices according to the relative value created versus competitors."*

**Caterpillar's dealer answering "why pay \$500,000 when Komatsu is \$420,000?" — the classic value stack:**

| | |
|---|---|
| price if equivalent to the competitor's dozer | \$420,000 |
| + superior reliability and durability | \$50,000 |
| + lower lifetime operating costs | \$40,000 |
| + superior service | \$40,000 |
| + longer parts warranty | \$20,000 |
| **= value-added price** | **\$570,000** |
| − discount | −\$70,000 |
| **final price** | **\$500,000** |

> [!note] ⚠️ The customer pays an \$80,000 premium and receives \$150,000 of added value — 1.88×
> **The \$70,000 "discount" is the visible concession; the real argument is the \$150,000 the buyer keeps.** **⇒ this is how a premium is *defended* rather than asserted, and it is the practical form of [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s finding that a sustainable premium requires a genuine difference.**

### 4. ⚠️ Appendix 2 — four methods, four prices, and a knife-edge

**HD's cost structure:** **\$10 million** invested refurbishing a facility, **\$20 million/year** fixed costs, **\$125** variable cost per unit, expected sales **1 million** units.

> [!note] ⚠️ The \$10 million is excluded from fixed costs, and Kotler says exactly why
> **"It is not considered a fixed cost because it is not a *relevant* cost."** **Relevant costs occur in the future and vary across the alternatives; the refurbishment is a *sunk* cost.** **⇒ this is the single most useful sentence in the appendix and the mistake it prevents is universal.** *(It reappears in the ROI price, where the \$10m is used — but only to set a **profit goal**, never as a cost.)*

$$\text{unit cost} = 125 + \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} = \$145$$

| method | | price |
|---|---|---|
| **break-even price** | unit cost | **\$145.00** |
| **markup price** | $145/(1-0.25)$, for a 25% return on sales | **\$193.33** |
| **ROI price** | $145 + 0.30\times10\text{m}/1\text{m}$ | **\$148.00** |
| **markup chain** | worked back from a \$300 MSRP | **\$168.00** |

**The markup chain** *(retailers want 30%, wholesalers 20%, both on their own selling prices)*:

| | |
|---|---|
| suggested retail price | \$300.00 |
| − retail margin (30%) | −\$90.00 |
| **retailer's cost / wholesaler's price** | **\$210.00** |
| − wholesaler's margin (20%) | −\$42.00 |
| **wholesaler's cost / HD's price** | **\$168.00** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ HD adopts \$168 — which is 13.10% BELOW the price its own 25%-return goal required
> **Remember that number.** **The appendix's final calculation is entirely a consequence of it.**

**Now the last calculation of section 1. For a 25% return on *sales*, the profit is carried inside the contribution:**

$$\text{unit volume} = \frac{F}{0.75P - v} = \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{0.75(168) - 125} = \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{\mathbf{1.00}} = 20{,}000{,}000 \text{ units}$$

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE DENOMINATOR IS EXACTLY ONE DOLLAR
> **$0.75\times168 = 126$, minus \$125, is \$1.00.** **That is not a coincidence of the arithmetic — it is a knife-edge, and the whole expression has a vertical asymptote at**
> $$P^* = \frac{v}{0.75} = \frac{125}{0.75} = \$166.67$$
> **⇒ below \$166.67 the 25% goal is IMPOSSIBLE AT ANY VOLUME — the denominator turns negative. HD's \$168 sits 0.80% above it.**

| price | $0.75P-v$ | **required volume** | vs at \$168 |
|---|---|---|---|
| \$166.67 | 0.00 | **∞** | — |
| \$167.00 | 0.25 | 80,000,000 | 4.00× |
| **\$168.00** | **1.00** | **20,000,000** | 1.00× |
| \$170.00 | 2.50 | 8,000,000 | 0.40× |
| \$175.00 | 6.25 | 3,200,000 | 0.16× |
| **\$193.33** | **20.00** | **1,000,000** | **0.05×** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ AT THE MARKUP PRICE THE REQUIRED VOLUME IS EXACTLY 1,000,000 UNITS
> **— which is the very sales figure the markup price was *defined* from.** **The circle closes exactly.**
>
> **⇒ THE \$3.36 BILLION ABSURDITY IS NOT A FEATURE OF THE PRODUCT. It is the price decision.** **A 13.10% price reduction multiplies the volume needed for a 25% return on sales by TWENTY.**
>
> **Kotler asks *"Could it possibly achieve this level of sales?"* and moves on to demand estimation.** **The answer is in his own section, fifteen pages earlier.**
>
> **⇒ AND THE GENERAL LESSON: a required-volume formula with price in the denominator is hyperbolic, so near the asymptote the answer is meaningless as a plan and precise as a warning.** **When a break-even calculation returns an absurd number, look at the denominator before blaming the market.**

### 5. Break-even and margin analysis

$$\text{break-even volume} = \frac{F}{P - v} = \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{168-125} = 465{,}116.3 \Rightarrow 465{,}117 \text{ units}$$

**Unit contribution \$43; contribution margin $43/168 = 25.595\%$, which Kotler rounds to 25.6%.**

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| BE sales, units × price | $465{,}117\times168$ | **\$78,139,656** |
| BE sales, $F/\text{CM}$ | $20\text{m}/0.256$ | **\$78,125,000** |
| difference | | **\$14,656 — "due to rounding" ✓** |

**Profit goals slot into the same formula:**

| goal | units | dollar sales |
|---|---|---|
| **\$5m profit** | 581,396 | \$97,674,528 |
| **30% ROI on \$10m (= \$3m)** | 534,884 | \$89,860,512 |

> [!note] ⚠️ Contribution margin can be computed without knowing price or unit cost
> **If variable costs are 74% of sales, the contribution margin is 26% — full stop.** **⇒ that is why the tactic analyses in §8 work on percentages alone**, and it is the most portable technique in the appendix.

> [!warning] ⚠️ The appendix's three sections do not compose, and a student who chains them gets a break-even 16× too large
> **Section 1 says variable cost is \$125 — 74.40% of the \$168 price.** **Section 2's pro forma puts COGS at 50% of sales, which is \$83.87 per unit — 32.9% BELOW the stated production cost.** **Section 3 puts total variable costs at 79% of sales (55 COGS + 10 commission + 10 freight + 4 co-op).**
>
> **Compose them — \$125 production cost *plus* the 24% of price that is commission, freight and co-op — and you get:**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | full variable cost | \$165.32 |
> | **unit contribution** | **\$2.68** |
> | contribution margin | **1.60%** |
> | **break-even sales** | **\$1,253,731,343** |
>
> **⇒ against the appendix's \$78,139,656 — A FACTOR OF 16.**
>
> **Not filed as an erratum:** COGS and "variable cost to produce" are different accounting objects, each section is internally correct, and **no single calculation in the appendix uses both figures.** **But the narrative runs continuously from section 1 to section 3 and invites exactly this chaining.** *(Rule: before combining two cost figures from different statements, check they measure the same thing.)*

### 6. Market potential, the P&L, and ⚠️ the variance bridge

**Total market demand** $Q = n\times q\times p$. **The chain ratio method** multiplies a base by a chain of adjusting percentages:

$$115\text{m households} \times 0.60 \times 0.30 = \mathbf{20.7\text{ million}}$$
$$20.7\text{m} \times 1 \text{ device} \times \$350 = \mathbf{\$7{,}245{,}000{,}000}$$

**At a forecast 3.6% share: $20.7\text{m}\times0.036 = 745{,}200$ units $\times\$168 = \$125{,}193{,}600$, rounded to \$125m.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Kotler switches to UNITS for a stated reason, and it is the right one
> **"The overall market potential in dollar sales can vary widely given the average price used. For this reason, HD will use unit sales potential."** **⇒ a chain ratio in dollars multiplies the uncertainty in every percentage by the uncertainty in an average price.**

**Both statements verified line by line:**

| | **pro forma 2017** | | **actual 2017** | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net sales | \$125,000,000 | 100% | \$100,000,000 | 100% |
| Cost of goods sold | 62,500,000 | 50% | 55,000,000 | **55%** |
| **Gross margin** | **\$62,500,000** | 50% | **\$45,000,000** | **45%** |
| Marketing expenses | 45,000,000 | 36% | 39,000,000 | **39%** |
| G&A expenses | 5,000,000 | 4% | 7,000,000 | **7%** |
| **Net profit before tax** | **\$12,500,000** | **10%** | **−\$1,000,000** | **−1%** |

*(Marketing = \$5m salaries + \$12.5m commission (10%) + \$10m advertising + \$5m co-op (4%) + \$12.5m freight (10%) = \$45m ✓)*

**Kotler's diagnosis: *"One obvious reason is that net sales fell \$25 million short."* ⚠️ Decompose it and that is less than half the story.**

| cause | **\$ impact** | **share of the miss** |
|---|---|---|
| sales \$25m short, at the **planned** 26% margin | \$6,500,000 | **48.15%** |
| **margin erosion** 26% → 21%, on actual sales | \$5,000,000 | **37.04%** |
| **fixed cost overrun** \$20m → \$22m | \$2,000,000 | **14.81%** |
| **TOTAL** | **\$13,500,000** | **100%** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ \$12.5m planned − (−\$1m) actual = \$13.5m. THE BRIDGE CLOSES EXACTLY.
> **"We missed our sales forecast" accounts for 48.1% of a profit miss that also destroyed five points of contribution margin and overran fixed costs by 10%.**
>
> **⇒ a variance that is not decomposed will be blamed on whatever is most visible**, and volume is always the most visible. **The appendix lists all three causes in prose and quantifies none.**
>
> *(Break-even at the actual structure: $22\text{m}/0.21 = \$104{,}761{,}905$ — so "another \$5 million in sales" would indeed have done it ✓.)*

### 7. ⚠️ Ratios, and the allocation that flipped the sign

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| gross margin % | $45\text{m}/100\text{m}$ | **45.00%** |
| net profit % | $-1\text{m}/100\text{m}$ | **−1.00%** |
| operating expense % | $46\text{m}/100\text{m}$ | 46.00% |
| **inventory turnover** | $55\text{m}/25\text{m}$ | **2.20×** |
| **ROI** | $-1\text{m}/(10\text{m}+25\text{m})$ | **−2.86%** |

**But now the appendix's best idea. Net marketing contribution counts only what marketing controls:**

$$\text{NMC} = 100\text{m} - 55\text{m} - 41\text{m} = \mathbf{\$4{,}000{,}000}$$

*(\$41m = sales \$15m + promotion \$14m + freight \$10m + marketing management \$2m. **It excludes the \$5m of indirect overhead.**)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **marketing ROS** | $4\text{m}/100\text{m} = $ **4.00%** |
| **marketing ROI** | $4\text{m}/41\text{m} = $ **9.76%** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE PRODUCT REPORTS A \$1 MILLION LOSS AND CONTRIBUTES +\$4 MILLION
> **Drop it and the company is \$4 million WORSE off, because the \$5 million of allocated overhead does not vanish — it gets allocated somewhere else.**
>
> **⚠️ And it is worse than that: the allocation was \$5m against \$3m planned, so \$2 million of pure bookkeeping turned a +\$1m profit into a −\$1m loss.** ***THE SIGN OF THE REPORTED RESULT WAS AN ACCOUNTING CHOICE MADE OUTSIDE MARKETING.***
>
> **⇒ FULL-COST ALLOCATION CAN KILL A PROFITABLE PRODUCT.** **The decision rule for keeping or dropping is *incremental* — does the contribution disappear with the product? — and allocated fixed overhead never does.** *[[Principle of Accounting/contents/00-Index|Principle of Accounting]]'s territory, and the reason marketing needs its own metric.*

### 8. ⚠️ The four tactics — and the price cut is the one that matters

**All four use one move: $\text{required sales increase} = \dfrac{\text{increase in fixed cost}}{\text{contribution margin}}$.**

**(1) Advertising +50%** *(\$10m → \$15m, a \$5m rise in fixed cost)*:
$$\frac{5{,}000{,}000}{0.21} = \$23{,}809{,}524 \Rightarrow \text{market share must go } 4.00\% \to 4.95\%$$

**(2) Sales force**, by the **workload method** $NS = (NC\times FC\times LC)/TA$:
$$NS = \frac{2{,}500\times20\times2}{1{,}250} = 80 \text{ reps} \Rightarrow \text{hire } 20 \Rightarrow \$1{,}000{,}000 \Rightarrow \frac{1\text{m}}{0.21} = \$4{,}761{,}905$$
*(At \$53,333 revenue per outlet that is **89.3 new outlets, 4.5 per new rep** — against the current 31.25 per rep. Kotler calls it "very reasonable.")*

> [!note] ⚠️ The workload method here just reproduces the status quo, and that is worth seeing
> **Accounts per rep implied by the formula: $TA/(FC\times LC) = 1{,}250/40 = 31.25$. Accounts per rep today: $1{,}875/60 = \mathbf{31.25}$.** **Identical.**
>
> **⇒ the workload method is a CAPACITY calculation, not an optimisation.** **It takes the call plan (20 calls × 2 hours) as given and says nothing about whether that plan is right** — and the entire answer is driven by that assumption.

**(3) ⚠️ A 10% price cut — the important one.**

| | old | new (−10%) |
|---|---|---|
| price | \$168.00 | \$151.20 |
| unit variable cost (79% of the old price) | \$132.72 | \$132.72 |
| **unit contribution** | **\$35.28** | **\$18.48** |
| **contribution margin** | 21.00% | **12.22%** |

**Kotler divides the original \$21m of contribution by 12% to get \$175,000,000.**

> [!note] The 12% is a rounded 12.22%, and the rounding matters
> **Exact: $21\text{m}/0.12222 = \$171{,}818{,}182$, so the required increase is \$71.8m, not \$75m — the rounding inflates it by 4.43%.** *Not filed: he rounds openly and flags rounding elsewhere. But he does not flag it here, and the answer moves.*

**⚠️ Now the general result. With contribution margin $m$ and a price cut of fraction $d$:**

$$\frac{Q_{\text{new}}}{Q_{\text{old}}} = \frac{m}{m-d} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\text{required elasticity} = \frac{1}{m-d}}$$

| price cut | **required volume** | **required elasticity** |
|---|---|---|
| 1% | +5.00% | **5.00** |
| 5% | +31.25% | 6.25 |
| **10%** | **+90.91%** | **9.09** |
| 15% | +250.00% | 16.67 |
| **21% (= $m$)** | **impossible** | **∞** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Kotler justifies the cut with "demand… is elastic", which means only $e>1$. He needs $e>9.09$.
> **His own stated justification is short by a factor of nine.**
>
> **⇒ and for scale, [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] computed Hearts On Fire's implied firm elasticity at 6.71 from its 15–20% premium.** **So this tactic requires HD to face demand MORE price-sensitive than a strongly differentiated premium brand — that is, it requires HD to have no differentiation at all.** *If HD does have differentiation, the cut destroys the thing that pays for it.*
>
> **⚠️ THE HARD BOUND: $d < m$ always.** **A firm can never break even on a price cut larger than its contribution margin, at any volume whatever** — because unit contribution reaches zero and no quantity of zero is positive. **⇒ know your contribution margin before quoting a discount.**

**(4) Line extension HD2** *(retail \$250 ⇒ \$175 ⇒ **\$140** to HD; variable cost \$120 ⇒ contribution **\$20** against HD1's **\$35**, so **−\$15 per cannibalised unit**)*:

| | HD1 only | HD1 + HD2 |
|---|---|---|
| HD1 | 600,000 × \$35 = **\$21,000,000** | 400,000 × \$35 = \$14,000,000 |
| HD2 | — | 700,000 × \$20 = \$14,000,000 |
| **total contribution** | **\$21,000,000** | **\$28,000,000** |

**Net +\$7,000,000 ⇒ launch it, unless fixed costs rise by more than \$7m.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Compute the break-even cannibalisation, as always
> **HD2 breaks even at 400,000 cannibalised units = 57.14% of its volume; the plan assumes 200,000 = 28.57%. A 2.00× cushion.**
>
> **⇒ contrast [[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]]'s Pop-Tarts, which failed by 1.43 percentage points.** **Same formula, opposite verdict — and only the arithmetic tells you which.**

### 9. New-product and product-mix pricing strategies

| **new product** | |
|---|---|
| **market-skimming** (price skimming) | a high price to skim revenues "layer by layer"; needs **quality and image supporting the price**, enough buyers who want it now, high-volume production not cheap enough to undercut, and **competitors unable to enter easily** |
| **market-penetration** | a low price to attract many buyers and a large share fast; needs **price-sensitive demand**, **costs that fall with volume** *(the experience curve)*, and the low price to **keep competition out** |

| **product mix** | |
|---|---|
| **product line pricing** | price *steps* across a line, reflecting cost and perceived-value differences |
| **optional-product** | accessories priced alongside the main product |
| **captive-product** | products that must be used with the main one *(blades, games, cartridges)* |
| **by-product** | price low-value by-products to offset disposal costs |
| **product bundle** | a bundle priced below the sum of its parts |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Skimming and penetration are the experience curve read in two directions
> **Penetration pricing is only rational if costs actually fall with accumulated volume** — which is §2's experience curve — **and skimming is only rational if entry is hard**, which is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s *preemptive* criterion. **⇒ neither is a preference; each has stated preconditions, and they are testable.**
>
> **And captive-product pricing is the sharpest of the mix strategies economically: it separates the price of *access* from the price of *use*, so the profit-maximising razor price can be below cost.** *Kotler notes services do this as two-part pricing — a fixed fee plus a variable usage rate.*

### 10. Price-adjustment strategies

| | |
|---|---|
| **discount and allowance** | cash, quantity, functional (trade), seasonal discounts; trade-in and promotional allowances |
| **segmented** | customer-segment, product-form, location-based, time-based |
| **psychological** | price signals quality; **reference prices** are what buyers carry in their heads |
| **promotional** | temporary reductions to spur short-run sales |
| **geographical** | FOB-origin, uniform-delivered, zone, basing-point, freight-absorption |
| **dynamic** | continual adjustment to customer and situation |
| **international** | adjusting for foreign markets |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Segmented pricing requires four conditions and the second is the one that fails
> The market must be **segmentable**; **the segments must show different intensities of demand**; **the cost of segmenting must not exceed the extra revenue**; and it must be **legal** *(Robinson-Patman)*. **⇒ if the segments don't differ in demand intensity you are giving money away, not price discriminating.**
>
> **And note what the geographic options really are: a choice about who pays for distance.** *(Peerless: \$100 freight to Atlanta, \$250 to Compton. **Uniform-delivered pricing at \$150 makes the Atlanta buyer overpay \$50 and the Compton buyer underpay \$100** — so it is a cross-subsidy that buys geographic reach.)*

> [!note] ⚠️ Kotler's honest warning about promotional pricing
> **"Marketers carpet-bomb"** with discounts, and the effect is to **train customers to wait**. **⇒ a promotion that always runs is not a promotion, it is a price cut with extra steps — and §8 says what a price cut costs.**
>
> **On dynamic pricing he is equally clear: it is "legal as long as companies do not discriminate based on age, gender, location, or other similar characteristics"**, and the risk is damaging the relationship, not the law.

### 11. ⚠️ Price changes — and the asymmetry that ends the chapter

**Reasons to cut:** excess capacity; falling demand under price competition. **Kotler's warning is blunt: "cutting prices in an industry loaded with excess capacity may lead to price wars."**

**Reasons to raise:** **cost inflation** and **over-demand**. **And here is his quantitative claim:**

> *"If the company's profit margin is 3 percent of sales, a 1 percent price increase will boost profits by 33 percent if sales volume is unaffected."*

**⚠️ It verifies exactly, and it is a general law.** Sales 100, profit 3, costs 97. Price +1% ⇒ sales 101, costs unchanged ⇒ profit 4. $4/3-1 = 33.33\%$.

$$\boxed{\text{profit change} = \frac{d}{m}} \quad\text{(no volume change, } m \text{ = net margin)}$$

| net margin | price +1% | |
|---|---|---|
| **3%** | **+33.33%** | *his figure ✓* |
| 5% | +20.00% | |
| 10% | +10.00% | |

> [!warning] ⚠️ PRICING IS RADICALLY ASYMMETRIC, AND THIS IS THE CHAPTER'S REAL LESSON
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | price **+1%**, volume unchanged | **+33% profit** |
> | price **−1%**, volume unchanged | **−33% profit** |
> | price **−10%** | needs **+90.91% volume** just to stand still |
>
> **⇒ the thinner the margin, the more powerful price is in BOTH directions.** **The same fact that makes a 1% rise worth 33% makes a 10% discount nearly unrecoverable.**
>
> **And it explains why Kotler's whole chapter pushes value-added pricing over discounting: the arithmetic is not symmetric, so the two are not opposite tactics of equal standing.**

**Buyer reactions are not straightforward:** a price *cut* may signal that the model is being replaced, that quality has fallen, or that the firm is in trouble; a price *rise* may signal a hot product. **Competitors react most when the firms are few, the product is uniform, and buyers are well informed** — *which is the definition of an oligopoly, and links to [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro ch. 06]].*

**Public policy** *(Figure 11.2)* — **within a channel level: price fixing and predatory pricing. Across levels: retail price maintenance, discriminatory pricing, deceptive pricing.** **The Sherman, Clayton and Robinson-Patman Acts.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Robinson-Patman's three defences are the exam point
> **Price discrimination is allowed if (i) costs genuinely differ by customer, (ii) the products are of different quality and the difference is proportional, or (iii) it meets competition "in good faith" — "temporary, localized, and defensive rather than" aggressive.** **⇒ so the legal test is about *justification*, not about *equality of price*.**

**2. (Hard — the knife-edge.)** HD has \$20m fixed costs, \$125 variable cost, and takes \$168 from the markup chain. (a) Derive \$168. (b) Compute the volume needed for a 25% return on sales. (c) Explain the answer.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Work back from the MSRP, subtracting each margin from the price at that level.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | suggested retail price | \$300.00 |
> | − retail margin 30% | −\$90.00 |
> | wholesaler's price | \$210.00 |
> | − wholesaler's margin 20% | −\$42.00 |
> | **HD's price** | **\$168.00** |
>
> **⚠️ The margins are taken on each level's *own selling price*, so they cannot simply be added** — 30% + 20% ≠ 50%. $300\times0.70\times0.80 = \$168$, a total take of 44%.
>
> **(b) 20,000,000 units — \$3.36 billion.**
> $$\frac{F}{0.75P-v} = \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{0.75(168)-125} = \frac{20{,}000{,}000}{1.00}$$
>
> **(c) ⚠️ The denominator is exactly \$1.00, and the expression has a vertical asymptote at \$166.67.**
>
> $P^* = v/0.75 = \$166.67$: **below it the goal is impossible at any volume, because the denominator turns negative.** **HD's \$168 sits 0.80% above it.**
>
> | price | required volume |
> |---|---|
> | \$167 | 80,000,000 |
> | **\$168** | **20,000,000** |
> | \$175 | 3,200,000 |
> | **\$193.33** | **1,000,000** |
>
> **⚠️ At the markup price of \$193.33 the required volume is EXACTLY 1,000,000 units — the sales figure that price was defined from. The circle closes.**
>
> **⇒ so the absurdity is the price decision, not the product.** **A 13.10% price cut multiplied the required volume by 20.** **Kotler asks "could it possibly achieve this level of sales?" and the answer is in his own section fifteen pages earlier.**
>
> **⇒ GENERAL LESSON: a required-volume formula with price in the denominator is hyperbolic.** **Near the asymptote the number is useless as a plan and precise as a warning — when a break-even returns something absurd, inspect the denominator before blaming the market.**

**3. (Hard — the price cut.)** HD's contribution margin is 21%. (a) What sales increase breaks even on a 10% price cut? (b) Derive the general rule. (c) Is his justification adequate?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Sales must rise from \$100m to about \$172m — volume by 90.91%.**
>
> | | old | new |
> |---|---|---|
> | price | \$168.00 | \$151.20 |
> | variable cost | \$132.72 | \$132.72 |
> | **unit contribution** | **\$35.28** | **\$18.48** |
> | margin | 21.00% | **12.22%** |
>
> Original total contribution \$21m ÷ 12.22% = **\$171,818,182**. *(Kotler rounds 12.22% to 12% and gets \$175m — the rounding inflates the required increase by 4.43%.)*
>
> **(b) $Q_{\text{new}}/Q_{\text{old}} = m/(m-d)$, so the required elasticity is $1/(m-d)$.**
>
> New unit contribution $= P(1-d) - P(1-m) = P(m-d)$; old $= Pm$. Equating total contributions gives the ratio, and $[m/(m-d)-1]/d = 1/(m-d)$.
>
> | cut | required volume | **required elasticity** |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1% | +5.00% | **5.00** |
> | **10%** | **+90.91%** | **9.09** |
> | **21% ($=m$)** | **impossible** | **∞** |
>
> **⚠️ THE HARD BOUND: $d < m$ always. A price cut larger than the contribution margin can never break even at any volume**, because unit contribution reaches zero.
>
> **(c) No — it is short by a factor of nine.**
>
> Kotler writes that *"demand for most types of consumer electronics products is elastic — that is, the percentage increase in the quantity demanded is greater than the percentage decrease in price."* **That defines $e>1$. The tactic needs $e>9.09$.**
>
> **⇒ and for scale, [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] backed out an implied firm elasticity of 6.71 for Hearts On Fire from its 15–20% premium.** **So this cut requires HD's demand to be *more* price-sensitive than a strongly differentiated premium brand — i.e. it requires HD to have no differentiation.** **If HD does have differentiation, the cut destroys the asset that pays for it.**
>
> *(Note also the asymmetry with §11: a 1% price RISE at a 3% net margin adds 33% to profit. Cutting and raising are not mirror-image tactics of equal standing — the arithmetic strongly favours defending price.)*

**4. (Hard — reading a P&L.)** HD planned \$12.5m profit and lost \$1m. (a) Explain the miss quantitatively. (b) Should HD drop the product? (c) What does that imply about full-cost allocation?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Three causes, and the sales shortfall is under half of it.**
>
> | cause | \$ | share |
> |---|---|---|
> | sales \$25m short at the **planned** 26% margin | 6,500,000 | **48.15%** |
> | **margin erosion** 26% → 21% on actual sales | 5,000,000 | **37.04%** |
> | **fixed cost overrun** \$20m → \$22m | 2,000,000 | **14.81%** |
> | **total** | **13,500,000** | 100% |
>
> **⚠️ And \$12.5m − (−\$1m) = \$13.5m, so the bridge closes exactly.**
>
> **⇒ Kotler's "one obvious reason is that net sales fell \$25 million short" is true and explains 48.1%.** **A variance that is not decomposed gets blamed on whatever is most visible, and volume is always most visible.**
>
> *(Worth adding: HD's **market share was HIGHER than forecast** — 4% against 3.6%. **It beat its share target and missed its sales target**, because the whole market came in at \$2.5bn against an estimated \$7.245bn potential. **⇒ share and sales can move in opposite directions, and only one of them is money.**)*
>
> **(b) No — dropping it makes the company \$4 million WORSE off.**
> $$\text{NMC} = 100\text{m} - 55\text{m} - 41\text{m} = \$4{,}000{,}000$$
> **The \$41m counts only what marketing controls and excludes the \$5m of allocated indirect overhead — which does not disappear if the product does. It gets allocated somewhere else.**
>
> *(Marketing ROS $=4/100=4\%$; marketing ROI $=4/41=9.76\%$.)*
>
> **(c) ⚠️ That full-cost allocation can kill a profitable product — and here it nearly did, on a \$2m bookkeeping change.**
>
> **The allocation was \$5m against \$3m planned. Had the planned figure been used, the product would have reported +\$1m instead of −\$1m.** ***THE SIGN OF THE RESULT WAS AN ACCOUNTING CHOICE MADE OUTSIDE MARKETING.***
>
> **⇒ the keep-or-drop decision is incremental: does this cash flow disappear with the product?** Contribution does; allocated overhead does not. **That is precisely why NMC exists as a separate metric** — [[Principle of Accounting/contents/00-Index|Principle of Accounting]]'s absorption-costing problem, arriving in marketing.

- **⚠️ Markup on cost $= m/(1-m)$** — "50%" means 100% on cost or 50% on price. **Always ask which.**
