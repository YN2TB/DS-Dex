# Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning

**The chapter [[00-Index]] flagged for the subject's biggest cross-subject result.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ KOTLER SAYS *DIFFERENTIATE* AND NEVER SAYS WHY IT PAYS
> **[[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro ch. 05–06]] says why it pays and never calls it *positioning*.** **His Hearts On Fire figure is the number that joins them — and §5 does the joining.**

**Four results.**

**§5 — ⚠️ a price premium over a comparable product IS a downward-sloping firm demand curve.** *(Computed from his own "15 to 20 percent premium": **Hearts On Fire's differentiation buys a firm-level demand elasticity of about 6.7 instead of infinity.**)* **⇒ Kotler's whole chapter is an instruction manual for entering monopolistic competition.**

**§5 — what the premium is worth, and what it costs.** *(Computed: a **17.5% price premium is a 43.8% profit-per-unit premium**, because it falls straight through to margin — **and it lets you lose 30.4% of your volume and break even.**)* **That break-even column is the arithmetic behind niche marketing.**

**§4 — ⚠️ one cell of the value-proposition grid is a trap.** **"More for less" gives up nothing, so it is not a positioning — it is a temporary state of superior efficiency.**

**§7 — Kotler's five segmentation criteria are the acceptance test for a clustering algorithm's output.** **The algorithm proposes; the five requirements dispose.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. The four bases of segmentation

| base | variables |
|---|---|
| **geographic** | nations, regions, states, counties, cities, neighbourhoods |
| **demographic** | age, life-cycle stage, gender, income, occupation, education, religion, ethnicity, generation |
| **psychographic** | lifestyle, personality |
| **behavioral** | occasions, **benefits sought**, user status, **usage rate**, **loyalty status** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Demographic is the most used and the least explanatory
> **Kotler's own reason for its popularity is telling: demographic variables are "easier to measure than most other types of variables."** **That is a *measurement* convenience, not a claim that they cause behaviour.**
>
> **⚠️ Behavioral is the one that predicts**, because it segments on *what people do* rather than who they are — and Kotler concedes it: **"many marketers believe that behavioral variables are the best starting point."**
>
> *(And **benefits sought** is the sharpest of the behavioural bases: grouping by the benefit the buyer wants is the closest thing in the chapter to segmenting on the actual decision rule.)*

### 2. The five requirements for effective segmentation

| | |
|---|---|
| **measurable** | size, purchasing power and profiles **can be measured** |
| **accessible** | the segment can be effectively **reached and served** |
| **substantial** | **large or profitable enough** to serve |
| **differentiable** | conceptually distinguishable **and respond differently** to marketing |
| **actionable** | effective programs **can be designed** to attract and serve it |

> [!warning] ⚠️ These are a filter, not a checklist — and each removes a different failure
> | if it fails | |
> |---|---|
> | measurable | you cannot size the prize |
> | accessible | you cannot reach them — no media, no channel |
> | substantial | the prize is too small to pay for the effort |
> | **differentiable** | **the segments are not really different, so separate programmes are wasted** |
> | actionable | you know who they are and can do nothing about it |
>
> **⚠️ Differentiable is a *causal* requirement and the other four are descriptive.** **It is the only one that cannot be established from the segmentation data itself** — knowing that two segments *respond differently* needs [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s causal research. **This is the criterion that kills most statistically-derived segmentations** *(see §7)*.

### 3. The four targeting strategies — a coverage/cost spectrum

| strategy | what it does | |
|---|---|---|
| **undifferentiated (mass)** | ignore segment differences, one offer for the whole market | lowest cost, highest volume |
| **differentiated (segmented)** | target several segments with separate offers | more sales, **more cost** |
| **concentrated (niche)** | a large share of **one or a few small** segments | efficient, and **higher risk** |
| **micromarketing** | tailor to specific individuals and locations | **local** and **individual** marketing |

> [!warning] ⚠️ It is a spectrum in two variables at once
> **As you move down, the *match to customer needs* improves and the *cost of serving* rises.** **There is no dominant choice** — the right point depends on whether the extra margin from a better match exceeds the extra cost. **§5 computes that.**

**Kotler's own cautions:** **concentrated marketing "can be highly profitable. At the same time, it involves higher-than-normal risks"** *(the segment can sour, or a larger competitor can enter it)*; **local marketing "can drive up manufacturing and marketing costs by reducing economies of scale."**

> [!note] ⚠️ So the chapter's real question is not "which strategy is best"
> **It is *how far down this list the arithmetic justifies going*.**

### 4. ⚠️ The value-proposition grid — and one cell is a trap

*(Figure 7.4, recovered — benefits × price:)*

| | **price: more** | **price: the same** | **price: less** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **benefits: more** | **more for more** ✓ | **more for the same** ✓ | **more for less** ⚠️ |
| **benefits: the same** | *losing* | *marginal* | **the same for less** ✓ |
| **benefits: less** | *losing* | *losing* | **less for much less** ✓ |

**Five winning cells (top row and right column), three losing (lower left), one marginal (centre).**

- **More for more** — the most upscale product at a higher price. *(Four Seasons, Patek Philippe, Louis Vuitton, Mercedes. **Hearts On Fire: "The World's Most Perfectly Cut Diamond", commanding a 15–20% premium.**)*
- **More for the same** — attack a rival's proposition. *(Target as the "upscale discounter".)*
- **The same for less** — same brands, deep discounts. *(Walmart; category killers.)*
- **Less for much less** — meeting lower performance requirements at much lower cost.

> [!warning] ⚠️ "More for less" is not like the other four, and Kotler treats it as though it were
> **Each of the other four is a *trade*: you give up price to gain benefits, or benefits to gain price.** **More-for-less gives up nothing.**
>
> **⇒ so it is not a positioning at all — it is a temporary state of superior efficiency.** **Any firm able to sustain it would take the entire market.** **It belongs on the diagram as an aspiration, not a strategy.**

> [!note] And his warning about more-for-more is the general form of the same point
> **It "often invites imitators who claim the same quality but at a lower price"** — Starbucks now faces Dunkin' Donuts and McDonald's. **Competitors attack by moving *left along the top row*, which is exactly what makes the top-right cell unstable.**

### 5. ⚠️ Why positioning pays — the cross-subject result

**Kotler: Hearts On Fire "commands a 15 to 20 percent *price premium* over *comparable* competing diamonds."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ A price premium over a comparable product IS a downward-sloping firm demand curve
> **Under perfect competition the firm's demand is *horizontal*: charge a cent more and you sell nothing, so the sustainable premium is exactly zero.** **Differentiation is what tilts it.**

*(Computed via the Lerner index $(P-MC)/P=1/e$, taking the undifferentiated rival's price as the competitive benchmark:)*

| premium | $(P-MC)/P$ | **implied firm elasticity** |
|---|---|---|
| **0%** | 0% | **infinite** — perfect competition |
| 5% | 4.8% | 21.00 |
| 15% | 13.0% | 7.67 |
| **17.5%** | **14.9%** | **6.71** |
| 20% | 16.7% | 6.00 |
| 50% | 33.3% | 3.00 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Hearts On Fire's differentiation buys it a firm-level demand elasticity of about 6.7 instead of infinity
> **That is the entire economic content of "positioning", and neither source states it.**
>
> **[[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro]]'s name for the result is *monopolistic competition*: many firms, differentiated products, each facing a downward-sloping demand curve and pricing above marginal cost.** **⇒ Kotler's whole chapter is an instruction manual for entering that market structure.**

**What the premium is worth** *(computed from price 100, cost 60, a 40% gross margin — the premium falls straight through to margin)*:

| premium | new price | margin \$ | margin % | **change in profit per unit** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 105.0 | 45.0 | 42.9% | **+12.5%** |
| 15% | 115.0 | 55.0 | 47.8% | +37.5% |
| **17.5%** | 117.5 | **57.5** | 48.9% | **+43.8%** |
| 20% | 120.0 | 60.0 | 50.0% | **+50.0%** |

> [!note] ⚠️ A 17.5% price premium is a 43.8% profit-per-unit premium
> **That is why positioning is worth spending on.**

**And the trade-off — differentiation narrows the market:**

| premium | new margin | break-even volume | **you can lose up to** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 45.0 | 88.9% | 11.1% |
| 15% | 55.0 | 72.7% | 27.3% |
| **17.5%** | **57.5** | **69.6%** | **30.4% of volume** |
| 30% | 70.0 | 57.1% | **42.9%** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ At a 17.5% premium you can lose 30.4% of your volume and be no worse off
> **That is the arithmetic behind *concentrated (niche) marketing*: a small share of a market at a high margin can beat a large share at a low one — and this table says exactly how small.**
>
> **⇒ it answers §3's real question.** **Moving down the targeting spectrum is justified when the margin gain from a better match exceeds the volume and cost lost by serving fewer people**, and the break-even column is the test.
>
> **And it is [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s contribution arithmetic in a new place.** There, *contribution to growth = share × growth rate*; here, *profit = margin × volume*. **⚠️ Both times the error is quoting one factor alone.**

### 6. Differentiation, positioning maps, and the statement

| step | | about |
|---|---|---|
| **segmentation** | dividing the market into groups | **buyers** |
| **targeting** | **choosing** which groups to enter | **a decision** |
| **positioning** | the place the product occupies **in the minds** of target consumers, relative to competitors | **minds** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The exam distinction, and the one most often muddled
> **Segmentation and targeting happen *in the market*; positioning happens *in the consumer's head*.** **A firm *chooses* its target and only *influences* its position.**

**Positioning maps** *(perceptual maps)* plot brands on two attributes with circle size for market share.

> [!note] ⚠️ Their value is the empty space — with one caveat Kotler does not give
> **A gap on the map is a possible position — but only if the gap exists because nobody *thought of* it, not because nobody *wants* it.** **That distinction is where positioning strategies die.**

**A difference is worth promoting if it is: important, distinctive, superior, communicable, *preemptive*, affordable, profitable.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Preemptive is the one with teeth
> **"Competitors cannot easily copy the difference."** **A difference that is important, distinctive and superior but *not* preemptive gives you a premium until someone imitates** — which is precisely Kotler's own more-for-more warning, and why §5's elasticity is a temporary asset rather than a permanent one.

**The positioning statement, in his fixed format:**

> **"To *(target segment and need)* our *(brand)* is *(concept)* that *(point of difference)*."**

> [!note] ⚠️ The format forces four elements: who, what need, what category, why different
> **Omitting the *category* is the commonest failure** — a brand that does not say what it *is* cannot be compared favourably to anything.

### 7. ⚠️ Enrichment — segmentation as a clustering problem

> [!warning] Flagged as an addition — Kotler (2017) predates the standard practice
> *(Per [[00-Index]]'s enrichment plan.)*

**RFM is the canonical behavioural segmentation, and it is Kotler's *behavioral* base made computable:**

| | | Kotler's variable |
|---|---|---|
| **recency** | how long since the last purchase | *occasions* |
| **frequency** | how often they buy | *usage rate* |
| **monetary** | how much they spend | *user status* |

**Each customer becomes a point in three dimensions, and segments are *clusters*.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Three warnings that follow from this chapter's own criteria
> **(1) A clustering algorithm always returns clusters.** **$k$-means with $k=5$ gives five segments on random data.** **⇒ §2's *differentiable* test — do they *respond differently*? — is not satisfied by the clustering.** **It requires [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s causal research.**
>
> **(2) The clusters depend on the scaling.** **Monetary value has a far larger numeric range than recency in days, so an unscaled distance metric segments almost entirely on spend.** **⇒ the segments are an artefact of the *units* unless the features are standardised** — [[Data Preparation and Visualization/contents/00-Index|Data Preparation and Visualization]]'s territory.
>
> **(3) Substantial and actionable still bind.** **An algorithm will happily return a segment of 40 customers, or one defined by a variable you cannot target on.**

> [!note] ⚠️ So Kotler's five criteria are not made obsolete by the algorithm — they are the acceptance test for its output
> **The algorithm proposes; the five requirements dispose.** **That is the most useful thing this chapter gives a Data Science reader.**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Segmentation.)** (a) Give the four bases. (b) Why is demographic most used? (c) Give the five requirements and say which is hardest.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Geographic, demographic, psychographic, behavioral.**
>
> **(b) Because it is easy to measure, not because it explains.**
>
> **Kotler's own justification is that demographic variables are "easier to measure than most other types of variables."** **⚠️ That is a *measurement* convenience masquerading as a segmentation rationale.**
>
> **⇒ behavioral segmentation predicts better because it groups on what people *do*** — occasions, benefits sought, usage rate, loyalty — **and Kotler concedes it: "many marketers believe that behavioral variables are the best starting point."**
>
> *(The deepest of these is **benefits sought**, which comes closest to segmenting on the buyer's actual decision rule rather than on a correlate of it. Two people of identical demographics can want opposite benefits from the same category.)*
>
> **(c) Measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable, actionable — and *differentiable* is hardest.**
>
> **The other four are descriptive: you can check them from the segmentation data itself.** **⚠️ Differentiable is *causal* — it asks whether the segments *respond differently to marketing*, which no amount of describing them can establish.**
>
> **⇒ it requires [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s causal research: an experiment in which segments receive different treatments.** **And §7 shows this is exactly the criterion that statistically-derived segmentations fail**, because a clustering algorithm demonstrates that customers *differ* and says nothing about whether they *respond* differently.

**2. (Hard — targeting.)** (a) Give the four strategies. (b) What varies along the spectrum? (c) How far down should a firm go?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Undifferentiated (mass), differentiated (segmented), concentrated (niche), micromarketing (local and individual).**
>
> **(b) Two things move together, in opposite directions.**
>
> **As you move down: the *match to customer needs* improves, and the *cost of serving* rises.**
>
> **⚠️ So there is no dominant choice.** **Kotler gives both sides: concentrated marketing "can be highly profitable" but "involves higher-than-normal risks"; local marketing "can drive up manufacturing and marketing costs by reducing economies of scale."**
>
> **(c) Until the margin gain stops covering the volume and cost lost — and §5 gives the test.**
>
> **A better match commands a price premium; a narrower target sells to fewer people.** **The break-even table says how much volume a given premium buys you room to lose:**
>
> | premium | you can lose up to |
> |---|---|
> | 5% | 11.1% of volume |
> | **17.5%** | **30.4%** |
> | 30% | 42.9% |
>
> **⇒ a niche strategy earning a 17.5% premium is worth it if narrowing the target costs less than 30.4% of volume**, plus whatever extra cost the tailoring adds.
>
> **⚠️ And this reframes the chapter's question.** **It is not "which targeting strategy is best" — it is "how far down the spectrum does the arithmetic justify going", and the answer is firm-specific.** *(A firm with high fixed costs and steep scale economies stops earlier; one with flexible production goes further, which is why mass customisation became feasible only when manufacturing costs of variety fell.)*

**3. (Hard — the value-proposition grid.)** (a) Reconstruct it. (b) Which cells win? (c) What is wrong with "more for less"?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** Benefits (more / same / less) × price (more / same / less):
>
> | | price: more | price: same | price: less |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **benefits: more** | more for more | more for the same | **more for less** |
> | **benefits: same** | *losing* | *marginal* | the same for less |
> | **benefits: less** | *losing* | *losing* | less for much less |
>
> **(b) Five: the top row and the right column.** **Three losing cells sit in the lower left — more price for the same or fewer benefits — and the centre is marginal.**
>
> **The logic is simple once seen: a winning cell must beat the alternative on *at least one* dimension without being worse on the other.**
>
> **(c) ⚠️ It gives up nothing, so it is not a positioning.**
>
> **Each of the other four is a *trade*: more benefits bought with a higher price, or a lower price bought with fewer benefits.** **More-for-less concedes neither.**
>
> **⇒ any firm able to sustain it would take the entire market**, so it cannot be an equilibrium. **It is a temporary state of superior efficiency — real while it lasts, and not a strategic position.** **It belongs on the diagram as an aspiration.**
>
> **And Kotler's own more-for-more warning is the general form of the same instability**: it "often invites imitators who claim the same quality but at a lower price" — Starbucks now facing Dunkin' Donuts and McDonald's. **⚠️ Competitors attack by moving *left along the top row*, which is what makes the whole top-right region unstable.**
>
> *(This connects to §6's *preemptive* criterion: a position survives only if the difference cannot be easily copied. More-for-less is by definition copyable, because efficiency is.)*

**4. (Hard — why positioning pays.)** (a) What does a price premium mean economically? (b) Compute what Kotler's number implies. (c) What does it cost?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) ⚠️ It is a downward-sloping firm demand curve.**
>
> **Under perfect competition a firm's demand is *horizontal*: products are identical, so charging a cent above the market price sells nothing.** **The sustainable premium is exactly zero.**
>
> **⇒ a firm that *can* charge more for a comparable product faces a demand curve that slopes down — meaning it can raise price and lose *some* volume rather than *all* of it.** **That is what differentiation buys, and it is the whole economic content of "positioning".**
>
> **(b) A firm elasticity of about 6.7 instead of infinity.**
>
> *(Kotler: Hearts On Fire "commands a 15 to 20 percent price premium over comparable competing diamonds." Via the Lerner index $(P-MC)/P=1/e$, taking the comparable rival's price as the competitive benchmark:)*
>
> | premium | implied elasticity |
> |---|---|
> | 0% | **infinite** |
> | 15% | 7.67 |
> | **17.5%** | **6.71** |
> | 20% | 6.00 |
>
> **⚠️ Neither source states this.** **[[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro]] calls the resulting structure *monopolistic competition* — many firms, differentiated products, each pricing above marginal cost — and Kotler's chapter is a manual for entering it.**
>
> **The premium is worth more than it looks, because it falls straight through to margin:** *(from price 100, cost 60)* **a 17.5% price premium is a 43.8% profit-per-unit premium.**
>
> **(c) Volume — and the break-even is computable.**
>
> | premium | you can lose up to |
> |---|---|
> | 17.5% | **30.4% of volume** |
>
> **⚠️ So a differentiated firm can shed nearly a third of its customers and be no worse off.** **That is the arithmetic behind niche marketing, and it explains why a small high-margin player can be more profitable than a large low-margin one.**
>
> **The caveat is §6's *preemptive* criterion.** **The elasticity is an asset only while the difference resists imitation** — and Kotler's own examples show what happens when it does not. **⇒ positioning is not a one-off investment but a defended one.**

**5. (Positioning and clustering.)** (a) Distinguish the three STP steps. (b) What is a positioning map good for? (c) How do Kotler's criteria apply to a clustering algorithm?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Two happen in the market; one happens in the head.**
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | **segmentation** | dividing the market into groups | about **buyers** |
> | **targeting** | *choosing* which to enter | a **decision** |
> | **positioning** | the place the product occupies **in the minds** of consumers | about **minds** |
>
> **⚠️ A firm *chooses* its target and only *influences* its position.** **That asymmetry is why positioning can fail while targeting succeeds: you can reach exactly the people you meant to and still occupy the wrong place in their heads.**
>
> **(b) Finding empty space — with a caveat Kotler does not give.**
>
> **A perceptual map plots brands on two attributes with circle size for share, and its value is the gaps.**
>
> **⚠️ But a gap exists for one of two reasons: nobody *thought of* it, or nobody *wants* it.** **The map cannot tell you which**, and treating the second as the first is where positioning strategies die. *(There is no premium low-cost airline for the same reason there is no gap in the market for it.)*
>
> **(c) They are the acceptance test for its output.**
>
> **RFM — recency, frequency, monetary — is Kotler's *behavioral* base made computable, and clustering finds segments in that space.** **Three warnings follow from his own criteria:**
>
> - **⚠️ A clustering algorithm always returns clusters.** $k$-means with $k=5$ gives five segments on random data. **So *differentiable* — do they respond differently? — is not established by the clustering and needs [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s causal research.**
> - **⚠️ The clusters depend on the scaling.** Monetary value has a far wider numeric range than recency in days, **so an unscaled distance metric segments almost entirely on spend** — the segments become an artefact of the units.
> - **Substantial and actionable still bind.** An algorithm will happily return a segment of 40 customers, or one defined by a variable you cannot target on.
>
> **⇒ the algorithm proposes and the five requirements dispose.** **⚠️ That is the most useful thing this chapter gives a Data Science reader: a domain-derived acceptance test that a purely statistical procedure cannot supply for itself.**

- **Five requirements: measurable, accessible, substantial, differentiable, actionable** — **a filter, not a checklist.**
- **⚠️ *Differentiable* is a CAUSAL requirement and the other four are descriptive** — it needs [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s experiments, not description.
- **Four targeting strategies: undifferentiated, differentiated, concentrated, micromarketing** — **⚠️ a spectrum where match improves and cost rises together.**

- **⚠️ "More for less" gives up nothing, so it is not a positioning** — it is temporary efficiency, and any firm sustaining it would take the whole market.
- **More-for-more "invites imitators"** — competitors attack by moving *left along the top row*.
- **⚠️ A PRICE PREMIUM OVER A COMPARABLE PRODUCT IS A DOWNWARD-SLOPING FIRM DEMAND CURVE.** **Under perfect competition it would be exactly zero.**
- **⚠️ Computed from Kotler's 15–20%: a firm elasticity of about 6.7 instead of infinity** — **the entire economic content of "positioning", stated by neither source.**
- **⇒ the chapter is an instruction manual for entering [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|monopolistic competition]].**

> - **⚠️ §5 is the note's principal addition and the cross-subject result [[00-Index]] promised.** **Kotler reports a 15–20% price premium as evidence that positioning works; he never asks what a sustainable premium *is*.** **Translating it — a premium over a comparable product is a downward-sloping firm demand curve, and the Lerner index converts it to a firm elasticity of ~6.7 — supplies the economics his chapter assumes and [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/06 - Monopoly, Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition|Macro/Micro ch. 05–06]] supplies without ever naming positioning.** **The margin arithmetic (17.5% price ⇒ 43.8% profit per unit) and the break-even volume table (lose 30.4% and be even) are also mine**, and the second is what makes §3's targeting spectrum decidable rather than merely descriptive.
