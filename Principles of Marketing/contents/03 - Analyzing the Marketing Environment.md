# Analyzing the Marketing Environment

**[[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|Ch. 02]] chose which businesses to be in. This chapter is about the forces that decide whether that choice stays right** — and it is the chapter where Kotler prints the most numbers and computes the fewest.

**Four results, all from the [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]] template — *take his scattered figures and divide them*.**

**§2 — ⚠️ boomers earn 2.69× the average and spend only 1.92×.** *(Computed from his own two figures: 70% of disposable income and half of consumer spending, on 26% of the population.)* **⇒ they convert income into spending at **less than a third** the rate of everyone else** — **so "a marketer's dream" is true of their income and much weaker of their spending.** **Kotler puts both figures in one paragraph and draws the conclusion from the first alone.**

**§2 — Gen X earns 1.91× the average from the *smallest* cohort.** **He calls them "sometimes-overlooked" and supplies the number showing why they should not be, without connecting the two.**

**§2 — Gen Z influences \$13.60 for every \$1 it spends** *(computed: \$600bn influenced against \$44bn of own money)*. **⇒ the buyer is the parent and the persuader is the child.**

**§3 — ⚠️ his own cohort definitions overlap by five years.** *(Millennials "born between 1977 and 2000"; Gen Z "born after 1995".)* **Not filed — generational boundaries are genuinely disputed — but the warning is real: a generational label is not a partition.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Micro- versus macroenvironment

| | |
|---|---|
| **microenvironment** | **actors close to the company** that affect its ability to serve customers — **the company itself** (other departments), **suppliers**, **marketing intermediaries**, **competitors**, **publics**, **customers** |
| **macroenvironment** | **larger societal forces** that affect the whole microenvironment — **demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/social, cultural** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The test is not distance — it is whether the firm has a *relationship* with it
> **A supplier is micro because you deal with it by name. The birth rate is macro because you can only respond to it.**
>
> **And §5 shows why the test matters: proactivity is available in proportion to how close the force sits.**

> [!note] Publics is the element students forget
> **Financial, media, government, citizen-action, local, general — and ⚠️ *internal* publics.** **The firm's own employees are a public**, which is [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s value chain seen from the outside.

> [!note] Micro/macro is NOT the same cut as SWOT's internal/external
> **The *company itself* appears in the microenvironment**, so part of the "micro" environment is internal to the firm. **Do not map one framework onto the other.** *([[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|Ch. 02]]'s SWOT test — "can the firm change it by itself?" — still decides internal versus external, and gives a different answer.)*

### 2. ⚠️ The generational cohorts — Kotler's figures, divided

| cohort | size | born | Kotler's shares |
|---|---|---|---|
| **baby boomers** | **78m** | 1946–1964 | **26% of population, 70% of disposable income**, half of all consumer spending |
| **Generation X** | **49m** | 1965–1976 | **29% of total national income** |
| **millennials** | **83m** | 1977–2000 | — |
| **Generation Z** | **82m** | after 1995 | spend **\$44bn** of own money, influence **\$600bn** of family spending |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Result 1 — boomers earn 2.69× the average and spend only 1.92×
> *(Computed from his own paragraph:)*
> $$\text{income per boomer}=\frac{70\%}{26\%}=\mathbf{2.69\times}\qquad\text{spending per boomer}=\frac{50\%}{26\%}=\mathbf{1.92\times}$$
>
> **Dividing one by the other:**
>
> | | spending ÷ income |
> |---|---|
> | **boomers** | 50/70 = **0.714** |
> | everyone else | 50/30 = **1.667** |
> | **ratio** | **0.43** |
>
> **⚠️ Boomers convert income into spending at less than a third the rate of everyone else.**
>
> **So "the wealthiest generation in U.S. history, a marketer's dream" is true about their *income* and much weaker about their *spending*** — **and which of the two matters depends entirely on the category.** **For consumables, target the spenders; for financial services and durables, target the earners.**
>
> **Kotler prints both figures in one paragraph and draws the "dream" conclusion from the first alone.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Result 2 — Gen X earns 1.91× the average, from the smallest cohort
> *(Computed: 49m is **15.2%** of a 323m population, and they take **29%** of national income.)*
> $$\frac{29\%}{15.2\%}=\mathbf{1.91\times}$$
>
> **Kotler calls them "a sometimes-overlooked consumer group" and supplies, two sentences later, the number that shows why they should not be.** **He does not connect them.**
>
> *(And the reason they are overlooked is structural: they sit between two cohorts that are each two-thirds larger, so they lose every argument decided by headcount.)*

> [!warning] ⚠️ Result 3 — Gen Z influences \$13.60 for every \$1 it spends
> $$\frac{\$600\text{bn influenced}}{\$44\text{bn of own money}}=\mathbf{13.6\times}$$
> *(Per Gen Zer: **\$537** of own money against **\$7,317** of influenced family spending.)*
>
> **⇒ this inverts the marketing problem. The buyer is the parent and the persuader is the child, so the message must work on *both at once*.**
>
> **Kotler's own illustration is exactly this tension** — Justice's CEO on a store the *mother* pays in: **"the last thing a 10- or 12-year-old girl wants is to look like her mom."** **And it explains his list of *Abercrombie Kids, Gap Kids, Old Navy Kids, Pottery Barn Kids*: parent brands with child extensions is the structure a dual-audience purchase requires** — the parent trusts the brand, the child chooses the line.

**The qualitative characterisations, which exams do test:** **boomers** — wealthy, active, redefining retirement, **82% open to new brands**. **Gen X** — cynical, sceptical of marketing, family-first, **82% own their homes**, "the most educated generation". **Millennials** — **frugal, practical, connected, mobile, impatient**; trust each other over marketers; first digital natives. **Gen Z** — **"digital in their DNA"**, over **3,000 texts a month**, blend online and offline seamlessly, **over half research products before buying**.

### 3. ⚠️ An internal inconsistency in the cohort definitions

**Kotler's own margin definitions:**

- **millennials** — *"the 83 million children of the baby boomers born **between 1977 and 2000**"*
- **Generation Z** — *"born **after 1995**"*

> [!warning] ⚠️ Those overlap by five years
> **Anyone born 1996–2000 is in *both* cohorts by Kotler's own definitions.**
>
> *(Computed: the four cohorts as stated total **292 million** against a US population of ~323m — **90.4%** — leaving 31m born before 1946. At ~4 million US births a year, 1996–2000 is roughly 20 million people, so the true four-cohort total is nearer **272 million**.)*

> [!note] Not filed as an erratum — and the reason matters
> **Generational boundaries are genuinely disputed and no two sources agree.** **Kotler is *reporting* conventions, not deriving them, and he hedges Gen Z's start — "born after 1995 *in this group*".** **Rule 4 requires ruling out alternative conventions first, and here the conventions *are* the disagreement.**
>
> **⚠️ But the practical warning is real: never sum cohort sizes from different sources, and never treat a generational label as a partition.** **They are marketing conveniences, not a mutually exclusive classification.**

> [!note] A second, smaller wrinkle
> *(His 26% boomer share implies a population of $78/0.26=\mathbf{300}$ million — the US figure around 2006, not 2016.)* **So the percentage and the headcount come from different vintages.** **Also not filed — sourced statistics routinely lag — but it is why §2's multiples should be read as orders of magnitude rather than decimals.**

### 4. The six macroenvironmental forces

| force | what it is | examples |
|---|---|---|
| **demographic** | the study of human **populations** — size, density, location, age, sex, race, occupation | generational cohorts; changing families; geographic shifts; better-educated; **increasing diversity** |
| **economic** | factors affecting **purchasing power** and spending patterns | income distribution; **value marketing**; the shrinking middle class |
| **natural** | the physical environment and resources needed as **inputs** | raw-material shortages; pollution; government intervention; **environmental sustainability** |
| **technological** | creates new products and opportunities — **and destroys old ones** | the internet of things; regulation of new technology |
| **political/social** | laws, agencies and pressure groups that limit organisations | legislation; ethics; **cause-related marketing** |
| **cultural** | institutions and forces affecting a society's basic **values** | **core vs secondary beliefs**; views of self, others, nature |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The one distinction worth memorising is inside the cultural force
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **core beliefs and values** | passed parent to child, reinforced by schools, churches, business and government — **highly persistent** |
> | **secondary beliefs** | **more open to change** |
>
> **Kotler's example: believing in *marriage* is core; believing people should marry *early* is secondary.**
>
> **⚠️ Marketers can shift secondary values and have almost no chance of shifting core ones.** **That is the practical content of the whole cultural section** — and it is the cultural analogue of [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s "marketing shapes wants, not needs".

> [!note] On the economic force — value marketing
> **Kotler's observation is that the rich have grown richer, the middle class has shrunk, and the poor remain poor** ⇒ **a two-tier market**, with firms serving either end and the middle hollowing out. **⚠️ This is a *segmentation* consequence disguised as an economic fact**, and [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] is where it gets acted on.

### 5. Responding to the environment — reactive versus proactive

| | |
|---|---|
| **reactive** | treat the environment as an uncontrollable given; watch and react |
| **proactive** | **take aggressive action to affect the publics and forces** — lobbying, press relations, lawsuits, cause campaigns |

**Kotler's position is explicit and not neutral: "rather than simply watching and reacting, these firms take aggressive actions to affect the publics and forces in their marketing environments."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ But he concedes the limit, and §1's test explains where it falls
> **"Marketing management cannot always control environmental forces."**
>
> **The micro/macro split sets the menu: you can negotiate with a supplier (micro) and you cannot negotiate with the birth rate (macro).** **⇒ proactivity is available in proportion to how close the force sits** — which is why §1's test, *do you have a relationship with it?*, is the operative one rather than any judgement about corporate assertiveness.

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Environment.)** (a) Distinguish micro from macro. (b) Classify six factors. (c) Why is this not the same cut as SWOT's?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Whether the firm has a relationship with it.**
>
> **Microenvironment: the company itself, suppliers, marketing intermediaries, competitors, publics, customers.** **Macroenvironment: demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/social, cultural.**
>
> **⚠️ The test is not distance or size — it is *nameability*.** **You deal with a supplier by name; you can only respond to the birth rate.**
>
> **(b)**
> - **A key component supplier raising prices** — **micro** *(supplier; you can negotiate)*.
> - **The proportion of over-65s rising** — **macro, demographic**.
> - **A consumer group campaigning against your packaging** — **micro** *(a citizen-action public; you can engage it directly)*.
> - **New data-protection legislation** — **macro, political/social**.
> - **A competitor cutting prices** — **micro**.
> - **Falling real incomes in your main market** — **macro, economic**.
>
> *(The third is the instructive one: a pressure group *feels* like an external force but is a public, hence micro — and that is precisely why it can be responded to proactively.)*
>
> **(c) Because the *company itself* is in the microenvironment.**
>
> **⚠️ So part of the "micro" environment is internal to the firm.** **[[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|Ch. 02]]'s SWOT test — "can the firm change it by itself?" — sorts the same factors differently: a supplier is *external* for SWOT and *micro* here.**
>
> **The two frameworks answer different questions.** **SWOT asks *whose problem is this?*; micro/macro asks *can I have a conversation with it?*** **Mapping one onto the other produces errors in both.**

**2. (Hard — the cohorts.)** (a) Give the four with their figures. (b) Compute what Kotler's boomer numbers imply. (c) What follows for targeting?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Boomers 78m (1946–64), Gen X 49m (1965–76), millennials 83m (1977–2000), Gen Z 82m (after 1995).** **Boomers: 26% of population, 70% of disposable income, half of consumer spending. Gen X: 29% of national income. Gen Z: \$44bn own spending, \$600bn influenced.**
>
> **(b) They earn 2.69× the average and spend 1.92× — so they save far more.**
>
> $$\frac{70}{26}=\mathbf{2.69\times}\ \text{(income)}\qquad\frac{50}{26}=\mathbf{1.92\times}\ \text{(spending)}$$
>
> | | spending ÷ income |
> |---|---|
> | boomers | **0.714** |
> | everyone else | **1.667** |
>
> **⚠️ Boomers convert income into spending at 0.43× the rate of everyone else — less than a third.**
>
> *(Note "everyone else" spends more than they earn, which is arithmetically what the two figures require: 50% of spending on 30% of income. That is consistent with younger cohorts borrowing — mortgages, student debt, car finance — and is itself a marketing fact.)*
>
> **(c) That "wealthiest generation" is a claim about income, not about spending — and the category decides which matters.**
>
> **⚠️ Kotler's paragraph contains both figures and draws the "marketer's dream" conclusion from the income one alone.**
>
> - **For fast-moving consumables, target the *spenders*.** Boomers' 1.92× is real but the multiple is far smaller than the income figure suggests.
> - **For financial services, durables, healthcare and travel, target the *earners*.** The 2.69× is the relevant number, and the low spending ratio *is* the opportunity — that unspent income is investable.
>
> **The general lesson is the one this vault keeps finding: two numbers in one paragraph can support opposite strategies, and quoting whichever supports your case is the commonest way to misuse market data.** *(Compare [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s Under Armour, where share and growth rate had to be multiplied before either meant anything.)*

**3. (Hard — Gen X and Gen Z.)** (a) Why is Gen X under-rated on Kotler's own figures? (b) What does Gen Z's influence multiple imply? (c) How does that shape the marketing?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) They earn 1.91× the average from the smallest cohort.**
>
> *(Computed: 49m is **15.2%** of a 323m population, taking **29%** of national income ⇒ **1.91×**.)*
>
> **⚠️ Kotler calls them "a sometimes-overlooked consumer group" and gives, two sentences later, the figure that shows why they should not be.**
>
> **The reason they are overlooked is structural: they sit between two cohorts each about two-thirds larger, so they lose every argument decided by headcount** — and marketing budgets are usually decided by headcount. **⇒ the cohort with the highest income per head gets the least attention**, which is an exploitable inefficiency rather than merely an oversight.
>
> *(Supporting figure: **82% of Gen Xers own their homes** — consistent with high income and a life stage that generates large durable purchases.)*
>
> **(b) That their importance is overwhelmingly *indirect*.**
>
> $$\frac{\$600\text{bn}}{\$44\text{bn}}=\mathbf{13.6\times}$$
>
> **Per Gen Zer: \$537 of their own money against \$7,317 of influenced family spending.**
>
> **⚠️ So for every dollar a Gen Zer spends, thirteen are spent by someone else at their urging.**
>
> **(c) The message must work on two audiences with different criteria, simultaneously.**
>
> **The *buyer* is the parent — who cares about price, durability, safety and value.** **The *persuader* is the child — who cares about identity and peer standing.** **A message that satisfies only one fails: the parent refuses, or the child does not ask.**
>
> **⚠️ Kotler's own illustration is exactly this tension**, from Justice's CEO about a store mothers pay in: **"the last thing a 10- or 12-year-old girl wants is to look like her mom."**
>
> **And it explains the brand architecture he lists** — **Abercrombie Kids, Gap Kids, Old Navy Kids, Pottery Barn Kids.** **A parent brand with a child extension is the structure a dual-audience purchase requires: the parent trusts the established name, the child chooses a line that is visibly *not* the parent's.** *(A single undifferentiated brand cannot do both, which is why the extensions exist rather than simply widening the range.)*

**4. (Hard — the definitions.)** (a) Identify the inconsistency. (b) Should it be filed? (c) What is the durable lesson?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Millennials and Gen Z overlap by five years.**
>
> **Kotler's margin definitions: millennials "born **between 1977 and 2000**"; Gen Z "born **after 1995**".** **⚠️ Anyone born 1996–2000 is in both.**
>
> *(Computed: the four cohorts total **292 million** against a US population of ~323m — 90.4%, leaving 31m born before 1946. At ~4m US births a year the overlap is roughly 20 million, so the true total is nearer **272 million**.)*
>
> *(A second wrinkle: his 26% boomer share implies a population of **300 million**, the US figure around 2006 — so the percentage and the headcount come from different vintages.)*
>
> **(b) No.**
>
> **Generational boundaries are genuinely disputed and no two sources agree** — Pew, Strauss–Howe and the census bureaus all differ. **Kotler is *reporting* conventions rather than deriving them, and he hedges Gen Z's start: "born after 1995 *in this group*".**
>
> **⚠️ Rule 4 requires ruling out alternative conventions before filing, and here the conventions *are* the disagreement.** **Filing an erratum would amount to asserting that one contested boundary is correct.**
>
> *(Likewise the vintage mismatch: sourced statistics routinely lag their publication, and a textbook citing a 2006-vintage percentage alongside a later headcount is doing something ordinary, not wrong.)*

>
> **⚠️ Never sum cohort sizes from different sources, and never assume the labels are mutually exclusive and exhaustive.** **They are marketing conveniences.**
>
> **And the consequence for analysis is concrete: any calculation that divides a total *across* cohorts — market sizing, share-of-segment, budget allocation — inherits the overlap.** **A "millennial market size" and a "Gen Z market size" taken from the same book cannot be added.**
>
> **⚠️ This is the vault's measurement theme in its most everyday form**: [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s *which metric?* and [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s *which market?* become **which boundary?** — and here the boundary is not merely a judgement, it is a *contested* one, so two competent analysts will disagree by twenty million people.

**5. (Forces and response.)** (a) Name the six forces. (b) Explain core versus secondary beliefs. (c) Can a firm shape its environment?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/social, cultural.**
>
> **(b) Persistence is the difference, and it decides what marketing can attempt.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **core** | passed parent to child, reinforced by schools, churches, business and government — **highly persistent** |
> | **secondary** | **more open to change** |
>
> **Kotler's example: believing in *marriage* is core; believing people should marry *early* is secondary.**
>
> **⚠️ Marketers can shift secondary values and have almost no chance of shifting core ones.** **That is the practical content of the entire cultural section** — a campaign attacking a core value will fail regardless of budget, while one repositioning a secondary belief can succeed.
>
> **And it is the cultural analogue of [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s distinction: marketing shapes *wants*, not *needs*; it shapes *secondary* beliefs, not *core* ones.** **Both draw the same boundary — marketing works on the mutable layer above something it did not create and cannot move.**
>
> **(c) In proportion to how close the force sits.**
>
> **Kotler's position is explicit: "rather than simply watching and reacting, these firms take aggressive actions to affect the publics and forces in their marketing environments"** — lobbying, press relations, lawsuits, cause campaigns. **He immediately concedes that "marketing management cannot always control environmental forces."**
>
> **⚠️ The honest reconciliation is §1's test.** **You can negotiate with a supplier, sue a competitor, or lobby a regulator — all micro or adjacent.** **You cannot negotiate with the birth rate, the price of oil, or a core cultural value.**
>
> **⇒ proactivity is not a matter of corporate assertiveness but of *which force you are facing*.** **The micro/macro split sets the menu**, and a firm that treats a macro force as negotiable wastes the budget it should have spent adapting.

## 📝 Summary

- **Microenvironment: company, suppliers, intermediaries, competitors, publics, customers.** **Macroenvironment: demographic, economic, natural, technological, political/social, cultural.**
- **⚠️ The test is *nameability*, not distance** — you deal with a supplier by name; you only respond to the birth rate.
- **Publics include the firm's own employees** — [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s value chain from outside.
- **⚠️ Micro/macro is NOT SWOT's internal/external** — the *company itself* sits in the microenvironment.
- **Four cohorts: boomers 78m, Gen X 49m, millennials 83m, Gen Z 82m.**
- **⚠️ Boomers earn 2.69× the average and spend only 1.92×** *(computed)* — **converting income to spending at 0.43× everyone else's rate.** **"A marketer's dream" is a claim about income; the category decides which figure matters.**
- **⚠️ Gen X earns 1.91× the average from the *smallest* cohort** *(computed)* — **overlooked because budgets follow headcount**, which makes it an exploitable inefficiency.
- **⚠️ Gen Z influences \$13.60 per \$1 spent** *(computed: \$600bn against \$44bn)* — **the buyer is the parent, the persuader is the child**, which is why parent-brand-plus-child-extension is the required architecture.
- **⚠️ Kotler's millennial and Gen Z definitions OVERLAP by five years** *(1977–2000 versus "after 1995")*; the four cohorts sum to **292m** against a ~323m population.
- **Not filed — generational boundaries are genuinely contested and he hedges** — **but never sum cohort sizes, and never treat a generational label as a partition.**
- **A second vintage mismatch: his 26% implies a 300m population, the US figure around 2006.**
- **Six macro forces.** **Economic: the middle class has shrunk ⇒ a two-tier market — a segmentation consequence dressed as an economic fact.**
- **⚠️ Core beliefs are highly persistent; secondary beliefs are movable.** **Marriage is core; marrying *early* is secondary.** **Marketers can shift the second and essentially never the first** — **the cultural analogue of "shapes wants, not needs".**
- **Reactive versus proactive, and Kotler favours proactive** — **⚠️ but proactivity is available in proportion to how close the force sits.** **The micro/macro split sets the menu.**
