# New Products and the Product Life Cycle

**Six results. ⚠️ And the chapter's centre is that KOTLER'S OWN MARKETING STRATEGY STATEMENT IS A FULLY SPECIFIED FINANCIAL PLAN — it can be solved, and solving it changes what you think of it.**

**§5 — ⚠️ HIS ELECTRIC-CAR PLAN IMPLIES A CONTRIBUTION OF \$1,125 PER CAR.** *(Two equations, two unknowns, from figures he states as illustration: **\$1,125 contribution, \$23,355 variable cost, \$21.25m fixed cost** — both years check to the cent.)* **⇒ 4.60% of the wholesale price, a break-even of 63,333 cars against a plan of 50,000, and a 20% volume miss makes the year-1 loss 75% worse than its own stated cap.** **He presents it as a template and never notices it is solvable.**

**§2 — ⚠️ ONE COMMERCIAL SUCCESS PER ~2,800 RAW IDEAS.** *(Chaining his own four separate funnel measurements — AT&T's 28,000→75, Cisco's 824→32→9, and his "two-thirds never launched / 60% fail".)* **He prints all four and never puts them on one scale.**

**§3 — ⚠️ R-W-W IS A HYPOTHESIS TEST, AND THE CONJUNCTION IS DELIBERATELY CONSERVATIVE.** *(Requiring three yeses **kills 27.1% of good ideas even at 90% per-question accuracy**, while letting only **0.10%** of bad ones through.)* **⇒ and that asymmetry is CORRECT, because §6 shows a false positive costs 21,845× a false negative.**

**§4 — ⚠️ HE FLAGS THE INTENTION GAP AND NEVER CORRECTS IT.** *(His 2% "definitely" + 5% "probably" = **7% naive**; standard weighting gives **2.90%** — **2.41× apart**, and on his own 50,000-car plan that is the difference between needing 714,286 and 1,724,138 target customers.)*

**§6 — ⚠️ HIS OWN POP-TARTS PROBLEM LOSES \$50,000** — break-even cannibalisation **78.57%** against an assumed 80%, **failing by 1.43 points.** **And the "premium" product's contribution is 7.14% LOWER than the one it replaces:** price +20%, variable cost **+83.3%**. ***Second confirmation of [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s cannibalisation result, this time in Kotler's own numbers.***

**§7 — ⚠️ NEVER DIVIDE TWO SHARES WITH DIFFERENT DENOMINATORS.** His "62% of revenue, 77% of device units" naively implies Apple's other devices cost **2.05×** an iPhone; **the true ratio is 0.96 — wrong by a factor of 2.13**, because 62% is of *all* revenue and 77% is of *device* units.

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Why new products, and the odds against them

**Two routes to a new product: acquisition** (buying a company, patent or licence) **and new product development** — *"original products, product improvements, product modifications, and new brands that the firm develops through its own product development."* **This chapter is about the second.**

**Kotler's two failure figures:**

| | |
|---|---|
| new consumer packaged products from established companies that **fail** | **60%** |
| new product concepts **never even launched** | **two-thirds** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Multiply them — a concept has a 13.33% chance, or 1 in 7.5
> $$\Pr(\text{success}) = \tfrac13 \times 0.40 = 13.33\%$$
> **And that is *conditional on already having a concept*, which is far down the funnel — §2 finishes the job.**

**Why they fail** *(his list, and it is worth memorising because every item is a different stage of the process)*: **overestimated market size; poor design; wrong positioning; wrong timing; wrong price; poor advertising; an executive pushing a favourite idea against the research; development costs above forecast; competitors fighting back harder than expected.**

### 2. ⚠️ The eight-step process — and the funnel measured

*(Figure 9.1, recovered:)*

**idea generation → idea screening → concept development and testing → marketing strategy development → business analysis → product development → test marketing → commercialization**

> [!note] The first step maximises the number of ideas; every step after it minimises it
> **Kotler is explicit: "The purpose of idea generation is to create a large number of ideas. The purpose of the succeeding stages is to reduce that number."** **⇒ the process is a funnel, and the whole design question is *where* to narrow it.**

**Idea sources.** **Internal** — formal R&D *(Ford's Silicon Valley centre, Chick-fil-A's "Hatch")* and employees *(AT&T's Innovation Pipeline; Facebook and Twitter "hackathons"; LinkedIn's monthly "hackdays" and InCubator, which gives an approved team **up to 90 days** away from regular work)*. **External** — distributors, suppliers, competitors *(buy the product and take it apart)*, trade media, agencies, labs, inventors. **And above all customers** *(LEGO Ideas: an idea reaching **10,000 votes** goes to the Review Board, and a creator whose idea ships earns **1% of total net sales**; **12 major products so far**)*.

**Crowdsourcing** — *"inviting broad communities of people… into the new product innovation process."* **And Kotler is honest about its cost:** Cisco's I-Prize drew *"more than 820 distinct ideas from more than 2,900 innovators from 156 countries"*, and **a team of six worked full-time for three months** to get to 32 semifinalists. *"The evaluation process was far more labor-intensive than we'd anticipated."*

**⚠️ Now put his four funnel measurements on one scale — something he never does.**

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AT&T TIP** | 28,000 ideas → 75 funded | **0.2679%** | 1 in 373 |
| **Cisco I-Prize** | 824 ideas → 32 semifinalists | 3.8835% | 1 in 26 |
| **Cisco I-Prize** | 824 ideas → 9 finalist teams | 1.0922% | 1 in 92 |
| **Kotler's own rates** | concept → success | **13.3333%** | 1 in 7.5 |

**Chaining AT&T's idea→project rate with his concept→success rate:**

> [!warning] ⚠️ 28,000 IDEAS → 75 FUNDED → ~10 SUCCESSES = 1 IN 2,800
> **That is 0.036%, and it is what "a company typically generates hundreds — even thousands — of ideas to find a few good ones" actually means.**
>
> **⇒ it is also the business case for crowdsourcing.** **If the conversion rate is fixed at roughly 1 in 3,000, the only lever on the number of successes is the number of ideas at the top** — which is exactly why firms open the funnel to people they do not employ.
>
> *(Caveat stated plainly: this chains rates measured at different firms on different kinds of product. It establishes the **order of magnitude and the structure**, not a precise estimate.)*

### 3. ⚠️ Idea screening — R-W-W as a hypothesis test

**The R-W-W screen asks three questions:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Is it REAL?** | Is there a real need and desire? Will customers buy it? Is there a clear concept? |
| **Can we WIN?** | Does it offer a **sustainable competitive advantage**? Do we have the resources? |
| **Is it WORTH DOING?** | Does it fit the growth strategy? Is there sufficient profit potential? |

**Kotler: *"The company should be able to answer yes to all three."*** **That is a conjunction, and a conjunction has statistical consequences.**

**If a *good* idea is judged correctly on each question with probability $q$, it survives all three only with probability $q^3$:**

| $q$ | $q^3$ | **good ideas wrongly killed** |
|---|---|---|
| 0.95 | 0.8574 | 14.26% |
| **0.90** | **0.7290** | **27.10%** |
| 0.80 | 0.5120 | 48.80% |

**If a *bad* idea is correctly rejected on each with probability $r$, any one "no" kills it, so it dies with probability $1-(1-r)^3$:**

| $r$ | | **bad ideas that slip through** |
|---|---|---|
| **0.90** | 0.9990 | **0.10%** |
| 0.70 | 0.9730 | 2.70% |
| 0.50 | 0.8750 | 12.50% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The screen buys enormous specificity with a lot of sensitivity — and that is the right trade
> **At 90% per-question accuracy it kills 27.1% of good ideas to keep 99.9% of bad ones out.** **A statistician would call that a very conservative test, and §6 explains why it should be: a bad idea that survives to commercialization costs 21,845 times what it costs to kill at stage one.**
>
> **⇒ THE SHAPE OF A SCREEN SHOULD FOLLOW THE COST ASYMMETRY OF THE TWO ERRORS.** **Kotler gives the rule and not the reason.**
>
> **⚠️ And it is [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s multiplicity result in a third setting** — there, 20 independent segment tests gave a **64.2%** chance of at least one false positive. **Same mathematics, opposite direction: combining independent tests with AND is conservative, with OR is permissive, and neither is neutral.**

### 4. Concept development and testing — and the intention gap

**Three things that get confused, and the exam distinction:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **product idea** | an idea for a possible product the company could offer |
| **product concept** | a detailed version **stated in meaningful consumer terms** |
| **product image** | **the way consumers perceive** an actual or potential product |

> [!note] ⚠️ Idea → concept → image is a chain of ownership
> **The firm writes the idea, drafts the concept, and only *influences* the image** — the same asymmetry [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] found between targeting and positioning.

**His four concepts for one electric car** *(same product, four different markets)*: a **second family car** for errands; a **mid-priced sporty compact** for young singles and couples; a **"green" everyday car**; a **compact crossover SUV** for people who want SUV space without the fuel bill.

**Concept 3 in full, as tested:** *"An efficient, fun-to-drive, battery-powered compact car that seats four… goes 300 miles on a single charge and costs pennies per mile to operate… Its fully equipped base price is \$28,800."*

**Table 9.1's nine concept-test questions** run: **do you understand it → do you believe it → benefits vs a conventional car → vs a hybrid → what improvements → for what uses → what price is reasonable → who decides and who drives → would you buy (definitely / probably / probably not / definitely not).**

**⚠️ And here is the gap.** Kotler: *"Suppose 2 percent of consumers say they 'definitely' would buy and another 5 percent say 'probably.' The company could project these figures to the full population… Even then, however, the estimate is uncertain because people do not always carry out their stated intentions."*

| method | forecast |
|---|---|
| **naive top-two-box** (2% + 5%) | **7.00%** |
| **weighted 0.70 / 0.30** *(standard practice — my addition)* | **2.90%** |
| weighted 0.60 / 0.20 (conservative) | 2.20% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The naive figure is 2.41× the adjusted one, and he stops at "uncertain"
> **He names the problem and supplies no correction, so a student who follows the text literally over-forecasts by a factor of two and a half.**
>
> **⇒ on his own 50,000-car year-one plan the two readings imply target populations of 714,286 and 1,724,138** — the difference between a market you have and one you do not.
>
> *(The standard fix is to discount stated intent: "definitely" converts at roughly 0.7 and "probably" at roughly 0.3, with the exact weights calibrated from the firm's own past launches. **The calibration matters more than the weights** — this is [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s territory, and it is what makes a concept test a measurement rather than a mood reading.)*

### 5. ⚠️ Marketing strategy development — his plan, solved

**The statement has three parts:** *(i)* **target market, value proposition, and the sales/share/profit goals for the first few years**; *(ii)* **planned price, distribution and marketing budget for year one**; *(iii)* **long-run sales and profit goals and the marketing mix strategy.**

**Everything he states:**

| | |
|---|---|
| retail base price | **\$28,800** |
| dealer discount | **15% off list** ⇒ wholesale **\$24,480** |
| year 1 | **50,000 cars, loss of not more than \$15 million** |
| year 2 | **90,000 cars, profit of \$25 million** |
| marketing budget yr 1 | **\$50 million**, split **40-30-30** ⇒ \$20m / \$15m / \$15m |
| budget growth | **"raised each year by about 10 percent"** ⇒ **\$55m** in year 2 |
| marketing research | \$100,000 yr 1, **\$60,000/yr** after |
| long run | **3% share of the total auto market, 15% after-tax ROI** |

**⚠️ Two years, two equations, two unknowns. Let $c$ be contribution per car and $F$ fixed cost:**

$$50{,}000c - F - 50\text{m} = -15\text{m} \qquad 90{,}000c - F - 55\text{m} = +25\text{m}$$

| **solved** | |
|---|---|
| **contribution per car** | **\$1,125.00** |
| implied variable cost | **\$23,355.00** |
| other fixed cost | **\$21,250,000** |
| contribution as % of wholesale | **4.60%** |
| contribution as % of retail | 3.91% |

***Both years check to the cent.***

> [!warning] ⚠️ His illustrative plan implies a contribution margin of 4.60%
> **That is very thin for a manufactured good — which is why part (iii) says "price will be raised in the second and third years if competition and the economy permit."** **The plan's own long-run viability depends on a price rise it treats as optional.**

**And the break-even follows immediately:**

| | break-even | plan | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **year 1** | **63,333 cars** | 50,000 | **plan is 21.1% BELOW break-even** |
| year 2 | 67,778 cars | 90,000 | 24.7% cushion |

> [!note] ⚠️ That is internally consistent, and it is the point
> **The plan forecasts a year-one loss precisely because it plans to sell 21% under break-even** — the loss is not an accident of launch costs, it is a planned volume shortfall. **The statement never says so.**

**⚠️ Now the sensitivity — the vault's standard second question:**

| volume miss | units | year-1 result | **vs the \$15m cap** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 50,000 | −\$15,000,000 | — |
| 10% | 45,000 | −\$20,625,000 | **+37.5% worse** |
| **20%** | 40,000 | **−\$26,250,000** | **+75.0% worse** |
| 30% | 35,000 | −\$31,875,000 | **+112.5% worse** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 20% volume miss makes the loss 75% worse than the stated cap
> **That is operating leverage: with a 4.60% contribution margin and \$71.25m of fixed and marketing cost, almost nothing absorbs a shortfall.** **⇒ the "loss of not more than \$15 million" is not a cap at all — it is a point forecast that holds only if the volume forecast does**, and §4 has just shown the volume forecast can be 2.41× too high.
>
> **⇒ THE TWO SECTIONS COMPOUND.** **A concept test read naively over-forecasts demand, and a thin-margin plan converts that over-forecast into a loss that more than doubles.** *Kotler prints both and connects neither.*

### 6. Business analysis → commercialization, and why screening early pays

> **Business analysis** — a review of the **sales, costs and profit projections** to see whether they satisfy the company's objectives. *Estimate minimum and maximum sales to assess the range of risk.*
> **Product development** — turn the concept into a physical product. **"A huge jump in investment."**
> **Test marketing** — the product and its whole marketing programme in realistic settings.
> **Commercialization** — introducing it to the market.

**Test marketing is a judgement call about the value of information.** **Do a lot** when the investment is big, the risk is high, or management is unsure *(Taco Bell: **three years and 45 prototypes** before the Doritos Locos Taco; Starbucks: **20 years** developing VIA instant coffee, now **\$300m+ a year**)*. **Do little or none** for simple line extensions, copies of competitors' products, or when speed matters *(Starbucks shipped a flawed mobile payments app and fixed it over six months; it now handles **8 million transactions a week**)*.

**Commercialization decisions: *when* to launch** *(delay if it will eat other products' sales; accelerate if competitors are ready)* **and *where*** *(Apple put the iPhone 6 into **115 countries in under three months**)*.

**⚠️ The cost gradient is what makes early screening valuable.** Kotler: *"Product development costs rise greatly in later stages."* **His own figures confirm it** — **\$38m** on one month of Apple Watch TV advertising, **\$150m** on the Tide Pods launch, **close to \$400m** on the original Microsoft Surface blitz.

*(Illustration: cost per surviving item multiplying by 4 at each of his eight stages — the multiplier is mine, the eight stages and the rising-cost claim are his.)*

| stage | cost | cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| 1. idea generation | 1 | 1 |
| 2. idea screening | 4 | 5 |
| 5. business analysis | 256 | 341 |
| 7. test marketing | 4,096 | 5,461 |
| **8. commercialization** | **16,384** | **21,845** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A bad idea killed at stage 8 costs 21,845× the same idea killed at stage 1
> **That is the economic content of "spot good ideas and drop poor ones *as soon as possible*", and it is why §3's screen is right to be conservative.**

**⚠️ And his own worked problem shows the arithmetic in real money — Pop-Tarts Gone Nutty!**

| | price | variable cost | **contribution** | margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gone Nutty!** | \$1.20 | \$0.55 | **\$0.65** | 54.2% |
| original Pop-Tarts | \$1.00 | \$0.30 | **\$0.70** | 70.0% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Price is +20% but variable cost is +83.3%, so contribution is −7.14%
> **The "premium" product earns LESS per package than the one it replaces.** **⇒ a higher price is not a higher margin, and reading the price alone gets the sign wrong.**

**With 5m packages, 80% cannibalisation and \$500,000 of added fixed cost:**

| | |
|---|---|
| new contribution | 5,000,000 × \$0.65 = **\$3,250,000** |
| cannibalised | 4,000,000 × \$0.70 = **\$2,800,000** |
| net contribution | \$450,000 |
| less added fixed cost | \$500,000 |
| **NET PROFIT EFFECT** | **−\$50,000** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The launch loses money — and the break-even cannibalisation rate is 78.57%
> **It fails by 1.43 percentage points.**
>
> **⇒ SECOND CONFIRMATION OF [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|CH. 08]]'S RESULT, now in Kotler's own numbers:** there I derived that a line extension's cannibalisation rate is approximately the brand's own market share; **here he assumes 80% for Pop-Tarts — a dominant brand — and the launch fails at exactly that rate.**
>
> **The lesson is the sharper one: a new product can be a commercial success and a financial failure at the same time.** *Five million packages sold is a good launch by any marketing measure.*

### 7. Managing new product development

**Three requirements, and Kotler treats them as jointly necessary.**

**Customer-centred.** *"Companies often rely too heavily on technical research in their R&D laboratories."* **One study: firms that directly engage customers in innovation had *twice* the return on assets and *triple* the growth in operating income.** *(Intuit's "Design for Delight": **10,000 hours a year of "follow-me-homes"**, then "go broad, go narrow.")*

**Team-based.** **Sequential development** passes the product department to department — *"orderly… but it can also be dangerously slow"*, and **a bottleneck at one phase stalls the whole project**. **Team-based** overlaps the steps with a cross-functional team that stays from start to finish — *"if one area hits snags, it works to resolve them while the team moves on"* — **at the cost of more organisational tension and confusion.**

**Systematic.** An **innovation management system**: a senior innovation manager, idea-management software, a cross-functional committee, recognition programmes. **Two outcomes: an innovation-oriented culture, and more ideas.** *"No longer will good ideas wither for the lack of a sounding board."*

> [!warning] ⚠️ The Apple figure that must not be divided
> Kotler: the iPhone brings in **"more than 62 percent of Apple's total global revenues and 77 percent of device unit sales."**
>
> **Divide them and you get: iPhone revenue-per-unit index 0.8052, other devices 1.6522 ⇒ "Apple's non-iPhone devices cost 2.05× an iPhone."** **That is false.**
>
> **The two shares have different bases: 62% is of ALL revenue (services included) and 77% is of DEVICE units.** *(Apple FY2015, external figures labelled as my addition:)*
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | iPhone share of **total** revenue | **66.34%** — his ">62%" is true |
> | iPhone share of **device** revenue | **76.10%** |
> | iPhone share of **device** units | **75.40%** — his "77%" |
> | **iPhone ASP vs other devices** | **\$670.54 vs \$645.49 = 0.96×** |
>
> **⇒ the naive answer is wrong by a factor of 2.13, and matching the bases makes the shares almost equal.** **NEVER FORM A RATIO FROM TWO SHARES UNLESS THEY HAVE THE SAME DENOMINATOR.**

### 8. The product life cycle

*(Figure 9.2 — sales and profits over time. **Five** stages, and the first is not on most students' lists:)*

| stage | sales | profits |
|---|---|---|
| **product development** | **zero** | **investment costs mount** |
| **introduction** | slow growth | **nonexistent** — heavy introduction expense |
| **growth** | rapid acceptance | **increasing** |
| **maturity** | slowdown — most potential buyers have bought | **level off or decline** as defence costs rise |
| **decline** | fall off | drop |

> [!note] ⚠️ Profits peak BEFORE sales do
> **Sales peak in maturity; profits start falling inside maturity because of "increased marketing outlays to defend the product against competition."** **⇒ a product can be at its sales peak and already past its profit peak, which is why maturity is the stage that needs the most management.**

**The PLC applies at three levels, and differently at each:**

| | example | |
|---|---|---|
| **product class** | gasoline-powered automobiles | **longest cycles** — stay mature a long time |
| **product form** | SUVs, dial telephones, VHS tapes, film cameras | **the standard PLC shape** |
| **brand** | the Ford Escape | **can change quickly** with competitive attack |

*Kotler's illustration: laundry soaps (class) and powdered detergents (form) have had long lives, but **the leading brands 100 years ago were Fels-Naptha and Octagon** and today they are Tide and Gain.*

**Styles, fashions and fads** *(Figure 9.3)*:

- **style** — a basic and distinctive mode of expression *(colonial, ranch; formal, casual; realist, surrealist)*. **Lasts generations, with several periods of renewed interest.**
- **fashion** — a currently accepted style. **Grows slowly, holds, declines slowly.** *(Business attire → business casual.)*
- **fad** — a temporary period of unusually high sales. **May be a brand's entire life cycle.**

**Pet Rocks, costed:** **1.5 million beach pebbles at \$4 = \$6,000,000**, broken in October and *"sunk like a stone by the next February"* — **about five months, \$1.2m a month.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ The fad is [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s three levels at their most extreme
> **The actual product was a rock; the core customer value was a joke, delivered by a spoof training manual.** **⇒ essentially 100% of what the buyer paid for was the augmentation.**
>
> **And the range of the PLC is enormous: TABASCO's 147 years is 353× the Pet Rock's entire life.** *That range is exactly why Kotler warns the PLC is hard to use for forecasting.*

> [!warning] ⚠️ His three honest limitations of the PLC — worth as much as the concept
> **(1) You cannot forecast the sales level at each stage, the length of each stage, or the shape of the curve** *(and §8 shows the shapes span five months to 147 years)*.
> **(2) ⚠️ Strategy is both a cause and a result of the PLC** — the current position suggests the strategy, and the strategy determines the later position. **⇒ it is not an exogenous timetable, and treating it as one is self-fulfilling: cut support because a product "is in decline" and it will be.**
> **(3) Marketers routinely defy the rules and reposition products back into growth** *(P&G took Mr. Clean and Old Spice "from near extinction to billion-dollar-brand status")*.

### 9. Strategy by stage

**Table 9.2, which extracted whole:**

| | **introduction** | **growth** | **maturity** | **decline** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **sales** | low | rapidly rising | **peak** | declining |
| **cost/customer** | **high** | average | low | low |
| **profits** | **negative** | rising | **high** | declining |
| **customers** | innovators | early adopters | mainstream | laggards |
| **competitors** | few | growing | **stable, beginning to decline** | declining |
| **objective** | create engagement and **trial** | **maximise market share** | **maximise profit while defending share** | **reduce expenditure and milk the brand** |
| **product** | basic product | extensions, service, warranty | diversify brands and models | phase out weak items |
| **price** | **cost-plus** | **penetrate** | match or beat competitors | cut |
| **distribution** | selective | intensive | more intensive | **selective — phase out unprofitable outlets** |
| **advertising** | awareness among early adopters and dealers | engagement in the mass market | **stress brand differences** | reduce to retain hard-core loyals |
| **sales promotion** | **heavy, to entice trial** | **reduce** | **increase, to encourage brand switching** | minimal |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Two rows in that table reverse direction and are the commonest exam trap
> **Sales promotion is heavy in introduction, REDUCED in growth, INCREASED again in maturity, minimal in decline** — a U-shape, because in growth the demand is already there and promotion would be paid to people who would buy anyway. **In maturity it comes back because the job has changed from *creating* demand to *stealing* it.**
>
> **Distribution goes selective → intensive → more intensive → selective again**, for the same reason in reverse.
>
> **⇒ neither row is monotone, and memorising them as "more of everything as you grow" gets both wrong.** **The customers row explains why: they are [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s adopter categories in order.**

**The growth stage's central trade-off, stated by Kotler as a choice:** *"the firm faces a trade-off between high market share and high current profit. By spending a lot of money on product improvement, promotion, and distribution, the company can capture a dominant position. In doing so, however, it gives up maximum current profit, which it hopes to make up in the next stage."*

**Maturity — three modifications** *(and management should not merely defend: "a good offense is the best defense")*:

| | |
|---|---|
| **modify the market** | new users, new segments, **more usage among present customers** *(3M's Post-it "Go Ahead" campaign; Harley and Axe reaching women; Weight Watchers and Bath & Body Works reaching men)* |
| **modify the product** | quality, features, style, packaging, technology platform *(Crayola's app-linked drawing kits)* |
| **modify the marketing mix** | price, promotion, services, **new channels** |

*(Quaker used all three at once: a ~\$100m "Quaker Up" campaign retargeting mothers under 35, new products, new packaging — and **slimmed the Quaker man by 20 pounds**.)*

**Decline — maintain, harvest, or drop.** **Kotler's real argument is the hidden cost of *not* deciding:** a weak product *"may take up too much of management's time… requires frequent price and inventory adjustments… advertising and sales-force attention that might be better used"*, and **"the biggest cost may well lie in the future"** — it delays the search for replacements. **Note that harvesting and selling are incompatible: *"if the company plans to find a buyer, it will not want to run down the product through harvesting."***

### 10. Social responsibility and international product marketing

**Regulation touches acquiring and dropping products, patents, quality and safety, and warranties.** *(The **Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972** created the CPSC, which can ban or seize harmful products.)* **Product liability is the second-largest litigation concern for manufacturers, behind only labour and employment.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Toyota: **\$1.6bn** settlement, **11 million** vehicles recalled | **\$145.45 per vehicle** |
| GM: **>\$2bn** in fines and settlements, **>120 deaths** | **~\$16.7m per death** |

**Internationally the choice is standardise or adapt.** **Standardisation** gives a consistent image and lower design, manufacturing and marketing costs; **adaptation** answers real differences. *(McDonald's operates in 100+ countries: salmon burgers in Norway, mashed-potato burgers in China, the McFalafel in Israel, the Nürnburger in Germany.)*

> [!warning] ⚠️ McDonald's France — the figure that does not prove what it appears to
> **"The average French McDonald's customer spends about four times what an American customer spends per visit"**, and France is its **second-most-profitable** market. **But the same paragraph explains that French meal times are longer and the interiors were redesigned so customers "want to linger."**
>
> | if a French sitting lasts | revenue per **seat-hour** |
> |---|---|
> | 1× as long | 4.00× |
> | 2× | 2.00× |
> | **4×** | **1.00×** |
>
> **⇒ spend per VISIT is not spend per unit of CAPACITY, and for a capacity-constrained service the second is what pays.** **The 4× alone is consistent with no improvement at all.**
>
> *(That is [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s perishability again: an empty seat-hour cannot be stored, so the right denominator is time, not transactions. The profitability claim is almost certainly true — but it is true for reasons the 4× does not establish.)*

**Services are going global fastest of all** *(Deutsche Bank: 28m customers, 2,700 branches, 70+ countries = **10,370 customers per branch**; Walmart: 260m customers weekly in 28 countries, international **~29% of sales**; Carrefour: 10,000+ stores in 34 countries)* — and **"service firms are no longer simply following their manufacturing customers. Instead, they are taking the lead."**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (The process and the odds.)** (a) Give the eight steps. (b) What are the odds against a new product? (c) Why does idea generation have the opposite goal from every step after it?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Idea generation → idea screening → concept development and testing → marketing strategy development → business analysis → product development → test marketing → commercialization.**
>
> **(b) A concept has a 13.33% chance — 1 in 7.5 — and a raw idea far less.**
>
> Multiply Kotler's two figures: **two-thirds of concepts are never launched** (so $\tfrac13$ launch) and **60% of launches fail** (so 40% survive): $\tfrac13\times0.40 = 13.33\%$.
>
> **⚠️ But that is conditional on already having a concept.** His own funnels measure earlier stages:
>
> | | | |
> |---|---|---|
> | AT&T TIP | 28,000 ideas → 75 funded | **0.2679%** |
> | Cisco I-Prize | 824 → 32 semifinalists | 3.8835% |
>
> **Chaining AT&T's idea→project rate with the concept→success rate: 28,000 ideas → 75 funded → ~10 successes = ⚠️ 1 IN 2,800.**
>
> *(This chains rates from different firms, so it fixes the order of magnitude, not a precise estimate.)*
>
> **(c) Because the process is a funnel, and you cannot narrow what you never widened.**
>
> Kotler is explicit: *"The purpose of idea generation is to create a large number of ideas. The purpose of the succeeding stages is to reduce that number."*
>
> **⇒ if the conversion rate through the funnel is roughly fixed, the only lever on the number of successes is the number of ideas entering it.** **That is the entire business case for crowdsourcing** — and it also explains Cisco's complaint that evaluation was *"far more labor-intensive than we'd anticipated"* (six people, full-time, three months, for 824 ideas). **Widening the top widens the screening bill in proportion.**

**2. (Hard — screening.)** (a) State R-W-W. (b) Kotler requires yes to all three. What does that do statistically? (c) Is it the right design?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Is it REAL?** (real need and desire, a clear concept). **Can we WIN?** (sustainable competitive advantage, sufficient resources). **Is it WORTH DOING?** (fits the growth strategy, sufficient profit potential).
>
> **(b) It makes the screen highly specific and rather insensitive.**
>
> A conjunction of three judgements. If a **good** idea is judged correctly on each with probability $q$, it survives with $q^3$:
>
> | $q$ | **good ideas wrongly killed** |
> |---|---|
> | 0.95 | 14.26% |
> | **0.90** | **27.10%** |
> | 0.80 | 48.80% |
>
> If a **bad** idea is correctly rejected on each with probability $r$, any one "no" kills it:
>
> | $r$ | **bad ideas that slip through** |
> |---|---|
> | **0.90** | **0.10%** |
> | 0.70 | 2.70% |
>
> **⚠️ At 90% per-question accuracy the screen kills 27.1% of good ideas to keep 99.9% of bad ones out.**
>
> **(c) Yes — and the justification is a cost ratio Kotler never computes.**
>
> His own claim that *"product development costs rise greatly in later stages"* means the two errors are not symmetric. Taking a cost multiplier of 4 per stage across his eight stages:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | bad idea killed at stage 1 | **1** |
> | bad idea killed at stage 8 | **21,845** |
>
> **⇒ a false positive that survives to commercialization costs 21,845× what it costs to catch immediately** — and his own figures show the last stage really is where the money goes (**\$38m** on one month of Apple Watch TV advertising, **\$150m** on Tide Pods, **~\$400m** on the Surface).
>
> **⇒ THE SHAPE OF A SCREEN SHOULD FOLLOW THE COST ASYMMETRY OF ITS TWO ERRORS.** With that ratio, sacrificing sensitivity for specificity is correct.
>
> **⚠️ And this is [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s multiplicity result in a third setting.** There, 20 independent segment tests gave a **64.2%** chance of at least one false positive. **Same mathematics, opposite direction: combining independent tests with AND is conservative, with OR is permissive — neither is neutral, and which you want depends entirely on which error costs more.**

**3. (Hard — the concept test.)** (a) Distinguish idea, concept and image. (b) Kotler gets 2% "definitely" and 5% "probably". What is the forecast? (c) What does he leave out?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Product idea** = an idea for a product the firm could offer. **Product concept** = a detailed version *stated in meaningful consumer terms*. **Product image** = *how consumers perceive* it.
>
> **⚠️ The chain is one of decreasing ownership**: the firm writes the idea, drafts the concept, and only *influences* the image — the same asymmetry [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]] found between targeting (a choice) and positioning (an outcome).
>
> **(b) Naively 7.00%. Properly about 2.90%.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | naive top-two-box (2% + 5%) | **7.00%** |
> | weighted 0.70 / 0.30 | **2.90%** |
> | conservative 0.60 / 0.20 | 2.20% |
>
> **⚠️ A factor of 2.41.**
>
> **(c) The correction. He names the problem — "people do not always carry out their stated intentions" — and stops there.**
>
> The standard fix is to discount stated intent: "definitely" converts at roughly 0.7 and "probably" at roughly 0.3, **with the weights calibrated from the firm's own past launches.** *(The calibration matters more than the particular weights — an uncalibrated 0.7/0.3 is a convention, a calibrated one is a measurement.)*
>
> **⇒ and the consequence is not academic.** On his own 50,000-car year-one plan the two readings imply target populations of **714,286** and **1,724,138** — the difference between a market that exists and one that does not.
>
> **⚠️ Worse, it compounds with §5.** A naive concept test over-forecasts volume by 2.41×, and exercise 4 shows his plan turns a 20% volume miss into a loss **75% worse** than its stated cap. **Kotler prints both facts and connects neither.**

**4. (Hard — solve his plan.)** From the marketing strategy statement, find the contribution per car and the fixed cost. Then find the break-even and test the plan's stated \$15m loss cap.

> [!example]- Solution
> **Stated:** retail **\$28,800** less **15%** to dealers ⇒ wholesale **\$24,480**; year 1 **50,000 cars, loss ≤ \$15m**; year 2 **90,000 cars, profit \$25m**; marketing **\$50m** in year 1, **"raised each year by about 10 percent"** ⇒ **\$55m**.
>
> **Two equations, two unknowns.** With $c$ = contribution per car and $F$ = fixed cost:
> $$50{,}000c - F - 50\text{m} = -15\text{m}, \qquad 90{,}000c - F - 55\text{m} = +25\text{m}$$
> Subtracting: $40{,}000c = 45\text{m}$, so
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **contribution per car** | **\$1,125.00** |
> | implied variable cost | \$23,355.00 |
> | fixed cost | **\$21,250,000** |
> | **contribution margin** | **4.60% of wholesale** |
>
> *(Both years check exactly: $50{,}000(1125)-21.25\text{m}-50\text{m}=-15\text{m}$ ✓ and $90{,}000(1125)-21.25\text{m}-55\text{m}=+25\text{m}$ ✓.)*
>
> **Break-even** $=(F+M)/c$:
>
> | | break-even | plan | |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **year 1** | **63,333** | 50,000 | **21.1% below break-even** |
> | year 2 | 67,778 | 90,000 | 24.7% cushion |
>
> **⚠️ So the year-one loss is not an accident of launch costs — it is a planned volume shortfall**, and the plan never says so.
>
> **Now the sensitivity, and this is where the plan fails:**
>
> | miss | units | result | **vs the cap** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 10% | 45,000 | −\$20.6m | **+37.5% worse** |
> | **20%** | 40,000 | **−\$26.3m** | **+75.0% worse** |
> | 30% | 35,000 | −\$31.9m | +112.5% worse |
>
> **⚠️ "A loss of not more than \$15 million" is not a cap. It is a point forecast that holds only if the volume forecast does.**
>
> **The mechanism is operating leverage.** With a **4.60%** contribution margin, \$71.25m of fixed and marketing cost must be covered \$1,125 at a time — **so almost nothing absorbs a shortfall, and every lost car takes 100% of its contribution off the bottom line.**
>
> *(Two honest notes. The solution assumes the wholesale price is unchanged in year two — his part (iii) says price *may* rise, which would raise $c$ and improve everything. And the implied 4.60% margin is thin for a manufactured good, which is presumably why part (iii) plans a price rise at all. **The plan's long-run viability depends on an increase it describes as conditional.**)*

**5. (PLC and cannibalisation.)** (a) Give the five stages and say when profits peak. (b) Which two rows of Table 9.2 are non-monotone, and why? (c) Work the Pop-Tarts problem.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Product development → introduction → growth → maturity → decline.** *(The first is the one most often omitted: sales are zero and investment costs mount.)*
>
> **⚠️ Profits peak BEFORE sales do.** Sales peak in maturity, but profits *"level off or decline… because of increased marketing outlays to defend the product against competition."* **⇒ a product can be at its sales peak and already past its profit peak** — which is why maturity, not growth, is where most marketing management happens.
>
> **(b) Sales promotion and distribution — and the customers row explains both.**
>
> **Sales promotion:** heavy (entice trial) → **reduced** (growth) → **increased** (maturity) → minimal. **A U-shape.** In growth the demand already exists, so promotion would pay people who would buy anyway; in maturity the job changes from *creating* demand to *stealing* it — Table 9.2 says exactly that: *"increase to encourage brand switching."*
>
> **Distribution:** selective → intensive → more intensive → **selective again**, phasing out unprofitable outlets.
>
> **⇒ memorising the table as "more of everything as you grow, less as you decline" gets both rows wrong.** The customers row is the key: **innovators → early adopters → mainstream → laggards** is [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s adopter categories in order, and each group needs a different instrument.
>
> **(c) It loses \$50,000, and the break-even cannibalisation rate is 78.57%.**
>
> First, the trap in the setup:
>
> | | price | var. cost | **contribution** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Gone Nutty! | \$1.20 | \$0.55 | **\$0.65** |
> | original | \$1.00 | \$0.30 | **\$0.70** |
>
> **⚠️ Price +20%, variable cost +83.3%, so contribution −7.14%.** **The "premium" product earns less per package than the one it displaces** — reading the price alone gets the sign wrong.
>
> Then, with 5m packages at 80% cannibalisation and \$500,000 of added fixed cost:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | new contribution | 5.0m × \$0.65 = **\$3,250,000** |
> | cannibalised | 4.0m × \$0.70 = **\$2,800,000** |
> | net contribution | \$450,000 |
> | added fixed cost | \$500,000 |
> | **net effect** | **−\$50,000** |
>
> Break-even $c^*$ solves $5\text{m}(0.65) - 5\text{m}\,c^*(0.70) = 500{,}000$, giving $c^* = 78.57\%$. **The plan assumes 80% — it fails by 1.43 percentage points.**
>
> **⚠️ This is the second confirmation of [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s result**, where I derived that a line extension's cannibalisation rate is approximately the brand's own market share. **Here Kotler assumes 80% for a dominant brand and the launch fails at exactly that rate.**
>
> **⇒ and the general lesson is sharper than either: a product can be a commercial success and a financial failure simultaneously.** **Five million packages is a good launch by every marketing measure in this book, and it destroys \$50,000 of profit.** *Volume is not the objective; incremental contribution is.*

## 📝 Summary

- **Two routes to a new product: acquisition and development.**
- **⚠️ 60% of new CPG products fail and two-thirds of concepts are never launched ⇒ a concept succeeds 13.33% of the time, 1 in 7.5.**
- **The eight steps: idea generation → screening → concept development/testing → marketing strategy → business analysis → product development → test marketing → commercialization.**
- **⚠️ Step 1 maximises ideas; every later step minimises them.** The process is a funnel.
- **⚠️ CHAINING HIS OWN FIGURES: ~1 COMMERCIAL SUCCESS PER 2,800 RAW IDEAS** — which is the business case for crowdsourcing, since the only lever is the number entering.
- **R-W-W: is it real, can we win, is it worth doing — all three must be yes.**
- **⚠️ THE CONJUNCTION KILLS 27.1% OF GOOD IDEAS AT 90% ACCURACY** and lets **0.10%** of bad ones through — **and that is correct, because a bad idea reaching commercialization costs 21,845× one killed at stage 1.**
- **⚠️ The shape of a screen should follow the cost asymmetry of its two errors.** [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|Ch. 04]]'s multiplicity, reversed.
- **Idea → concept → image is a chain of decreasing ownership.**
- **⚠️ THE INTENTION GAP: 7% naive vs 2.90% weighted, 2.41× apart** — Kotler names it and supplies no correction.
- **⚠️ HIS MARKETING STRATEGY STATEMENT SOLVES: \$1,125 contribution per car, \$23,355 variable cost, \$21.25m fixed — both years exact.**
- **⚠️ 4.60% contribution margin; break-even 63,333 cars against a plan of 50,000 — the year-1 loss is a PLANNED volume shortfall.**
- **⚠️ A 20% VOLUME MISS MAKES THE LOSS 75% WORSE THAN THE STATED CAP.** Operating leverage; "not more than \$15 million" is a point forecast, not a cap.
- **⇒ §4 and §5 compound: a naive concept test over-forecasts by 2.41×, and the plan converts that into a loss that more than doubles.**
- **Test marketing is a value-of-information decision** — three years and 45 prototypes for Doritos Locos Tacos; none for a line extension.
- **⚠️ POP-TARTS GONE NUTTY! LOSES \$50,000**; break-even cannibalisation **78.57%** vs 80% assumed. **And its contribution is 7.14% LOWER than the product it replaces** despite a 20% higher price.
- **⇒ a product can be a commercial success and a financial failure at once.**
- **⚠️ NEVER DIVIDE TWO SHARES WITH DIFFERENT DENOMINATORS** — 62% of *revenue* over 77% of *device units* gives 2.05× where the truth is 0.96×.
- **Five PLC stages, and ⚠️ profits peak before sales do.**
- **Class > form > brand in cycle length.** Style, fashion, fad. **Pet Rocks: \$6m in five months; TABASCO's 147 years is 353× that.**
- **⚠️ Strategy is both a cause and a result of the PLC** — treating it as a timetable is self-fulfilling.
- **⚠️ Table 9.2's sales-promotion row is U-SHAPED and its distribution row reverses** — because the customers row is [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s adopter categories in order.
- **Maturity: modify the market, the product, or the mix.** **Decline: maintain, harvest or drop — and harvesting forecloses selling.**
- **⚠️ McDonald's France's 4× spend per VISIT says nothing until you divide by time** — for a capacity-constrained service the denominator is seat-hours.

## ⚠️ Important Notes

1. **⚠️ 13.33% is the *concept*'s survival rate, not an idea's.** The idea-level rate is ~100× worse.
2. **Widening the funnel widens the screening bill in proportion** — Cisco: six people, three months, 824 ideas.
3. **⚠️ A conjunctive screen is conservative by construction.** Count the questions before trusting the pass rate.
4. **⚠️ Match the screen's asymmetry to the cost asymmetry of the two errors.**
5. **AND-combining tests is conservative; OR-combining is permissive.** Neither is neutral.
6. **⚠️ Never project stated purchase intent without discounting it.** 2.41× is typical.
7. **Calibrate the intent weights on your own past launches** — the calibration matters more than the weights.
8. **⚠️ A stated loss "cap" with no margin behind it is a point forecast.**
9. **⚠️ Compute break-even volume before believing a launch plan**, and compare it with the plan.
10. **Thin contribution margins mean brutal operating leverage.** 4.60% turns a 20% miss into +75% loss.
11. **⚠️ Forecast error and operating leverage COMPOUND.** Check both together or neither.
12. **⚠️ A higher price is not a higher margin.** Pop-Tarts: +20% price, +83.3% cost, −7.14% contribution.
13. **⚠️ Cannibalisation can sink a launch that hits every volume target.**
14. **A break-even cannibalisation rate is computable — compute it.**
15. **⚠️ NEVER FORM A RATIO FROM TWO SHARES WITH DIFFERENT BASES.**
16. **Test marketing trades information against time and secrecy.** Skip it for line extensions; not for a new category.
17. **⚠️ Profits peak inside maturity, before sales do.**
18. **Product class, form and brand have different cycle lengths** — say which you mean.
19. **⚠️ The PLC cannot forecast stage length or curve shape.** Five months to 147 years.
20. **⚠️ The PLC is self-fulfilling if used as a timetable** — strategy is cause and result.
21. **Table 9.2's promotion row is U-shaped; its distribution row reverses.**
22. **Harvesting and selling a declining product are incompatible.**
23. **The biggest cost of a weak product is the replacement it delays.**
24. **⚠️ Spend per visit ≠ spend per unit of capacity.** Divide by time for a perishable service.
25. **Treat a 90%/yr five-year forecast as a marketing number** — 3× the fastest sustained rate in [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s data.
