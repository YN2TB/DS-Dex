# Business Markets and Business Buyer Behavior

**[[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|Ch. 05]] opened the consumer's black box. This chapter opens an organisation's — where the box contains several people who disagree.**

**Three results.**

**§2a — ⚠️ "business demand is inelastic" is not a separate fact.** *(Computed: a **30% collapse in the leather price raises leather demand 9%** — a derived elasticity of **0.30** — even though final shoe demand is *elastic* at 1.5.)* **⇒ derived elasticity ≈ cost share × final elasticity, so inelasticity follows from *derived demand plus a cost share below one*.**

**§2b — ⚠️ and neither is "more fluctuating".** *(Computed: a **10% rise in consumer demand doubles machine orders; a 10% fall wipes them out entirely.** And the amplification factor is the **asset life** — 4-year assets amplify ×4, 20-year assets ×20.)* **Kotler states the effect — "a small percentage increase in consumer demand can cause large increases in business demand" — and never shows it.**

**⇒ so of Kotler's three demand properties, *two follow from the first*.** **He lists them as three parallel facts.**

**§4 — the buy class is a contest over classification.** **An incumbent wants every purchase to be a straight rebuy; a challenger's entire strategy is to convert one into a modified rebuy.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. How business markets differ

| | |
|---|---|
| **market structure and demand** | far **fewer but far larger** buyers; demand is **derived**, **inelastic** and **more fluctuating** |
| **nature of the buying unit** | more **decision participants**, more **professional** purchasing effort — **"business buyers usually face more complex buying decisions"** |
| **decision process** | more **formalized** — detailed specifications, written purchase orders, careful supplier searches, formal approval; and **both sides depend on each other** |

> [!note] The concentration point
> **"A few buyers often account for most of the purchasing."** **Goodyear's consumer market is millions of car owners; its business fate depends on "getting orders from only a handful of large automakers."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ And §2 shows the three demand properties are not three facts
> **Two of them follow from the first.**

### 2. ⚠️ Derived demand, and what it causes

**Business demand is *derived* — "it ultimately derives from the demand for consumer goods."** *(Gore-Tex sells only when people buy outdoor apparel; Corning's Gorilla Glass sells only when people buy phones with it.)*

#### 2a. Derived demand ⇒ inelastic demand

**Kotler's own example, stated and not worked:** **"A drop in the price of leather will not cause shoe manufacturers to buy much more leather *unless* it results in lower shoe prices that, in turn, increase consumer demand for shoes."**

**That is a chain, and each link dilutes the signal:**

$$\text{leather price}\downarrow x\%\ \to\ \text{shoe cost}\downarrow(x\cdot\text{share})\%\ \to\ \text{shoe quantity}\uparrow(\cdot\eta)\%\ \to\ \text{leather quantity}\uparrow$$

$$\boxed{\ \text{derived elasticity}\approx(\text{cost share})\times(\text{final elasticity})\ }$$

| leather price falls | cost share | final elasticity | shoe price falls | shoe qty rises | **derived elasticity** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **30%** | **20%** | **1.5** | 6.0% | **9.0%** | **0.30** |
| 30% | 20% | 3.0 | 6.0% | 18.0% | 0.60 |
| 30% | 50% | 1.5 | 15.0% | 22.5% | 0.75 |
| **30%** | **5%** | 1.5 | 1.5% | 2.2% | **0.07** |
| 30% | 20% | **0.5** | 6.0% | 3.0% | **0.10** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 30% collapse in the leather price raises leather demand by 9%
> **The derived elasticity is 0.30 — *inelastic* — even though final shoe demand is *elastic* at 1.5.**
>
> **Two drivers, both visible in the table: a *small cost share* makes it more inelastic (a 5% share gives 0.07), and *inelastic final demand* makes it more inelastic (0.10).**
>
> **⇒ "business demand is inelastic" is not a fact about business buyers. It is derived demand plus a cost share below one.**

> [!note] ⚠️ And the marketing consequence is sharp
> **Cutting your price buys almost no extra volume**, so a component supplier's growth must come from the **final** market, not from its own price.
>
> **Which is exactly why Kotler's Corning and Gore-Tex examples are *consumer* campaigns run by *ingredient* suppliers** — Corning's "Tough, yet beautiful" is aimed at people who will never buy glass. **That is not brand vanity; it is the only lever the elasticity leaves.**
>
> *(Elasticity itself is [[Macroeconomics & Microeconomics/contents/02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|Macro/Micro ch. 02]]'s, per [[00-Index]]'s boundary. This uses it and does not re-derive it.)*

#### 2b. ⚠️ Derived demand ⇒ fluctuating demand: the accelerator

**Kotler: "the demand for many business goods and services tends to change more — and more quickly — than does the demand for consumer goods… ⚠️ *A small percentage increase in consumer demand can cause large increases in business demand.*"** **He states the effect and never shows it.**

*(Modelled: a shoe factory needs 1 machine per 1,000 pairs/year. **Machines last 10 years, so a stable stock of 100 generates 10 replacement orders a year.**)*

| consumer demand | required stock | expansion | replacement | **total orders** | **change** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 100 | 0 | 10 | 10 | — |
| **+10%** | 110 | 10 | 10 | **20** | **+100%** |
| +20% | 120 | 20 | 10 | 30 | **+200%** |
| **−10%** | 90 | −10 | 10 | **0** | **−100%** |
| −20% | 80 | −20 | 10 | **0** | −100% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 10% rise in consumer demand doubles machine orders. A 10% fall wipes them out entirely.
> **The factory already has more machines than it needs, so it buys none — not even replacements.**
>
> **⚠️ And the amplification factor is the *asset life*:**
>
> | asset life | a +10% demand change moves orders by |
> |---|---|
> | 4 years | **+40%** |
> | 10 years | **+100%** |
> | 20 years | **+200%** |
>
> **⇒ longer-lived capital amplifies more.** **That is why capital-goods markets are the most volatile in the economy, and it has nothing to do with business buyers being fickle.**

> [!note] ⚠️ And the asymmetry matters
> **The downside is capped at −100% — you cannot order fewer than zero machines — while the upside is not.** **So the series is bounded below and unbounded above: boom-bust, not symmetric oscillation.**
>
> *([[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|Ch. 03]] named "economic" as a macro force to watch. **This is the mechanism by which a mild consumer recession becomes a catastrophic one for a machine-tool supplier.**)*

### 3. The buying center — five roles, and it is not an org chart

| role | what they control |
|---|---|
| **users** | will use the product — **often *initiate* the proposal** and help define specifications |
| **influencers** | help define specifications and supply evaluation information — **technical personnel are "particularly important"** |
| **buyers** | **formal authority to select the supplier** and arrange terms — their major role is vendor selection and negotiation |
| **deciders** | formal or informal power to **select or approve** final suppliers |
| **gatekeepers** | **control the flow of information** — purchasing agents, technical staff, even secretaries |

**Three things make this hard, all Kotler's own:**

1. **It is not a fixed unit.** **"A set of buying roles assumed by different people for different purchases"** — **one person for a routine buy, ⚠️ *20, 30 or more* for a complex purchase in a large company.**
2. **Participants may be invisible.** **"Sometimes, even the people in the buying center are not aware of all the buying participants."** *(His corporate-jet example: the decision may actually be made by a board member who likes flying and "may work behind the scenes to sway the decision.")*
3. **Each participant uses different evaluation criteria.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ So the selling problem is not persuasion — it is identification
> **You cannot address criteria you have not discovered, held by people you have not identified.**
>
> **That is why B2B selling is *personal* ([[12 - Integrated Marketing Communications and Digital Marketing|ch. 12]]'s seven-step process) rather than broadcast — broadcast cannot find a gatekeeper.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Gatekeepers are the role with no consumer analogue
> **They do not decide anything — they decide *who gets to speak*.** **A purchasing agent who blocks access has settled the outcome without expressing a preference.**

### 4. Buy classes and the eight-stage process

| buy class | | who is advantaged |
|---|---|---|
| **straight rebuy** | reorder routinely, no modifications — **fewest decisions** | **the "in" supplier** |
| **modified rebuy** | modify specifications, prices, terms or suppliers | **"out" suppliers "may see the modified rebuy as an opportunity"** |
| **new task** | buying for the first time — **most decisions, most participants, most information** | **"the marketer's greatest opportunity and challenge"** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The buy class predicts everything else — and it is a contest over classification
> **It determines the size of the buying center, the length of the process, the information sought, and which supplier is advantaged.**
>
> **⇒ an incumbent wants every purchase classified a *straight rebuy*; a challenger's entire strategy is to convert one into a *modified rebuy*.** **Kotler describes the three and does not say they are something to be fought over.**

**Systems selling (or solutions selling)** — **buying a complete packaged solution from a single seller.** **⚠️ It is a straight-rebuy strategy: bundling raises switching costs, converting a repeatable competitive purchase into a relationship.**

**The eight stages** *(a new task runs all eight; rebuys compress or skip)*:

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | problem recognition | 5 | proposal solicitation |
| 2 | general need description | 6 | supplier selection |
| **3** | **product specification** | 7 | order-routine specification |
| 4 | supplier search | **8** | **performance review** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Stage 3 is the highest-leverage moment, and it happens before any supplier is formally contacted
> **A supplier who helps write the specification has shaped the criteria every rival will be judged against.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Stage 8 has no consumer counterpart
> **The performance review feeds straight back into stage 1 of the next cycle — so today's delivery performance is tomorrow's buy-class decision.** **[[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]]'s customer lifetime value with a formal measurement process attached.**

### 5. Influences, e-procurement, and the other markets

**Four layers of influence, nesting outward:** **environmental** (demand, economic outlook, technology, politics, competition) → **organizational** (objectives, policies, procedures, systems) → **interpersonal** (authority, status, empathy, persuasiveness) → **individual** (age, income, education, personality, risk attitudes).

> [!warning] ⚠️ Same shape as ch. 05's four factor groups — and the same point
> **Outermost is least controllable, innermost most.** **And *business buyers are people*: interpersonal and individual factors are on the list precisely because the rational-buyer model is incomplete.**
>
> **⇒ the recurring exam trap is assuming B2B buying is rational and B2C emotional.** **Both are both.** **What differs is the *number of people* and the *formality of the process*.**

**E-procurement and online purchasing** — lower transaction costs, faster ordering, wider supplier search.

> [!note] ⚠️ But note its directional effect
> **It pushes purchases toward the *straight-rebuy* end by making reordering frictionless — which advantages incumbents.** **The mirror of [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s perceptual filters, achieved by workflow rather than psychology.**

**Two more markets with different rules:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **institutional** | schools, hospitals, prisons — **low budgets and captive patrons**, so the objective is not profit and the criterion is often cost minimisation subject to a standard |
| **government** | **the largest buyer of all**, and **dominated by public visibility**: open bidding, documentation, and **non-economic objectives** (favouring small or minority-owned firms) |

> [!note] ⚠️ The government implication
> **A supplier optimising only on price and quality will lose to one that also handles the paperwork and the policy criteria.**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Differences.)** (a) How do business markets differ? (b) What is derived demand? (c) Why is the concentration point important?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Three groups: market structure and demand, the buying unit, the decision process.**
>
> **Structure and demand: fewer and larger buyers; demand derived, inelastic, more fluctuating.** **Buying unit: more participants, more professional.** **Process: more formalized, and mutually dependent.**
>
> **⚠️ But §2 shows the demand list is not three parallel facts** — inelasticity and volatility both *follow* from derived demand, which Kotler does not say.
>
> **(b) Demand that "ultimately derives from the demand for consumer goods."**
>
> **Gore-Tex sells only when people buy outdoor apparel; Gorilla Glass sells only when people buy phones containing it.** **⇒ a business supplier's market is not its customer's budget — it is its customer's customer's behaviour.**
>
> **(c) Because it inverts the marketing problem.**
>
> **Goodyear's consumer market is millions of car owners; its business fate depends on "a handful of large automakers".** **⇒ with few large buyers, losing one account is a strategic event rather than a statistical one**, so account management replaces market management, and the relationship investment [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]] reserved for "true friends" becomes the *normal* mode rather than a selective one.
>
> *(It also explains the mutual dependence Kotler notes: when a supplier has five customers, each one has real power over it — and knows it.)*

**2. (Hard — inelasticity.)** (a) Work Kotler's leather example. (b) What determines the derived elasticity? (c) What follows for a component supplier?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) A 30% price collapse raises demand 9%.**
>
> **The chain: leather price falls 30% → with leather at 20% of shoe cost, shoe cost falls 6% → with final shoe elasticity 1.5, shoe quantity rises 9% → leather quantity rises 9%.**
>
> $$\text{derived elasticity}=\frac{9\%}{30\%}=\mathbf{0.30}$$
>
> **⚠️ Inelastic — even though final shoe demand is *elastic* at 1.5.**
>
> **(b) Roughly the cost share times the final elasticity.**
>
> | | derived elasticity |
> |---|---|
> | 20% share, final 1.5 | **0.30** |
> | **5% share**, final 1.5 | **0.07** |
> | 20% share, **final 0.5** | **0.10** |
>
> **⇒ a small cost share or an inelastic final demand each make the derived demand more inelastic, and they compound.**
>
> **⚠️ So "business demand is inelastic" is not a fact about business buyers at all** — it is arithmetic. **A business buyer facing a 30% input price cut is not being stubborn by failing to double its order; it has nowhere to sell the extra output.**
>
> **(c) Growth must come from the final market, not from your price.**
>
> **⚠️ Cutting your price buys almost no extra volume** — it transfers margin to your customer and moves 9% of quantity for 30% of price, which is value-destroying at any sane cost structure.
>
> **⇒ so a component supplier's only real lever is to increase *final* demand**, which is exactly why Kotler's Corning and Gore-Tex examples are **consumer campaigns run by ingredient suppliers**. **"Tough, yet beautiful" is aimed at people who will never buy a sheet of glass.**
>
> **That is not brand vanity. It is the only lever the elasticity leaves** — and it also explains ingredient branding ("Intel Inside", "Gore-Tex", "Dolby") as a category: **the supplier is trying to make itself a reason the *final* buyer chooses the *customer's* product.**

**3. (Hard — the accelerator.)** (a) Model the machine orders. (b) What sets the amplification? (c) Why is it asymmetric?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) A 10% consumer swing doubles or eliminates machine orders.**
>
> *(A stable stock of 100 machines lasting 10 years generates 10 replacement orders a year.)*
>
> | consumer demand | required stock | expansion | replacement | **orders** | change |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 0% | 100 | 0 | 10 | 10 | — |
> | **+10%** | 110 | +10 | 10 | **20** | **+100%** |
> | **−10%** | 90 | −10 | 10 | **0** | **−100%** |
>
> **⚠️ On the downside the factory already has more machines than it needs, so it orders none — not even replacements.**
>
> **This is Kotler's stated but unshown claim: "a small percentage increase in consumer demand can cause large increases in business demand."**
>
> **(b) The asset life.**
>
> **Replacement demand is $1/L$ of the stock, so a growth rate $g$ moves orders by $g\times L$ relative to the replacement baseline.**
>
> | asset life | +10% demand ⇒ |
> |---|---|
> | 4 years | +40% |
> | 10 years | +100% |
> | **20 years** | **+200%** |
>
> **⇒ longer-lived capital amplifies more**, which is why machine tools, aircraft and construction equipment are the most cyclical markets in any economy. **It has nothing to do with business buyers being fickle** — the same steady consumer market produces wild swings in their orders.
>
> **(c) Because orders cannot go below zero.**
>
> **⚠️ The downside is capped at −100% and the upside is not.** **⇒ the series is bounded below and unbounded above — boom-bust, not symmetric oscillation.**
>
> **And a second asymmetry follows: recovery is delayed.** **After a downturn the factory holds excess machines, so orders stay at zero until consumer demand climbs back *past* its old peak.** **The supplier's recession therefore starts earlier and ends later than its customer's** — which is the mechanism [[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|ch. 03]] gestured at when it listed "economic" as a force to monitor.

**4. (The buying center.)** (a) Name the five roles. (b) What makes it hard to work with? (c) Which role has no consumer analogue?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Users, influencers, buyers, deciders, gatekeepers.**
>
> **Users often *initiate* the proposal. Influencers (especially technical staff) define specifications. Buyers hold formal authority to select and negotiate. Deciders approve. Gatekeepers control information flow.**
>
> **(b) It is not fixed, not visible, and not unanimous.**
>
> - **Not fixed:** "a set of buying roles assumed by different people for different purchases" — **one person for a routine buy, 20 or 30 for a complex one.**
> - **Not visible:** **"sometimes, even the people in the buying center are not aware of all the buying participants"** — Kotler's corporate-jet decision may actually be made by a board member who likes flying and works behind the scenes.
> - **Not unanimous:** each participant uses **different evaluation criteria**.
>
> **⚠️ So the selling problem is *identification*, not persuasion.** **You cannot address criteria you have not discovered, held by people you have not identified.**
>
> **⇒ which is why B2B selling is personal rather than broadcast: broadcast cannot find a gatekeeper**, and no amount of advertising reveals who the hidden decider is.
>
> **(c) Gatekeepers.**
>
> **⚠️ They do not decide anything — they decide *who gets to speak*.** **A purchasing agent who prevents a salesperson from reaching the users has settled the outcome without ever expressing a preference about the product.**
>
> **There is no consumer equivalent** because a consumer's information channels are not controlled by a third party with an interest in the decision. *(The nearest analogue is [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s selective attention — but that is the buyer filtering their own inputs, not someone else filtering them.)*

**5. (Buy classes and process.)** (a) Give the three classes. (b) Why is the classification contested? (c) Which stages matter most?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Straight rebuy** (routine reorder, fewest decisions), **modified rebuy** (change specifications, price, terms or supplier), **new task** (first-time purchase, most decisions and participants).
>
> **(b) ⚠️ Because the class determines who wins before anyone competes.**
>
> **It sets the size of the buying center, the length of the process, the information sought — and which supplier is advantaged.**
>
> - **A straight rebuy is nearly unwinnable for an outsider**: no search, no proposals, no comparison.
> - **A modified rebuy reopens the decision** — Kotler notes "in" suppliers "get nervous" and "out" suppliers "may see the modified rebuy situation as an opportunity."
>
> **⇒ an incumbent's strategy is to keep every purchase classified a straight rebuy; a challenger's entire strategy is to convert one into a modified rebuy** — by creating dissatisfaction, offering a materially different specification, or exploiting a change in the buyer's requirements. **Kotler describes the three classes and never says they are something to be fought over.**
>
> *(And **systems selling** is the incumbent's version of the same move: bundling a complete solution raises switching costs, which converts a repeatable competitive purchase into a relationship. **E-procurement does it too**, by making reordering frictionless.)*
>
> **(c) Stage 3 and stage 8.**
>
> **⚠️ Stage 3 — product specification — is the highest-leverage moment in the whole process, and it happens *before any supplier is formally contacted*.** **A supplier who helps write the specification has shaped the criteria every rival will be judged against.** **By stage 5 (proposal solicitation) the contest may already be decided.**
>
> **⚠️ Stage 8 — performance review — has no consumer counterpart**, and it is what makes B2B relationships sticky in both directions: **the review feeds directly into stage 1 of the next cycle, so today's delivery performance determines tomorrow's buy class.**
>
> **⇒ together they close a loop: perform well → next purchase is a straight rebuy → you help write the next specification → you win again.** **That is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s customer lifetime value with a formal measurement process attached, and it is why B2B account retention is worth what it is.**

## 📝 Summary

- **Business markets: fewer and larger buyers, more participants, more formal processes, mutual dependence.**
- **Demand is derived, inelastic and more fluctuating** — **⚠️ but these are not three facts: the last two FOLLOW from the first.**
- **⚠️ Derived elasticity ≈ cost share × final elasticity** *(computed: a **30% leather price fall raises leather demand 9%** — elasticity **0.30** — from a final elasticity of 1.5)*.
- **⇒ cutting your price buys almost no volume**, so a component supplier's growth must come from the **final** market — **which is why Corning and Gore-Tex run consumer campaigns.**
- **⚠️ THE ACCELERATOR: a 10% rise in consumer demand DOUBLES machine orders; a 10% fall ELIMINATES them** *(computed)*.
- **⚠️ Amplification = asset life** — ×4 for 4-year assets, **×20 for 20-year assets.** **Capital goods are the most cyclical markets in any economy.**
- **⚠️ And it is asymmetric: orders cannot go below zero**, so the pattern is boom-bust, and recovery waits until demand passes its old peak.
- **The buying center has five roles: users, influencers, buyers, deciders, gatekeepers.**
- **⚠️ It is not fixed (1 to 30 people), not visible ("even the people in it are not aware of all the participants"), and not unanimous.** **⇒ the selling problem is IDENTIFICATION, not persuasion.**
- **⚠️ Gatekeepers have no consumer analogue** — they decide *who gets to speak*, settling outcomes without a preference.
- **Three buy classes: straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new task.**
- **⚠️ The classification is contested**: incumbents want straight rebuys, challengers want modified ones. **Systems selling and e-procurement both push toward straight rebuy, advantaging incumbents.**
- **Eight stages.** **⚠️ Stage 3 (specification) is highest-leverage and precedes formal contact; stage 8 (performance review) has no consumer counterpart and feeds the next cycle's buy class.**
- **Four influence layers: environmental → organizational → interpersonal → individual** — **⚠️ same shape as [[05 - Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior|ch. 05]]'s, and the same point: business buyers are people.**
- **⚠️ B2B is not "rational" and B2C "emotional"** — both are both; what differs is the number of people and the formality.
- **Institutional markets: low budgets, captive patrons.** **Government: the largest buyer, dominated by public visibility and non-economic objectives.**

## ⚠️ Important Notes

1. **⚠️ Derived demand causes both inelasticity and volatility.** They are not independent properties.
2. **⚠️ Derived elasticity ≈ cost share × final elasticity.** A small cost share means a very inelastic input.
3. **Price cuts do not buy volume in derived markets** — they transfer margin.
4. **⚠️ Ingredient brands advertise to consumers because that is their only lever.**
5. **⚠️ The accelerator: orders respond to the CHANGE in demand, not its level.**
6. **Amplification equals asset life.** Long-lived capital is the most cyclical.
7. **⚠️ Orders cannot fall below zero**, so downturns are capped and recoveries delayed.
8. **A supplier's recession starts earlier and ends later than its customer's.**
9. **⚠️ The buying center is a set of roles, not a department.** One person or thirty.
10. **Participants can be invisible even to each other.**
11. **⚠️ The B2B problem is identifying the decision-makers**, not convincing them.
12. **Gatekeepers settle outcomes without stating preferences.**
13. **⚠️ The buy class is fought over.** Incumbents want straight rebuys.
14. **Systems selling raises switching costs** — an incumbent strategy.
15. **⚠️ E-procurement advantages incumbents** by making reordering frictionless.
16. **⚠️ Writing the specification decides the contest** before proposals are invited.
17. **The performance review links each purchase to the next.**
18. **⚠️ Business buyers are people.** Interpersonal and individual factors are on Kotler's own list.
19. **Government buyers optimise on visibility and policy**, not only price and quality.
