# Products, Services and Brands

**Five results, all computed from Kotler's own figures — and ⚠️ SIX FIGURES RECOVERED INTACT, the most in any chapter of this book.**

**§3 — ⚠️ THE PACKAGE HAS 0.2 SECONDS.** *(From his own "300 items per minute": a supermarket's 42,000 items would take **2h20m** to walk past and a supercenter's 120,000 **6h40m** — so **a 40-minute trip sees 28.6% of one store and 10.0% of the other**, at **0.20 seconds per item**.)* **He gives the three numbers on one page and never divides them.**

**§5 — ⚠️ "98% SOUNDS GOOD" IS NOT A QUALITY STANDARD, IT IS AN ERROR COUNT — AND IT COMPOUNDS.** *(Back-out: his 356,000 misdirected letters an hour implies **155.9 billion pieces a year**, against the USPS's actual 154.2bn — **1.1% off**; his 1.5m misfilled prescriptions a week implies **3.9 billion a year**, actual ~4.0bn — **2.5% off**. **Both his figures are real.**)* **Then across Emirates' OWN nine-stage service encounter, printed five pages later, 98% per stage is 83.37% end-to-end — one journey in six fails.**

**§7 — ⚠️ KOTLER SAYS LICENSING HAS "GROWN RAPIDLY IN RECENT YEARS". HIS THREE NUMBERS SAY IT DECELERATED FOR THIRTY.** *(\$4bn 1977 → \$55bn 1987 → \$259bn today: **29.97%/yr, then 5.30%/yr** — **a factor of 5.7**, and in real terms **22.1% → 2.7%**.)*

**§7 — ⚠️ THE CANNIBALISATION RATE OF A LINE EXTENSION IS THE BRAND'S OWN MARKET SHARE.** **⇒ the market leader gains least from one, and every extension raises the share that eats the next.** *Kotler asks this exact question about Doritos and leaves it unanswered.*

**§6 — ⚠️ PERISHABILITY IS WHAT MAKES THE FOUR SEASONS PERK CHEAP**, and he defines perishability five pages earlier without connecting them. *(Six free room-nights break even at a **\$2,500** replacement cost if the room is costed at margin and **\$33,333** at rack rate — **13.3× apart on the same policy**.)*

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. What a product is, and its three levels

> **Product** — anything that can be offered to a market for **attention, acquisition, use, or consumption** that might satisfy a want or need.
> **Service** — an activity, benefit or satisfaction offered for sale that is **essentially intangible** and **does not result in the ownership of anything**.

**⚠️ The definition is deliberately broad: products include services, events, persons, places, organizations and ideas.**

*(Figure 8.1, recovered — three concentric levels, each adding customer value:)*

| level | question | contents |
|---|---|---|
| **core customer value** | ***"What is the buyer really buying?"*** | the **problem-solving benefit** |
| **actual product** | what is it made of? | **features, design, quality level, brand name, packaging** |
| **augmented product** | what comes with it? | **delivery and credit, warranty, after-sale service, product support** |

**Kotler's illustrations:** *"In the factory, we make cosmetics; in the store, we sell hope"* (Revson of Revlon); an iPad buyer is buying **"entertainment, self-expression, productivity, and connectivity — a mobile and personal window to the world."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ The three levels are a hierarchy of imitability, and that is the point Kotler leaves implicit
> **The actual product is the easiest to copy and the core value the hardest to displace.** **⇒ a competitor can match your features next quarter; matching what the buyer is *really* buying takes a different offer entirely.**
>
> **This is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s *preemptive* criterion restated as a product architecture** — and §7's brand-positioning ladder is the same ordering a third time.

**Beyond products and services: experiences.** *"Today, as products and services become more commoditized… they are creating and managing customer experiences."* Disney's **"small wows"**; Apple's stores where *"you don't just visit… you experience it."*

### 2. Classifying products

**Consumer products, by how consumers buy them** *(Table 8.1, which extracted whole)*:

| | **convenience** | **shopping** | **specialty** | **unsought** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **buying behavior** | frequent, little planning, **low involvement** | less frequent, much planning, **comparison on price/quality/style** | **strong brand preference and loyalty**, special effort, **little comparison**, **low price sensitivity** | little awareness or knowledge; **negative interest** |
| **price** | low | higher | **high** | varies |
| **distribution** | **widespread** | selective, fewer outlets | **exclusive**, one or a few per area | varies |
| **promotion** | mass, by producer | advertising + personal selling, both | carefully targeted, both | **aggressive** advertising and personal selling |
| **example** | toothpaste, magazines | appliances, furniture | Rolex, fine crystal | life insurance, blood donation |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The table is a *derivation*, not a list — every row follows from the first
> **Because convenience buyers won't search, you must be everywhere** (widespread distribution) **and cheap.** **Because specialty buyers WILL travel, you can be nowhere** (exclusive distribution) **and expensive.** **⇒ the distribution row is the buying-behavior row's consequence.**
>
> **And the *specialty* column is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s pricing power appearing under another name — "low price sensitivity" IS a low elasticity, which IS a downward-sloping firm demand curve.** *Neither chapter says so.*

**Industrial products** *(bought for further processing or to conduct a business — the distinction is **purpose**, not the object: the same lawn mower is a consumer product at home and an industrial product in a landscaping business)*: **materials and parts** (raw; manufactured), **capital items** (installations; accessory equipment), **supplies and services**.

**Also marketable:** organizations, persons, places and ideas — the last being **social marketing**.

### 3. ⚠️ Individual product decisions, and what a package actually gets

*(Figure 8.2, recovered:)* **product attributes → branding → packaging → labeling and logos → product support services.**

**Attributes.** **Quality has two dimensions: *level* (performance quality) and *consistency* (conformance quality — freedom from defects).**

> [!note] ⚠️ The distinction that most students miss
> **"A Chevrolet can have just as much quality as a Rolls-Royce."** **Level should match the *positioning*; consistency should always be high.** **⇒ "high quality" is two claims and only one of them is a strategic choice.**

**Features** are "a competitive tool for differentiating"; add one when **value to customers exceeds cost to the company**. **Style is appearance; *design* "goes to the very heart of a product"** — and *"design begins with observing customers"*, not with prototypes.

**Packaging — and here his own numbers are worth far more than his prose.**

*(Kotler's figures: an average supermarket stocks **42,000 items**; a Walmart supercenter **120,000**; the typical shopper makes **three of four purchase decisions in store** and passes **~300 items per minute**.)*

| | items | **time to walk past them all** |
|---|---|---|
| average supermarket | 42,000 | **140 min = 2h20m** |
| Walmart supercenter | 120,000 | **400 min = 6h40m** |

| trip length | items passed | % of a supermarket | % of a supercenter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 min | 6,000 | 14.3% | 5.0% |
| **40 min** | **12,000** | **28.6%** | **10.0%** |
| 60 min | 18,000 | 42.9% | 15.0% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ At 300 items per minute, each package gets 0.20 SECONDS
> **And no shopper ever sees the whole store — a normal trip passes under a third of a supermarket and a tenth of a supercenter.**
>
> **⇒ that is why three of four decisions are made in store *and* why the package must work in a fifth of a second.** **Kotler prints all three numbers on one page and never divides them.**
>
> **It is also the whole argument of his Real Marketing 8.1 on logo redesign**: a mark that must survive 0.2 seconds on a shelf must also survive a **2.5-inch smartwatch**, which is why Google rebuilt its logo around a sans-serif that "shrinks down more legibly."

**Poor packaging costs both ways:** "wrap rage" from sealed clamshells, and **overpackaging**. *(Amazon's Frustration-Free Packaging: 2,000+ companies, 200,000+ items, 75 million shipped, "nearly 60 million square feet of cardboard and 25 million pounds of packaging waste" eliminated — **0.80 sq ft and 5.33 oz per item**, checked in the gaps callout.)*

**Labels and logos** identify, describe and promote — and are legally constrained by the **FTC Act 1914**, the **Fair Packaging and Labeling Act 1966** and the **Nutritional Labeling and Educational Act 1990**.

**Support services** augment the actual product. *(Lexus: "the best dealership visit is the one you never have to make.")*

### 4. Product lines and the product mix

> **Product line** — products closely related in function, customer group, outlet type or price range.
> **Product mix (portfolio)** — all the lines and items a seller offers.

**Line length is a profit test, stated as a rule:** *"The line is too short if the manager can increase profits by adding items; the line is too long if the manager can increase profits by dropping items."*

- **Line filling** — more items *within* the present range. **Overdone when it causes cannibalization or customer confusion** *(see §7, where this is computed)*.
- **Line stretching** — *beyond* the current range: **downward** (Mercedes CLA for younger buyers), **upward** (P&G's "Platinum" versions), or **both** (BMW: three brands, 14 Series, from MINI to Rolls-Royce).

**The mix has four dimensions:** **width** (number of lines), **length** (total items), **depth** (versions per item), **consistency** (how related the lines are).

> [!note] ⚠️ Memorise these by what each counts
> **Width counts lines; length counts items; depth counts versions; consistency is the only one that is not a count.**

**P&G's megabrand strategy, costed.** *(His figures: 23 billion-dollar brands; **70–80 core brands yielding 90% of sales and more than 95% of profits**.)*

| | share of sales | share of profit | **profit per sales dollar** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **core** | 90% | 95% | **1.0556** |
| **non-core** | 10% | 5% | **0.5000** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The core brands are 2.11× as profitable per dollar of sales
> **Divesting the tail costs 10% of sales and only 5% of profit.** **⇒ "Less [can] be much more" is not a slogan — it is a ratio, and his own two percentages contain it.**
>
> **⚠️ It is [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s lesson in a fourth setting: never read a share of sales without the profitability that goes with it.**

### 5. ⚠️ Services — and what a 98% standard really is

**Four characteristics** *(Figure 8.3, recovered):*

| | |
|---|---|
| **intangibility** | cannot be seen, tasted, felt, heard or smelled **before purchase** |
| **inseparability** | **produced and consumed at the same time**; cannot be separated from the provider |
| **variability** | quality depends on **who** provides them and **when, where and how** |
| **perishability** | **cannot be stored** for later sale or use |

> [!note] Each characteristic has a matching marketing response
> **intangibility → make it tangible** *(Mayo Clinic's "organized and honest evidence")*; **inseparability → the customer coproduces**, so manage the interaction; **variability → standardise**; **perishability → match demand to supply** *(off-season pricing, part-time staff)*.

**The service profit chain** — five links: **internal service quality → satisfied and productive employees → greater service value → satisfied and loyal customers → healthy profits and growth.**

**Three types of services marketing** *(Figure 8.4, recovered — the company/employee/customer triangle)*: **external** (company→customers), **internal** (company→employees, and it *"must precede external marketing"*), **interactive** (employees↔customers).

**Now the arithmetic.** Kotler: *"A 98 percent performance standard may sound good, but using this standard, the U.S. Postal Service would lose or misdirect 356,000 pieces of mail each hour, and U.S. pharmacies would misfill more than 1.5 million prescriptions each week."*

**Back out what volumes those imply:**

| | his error figure | ÷ 2% ⇒ implied volume | **actual** | **check** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **USPS** | 356,000 / hour | **155.9 billion pieces/year** | 154.2 bn (FY2015) | **1.1% off ✓** |
| **US pharmacies** | 1.5m / week | **3.9 billion prescriptions/year** | ~4.0 bn | **2.5% off ✓** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Both of his figures are real, and that is the finding
> **A percentage standard is not a quality level — it is an error count that scales with volume.** **⇒ 2% is negligible for a corner shop and catastrophic for a postal service, and the number that decides which is the throughput, not the percentage.**
>
> *(This is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/07 - Financial Crises|Monetary Theories ch. 07]]'s rule in a service setting: **always ask what the neglected term is proportional to.**)*

**And it compounds.** Five pages later Kotler prints Emirates' service encounter in full — *"from flight booking to check-in, baggage drop, boarding, plane conditions, inflight services such as food and beverage, entertainment and shopping, to disembarkation and final baggage claim"* — **nine stages.**

| stages | end-to-end at 98% each | **≥1 failure** | 1 journey in |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 98.0000% | 2.00% | 50.0 |
| 3 | 94.1192% | 5.88% | 17.0 |
| 5 | 90.3921% | 9.61% | 10.4 |
| 7 | 86.8126% | 13.19% | 7.6 |
| **9** | **83.3748%** | **16.63%** | **6.0** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ 98% per stage is 83.37% per journey — one flight in six goes wrong somewhere
> **To reach 98% *end-to-end* over nine stages each stage must run at 99.7758% — an error rate of 0.2242%, ⚠️ 8.9× tighter than the 2% that "sounds good".**
>
> **⇒ THE PER-STAGE STANDARD MUST BE SET FROM THE NUMBER OF STAGES.** *(At six-sigma per stage the whole journey fails 3.06 times per 100,000 against 16,625 — a factor of 5,433.)*
>
> **⚠️ And this is why service *recovery* is a strategy and not an apology.** **If one journey in six contains a failure, recovery is a routine process with a designed capacity** — which is exactly what his Southwest figure describes: **29 people, ~80,000 posts a month ⇒ 2,759 per person per month, 138 per workday, 3.48 minutes each across an eight-hour day.** *He calls for "a quick and thoughtful response"; that is its budget.*

**Three service tasks:** **differentiation** (offer, delivery, image — the Aflac duck, the GEICO gecko), **quality** (*"customer retention is perhaps the best measure of quality"*), and **productivity** — with a warning: **"be careful not to take service out of the service."**

> [!note] ⚠️ His sharpest sentence, and it is an economics claim
> **"A company may purposely lower service productivity in order to improve service quality, in turn allowing it to maintain higher prices and profit margins."** **⇒ that is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s premium-for-differentiation trade, in the one place a productivity metric would rule it out.** **A firm optimising output per employee would never choose it.**

### 6. ⚠️ Four Seasons — where perishability pays for the perk

*(His figures: turnover **18%, "half the industry average"** ⇒ **36%**; every employee gets **six free room-nights a year**; guests pay **"\$1,000 or more a night"**.)*

| | Four Seasons | industry |
|---|---|---|
| annual turnover | **18%** | 36% |
| **expected tenure** | **5.56 years** | 2.78 years |

**18 replacements per 100 employees per year are avoided. What does the perk cost?**

| the six nights costed at… | perk per employee/yr | **break-even replacement cost** |
|---|---|---|
| **marginal cost of an empty room** *(my \$75)* | \$450 | **\$2,500** |
| **rack rate** *(his "\$1,000 or more")* | \$6,000 | **\$33,333** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The same policy is obviously profitable or obviously ruinous — 13.3× apart — depending only on how the room is costed
> **And only marginal cost is right, because an unsold room-night is worth exactly zero at midnight.**
>
> **⇒ THAT IS SERVICE PERISHABILITY — §5's fourth characteristic — and it is what makes the perk cheap.** **Kotler defines perishability five pages earlier and never connects it to the example.**
>
> *(The general rule: **cost a giveaway at what the alternative use would have earned.** For a perishable service that is often nothing, which is why hotels, airlines and gyms can give away capacity that looks expensive at list price. Costing it at rack rate would reject a policy that halves turnover.)*

### 7. Branding

> **Brand equity** — the **differential effect** that knowing the brand name has on customer response to the product and its marketing. *(Positive if consumers react more favourably than to an unbranded version — **negative equity is possible**.)*
> **Brand value** — the **total financial value** of a brand.
> **Customer equity** — the value of the customer relationships the brand creates. ***"The fundamental asset underlying brand equity."***

**⚠️ Kotler defines brand equity as a differential and then never measures one — but his Joshua Bell story is a measurement.**

*(Boston Symphony Hall, a packed house at an average **\$100 a seat**. Three days later, in jeans and a baseball cap in a Washington DC metro station with the same **\$4 million Stradivarius** and the same repertoire: **45 minutes, ~1,100 people passed, \$32 earned.**)*

| | willingness to pay per listener |
|---|---|
| **"branded" Bell** | **\$100.00** |
| **"unbranded" Bell** | **\$0.029091** |
| **ratio** | **⚠️ 3,437.5×** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The physical product was identical, unimprovable, and three days apart
> **Same performer, same instrument, same music. The entire difference is the brand — and that difference is precisely what "brand equity" was defined to mean.** **His hourly take was \$42.67.**

**Brand value, made interpretable.** *(His figures: Apple **\$246bn**, Google \$174bn, Microsoft \$115bn, IBM \$94bn, AT&T \$92bn, Verizon \$86bn — **\$807bn for six brands**.)* **A perpetual stream is worth $V = X/(k-g)$, so $X = V(k-g)$ is the annual profit the name earns:**

| brand | value (\$bn) | $k-g=5\%$ | $k-g=8\%$ | $k-g=12\%$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Apple** | 246 | 12.30 | **19.68** | 29.52 |
| Google | 174 | 8.70 | 13.92 | 20.88 |
| Verizon | 86 | 4.30 | 6.88 | 10.32 |

> [!warning] ⚠️ "Apple's brand is worth \$246 billion" IS the claim that Apple earns \$19.7 billion a year purely because the logo says Apple
> **Stated that way it is checkable; stated as a stock it is not.**
>
> **⚠️ And the same brand value implies annual figures 2.40× apart depending only on the discount rate.** **⇒ that is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/05 - The Stock Market, Rational Expectations and Efficient Markets|Monetary Theories ch. 05]]'s Gordon sensitivity again** *(three investors agreeing on every cash flow and valuing the stock 71.4% apart)*. **Kotler concedes "measuring such value is difficult" and then quotes six figures to the nearest billion.**

**Young & Rubicam's BrandAsset Valuator scores four dimensions:** **differentiation** (what makes it stand out), **relevance** (does it meet my needs), **knowledge** (do I understand it), **esteem** (do I respect it).

> [!note] ⚠️ Read them as a funnel, not a scorecard — Kotler's own text does
> **"The brand must be distinct, or consumers will have no reason to choose it… However, the fact that a brand is highly differentiated doesn't necessarily mean that consumers will buy it… Even a differentiated, relevant brand is far from a shoe-in."** **⇒ each is necessary and none is sufficient, so a brand fails at the first dimension it misses.** **Averaging the four would hide exactly that.**

**Four brand strategy decisions** *(Figure 8.5, recovered)*:

| decision | options |
|---|---|
| **brand positioning** | **attributes → benefits → beliefs and values** |
| **brand name selection** | **selection**, **protection** |
| **brand sponsorship** | manufacturer's brand · private (store) brand · licensing · co-branding |
| **brand development** | line extensions · brand extensions · multibrands · new brands |

**Positioning is a ladder, weakest first.** **Attributes are "the least desirable level" — "competitors can easily copy attributes"** and customers want what the attribute *does*. Benefits are better *(FedEx: guaranteed on-time delivery)*. **Beliefs and values are strongest** *(Whirlpool's "Every Day, Care")* — Saatchi & Saatchi's **lovemarks**, brands that *"inspire loyalty beyond reason."*

> [!note] ⚠️ Whirlpool's two results imply a third he never states
> **Sales rose 6.6% while share rose 10% ⇒ the market itself contracted 3.09%.** *(Reading "10 percent" as a relative rise — the only computable reading.)* **⇒ Whirlpool grew sales in a shrinking market, which is a considerably stronger claim than the one he makes.**

**Name selection** wants six qualities: suggest a benefit; easy to pronounce and remember; distinctive; **extendable** *(Amazon began as a bookseller and chose a name that allowed expansion)*; translatable; registrable.

> [!warning] ⚠️ Success can destroy a trademark
> **Cellophane, aspirin, nylon, kerosene, linoleum, yo-yo, trampoline, escalator, thermos and shredded wheat were all protected brand names and are now generic**, which is why Kleenex advertises to *advertisers* asking for "Kleenex® Brand Tissue". **⇒ becoming the category name is the goal and the danger at once.**

**Sponsorship — store brands.** *(His figures: private brands yield **"a 25 percent savings"**; Kroger's own brands are **25% of sales, "nearly \$25 billion"**; ALDI **>90%**, Trader Joe's **~90%**, Kohl's private label **>half of revenue**; P&G's premium detergents hold **60% of a \$7 billion market = \$4.2bn**.)*

**⚠️ The store brand is 25% cheaper AND earns the retailer a higher margin. Where does the money come from?**

*(Retail prices and the direction of the margin are Kotler's; the margin levels are mine.)*

| | retail | retailer margin | retailer \$/unit | wholesale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **national brand** | 100.00 | 20% | 20.00 | **80.00** |
| **store brand** | 75.00 | 30% | **22.50** | **52.50** |

**Consumer saves \$25.00 (25%). Retailer gains \$2.50 (+12.5%). ⇒ the manufacturer's side gives up \$27.50 — 34.4% of the national brand's wholesale price.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ The 25% saving measures branding, not quality
> **Of that \$27.50, advertising at P&G's own stated "about 10 percent of sales" accounts for \$8.00 (29%). The remaining \$19.50 (71%) is brand rent plus whatever real quality difference exists.**
>
> **⇒ that is why a retailer can cut the price a quarter and still earn more: it is not making a better product more cheaply, it is not paying for the brand.** **And it is why Kotler's own advice to national brands is right — they must "sharpen their value propositions", because the store brand has removed the part of the price that positioning was buying.**

**Licensing.** *(Retail sales of licensed products: **\$4bn in 1977 → \$55bn in 1987 → more than \$259bn today**.)* Kotler: *"Name and character licensing has grown rapidly in recent years."*

| period | | **CAGR** |
|---|---|---|
| **1977 → 1987** | \$4bn → \$55bn | **29.97%/yr** |
| **1987 → 2017** | \$55bn → \$259bn | **5.30%/yr** |
| 1977 → 2017 | \$4bn → \$259bn | 10.99%/yr |

> [!warning] ⚠️ His own numbers say licensing decelerated for thirty years — by a factor of 5.7
> **In real terms** *(deflating by US CPI — my addition)* **the collapse is starker still: 22.05%/yr → 2.71%/yr.** **⇒ licensing is a mature category growing barely faster than the economy, not a fast-growing one.**
>
> **⚠️ This is [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s discipline exactly: a large absolute number (\$259bn) is being read as evidence of rapid growth, and a growth *rate* says the opposite.** **Compute the rate whenever a source offers a level.**

**Co-branding** *(Doritos Locos Taco: 100 million in ten weeks, over a billion since; Nike+iPod gave Apple a foothold in sport and Nike new value)* — **limits: complex contracts, coordinated marketing, and each partner's reputation is now hostage to the other's.**

**⚠️ Brand development** *(Figure 8.6, recovered — a 2×2 of **product category** × **brand name**)*:

| | **existing product category** | **new product category** |
|---|---|---|
| **existing brand name** | **line extension** | **brand extension** |
| **new brand name** | **multibrands** | **new brands** |

**And here Kotler asks the right question and does not answer it:** of a new Doritos flavour, *"how much would adding yet another flavor steal from Doritos' own sales versus those of competitors? A line extension works best when it takes sales away from competing brands, not when it 'cannibalizes' the company's other items."*

*(His figures: **more than 20 US flavours** and **more than \$1.3 billion** in US sales ⇒ **≈\$65m per flavour**.)*

**If a new flavour's buyers are drawn from category buyers in proportion to existing shares, the fraction taken from the brand *itself* is its own share $s$:**

| own share $s$ | cannibalised | **incremental sales** | contribution at 40% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | \$6.5m | **\$58.5m** | \$23.4m |
| 40% | \$26.0m | \$39.0m | \$15.6m |
| **50%** | **\$32.5m** | **\$32.5m** | \$13.0m |
| 70% | \$45.5m | \$19.5m | \$7.8m |
| **80%** | **\$52.0m** | **\$13.0m** | **\$5.2m** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ THE CANNIBALISATION RATE IS THE BRAND'S OWN MARKET SHARE
> **⇒ the market leader gains least from a line extension** — the very firm with the strongest name to extend. **At 80% share, four fifths of the "new" \$65m is money the brand already had.**
>
> **⚠️ And it is self-limiting: every successful extension raises $s$, which shrinks the next one's incremental sales.** **That is the mechanism behind all three of his cautionary examples at once** — McDonald's menu *(cut back because complexity slowed the drive-thru, which is **70% of sales**)*, Doritos' 20 flavours where *"the original… now seem like just another flavor"*, and P&G's divestitures.

**Brand extension** carries a different risk: *"a good brand extension should fit the parent brand, and the parent brand should give the extension competitive advantage in its new category."* **Duracell Powermat and Vicks ZzzQuil fit; Cheetos lip balm, Heinz pet food, Hooters Air and an Evian water-filled padded bra did not.** **Multibrands** capture shelf space and segments (PepsiCo's eight soft drinks) at the cost of spreading resources thin (GM cut Saturn, Oldsmobile, Pontiac, Hummer, Saab; Ford went from 97 nameplates to under 20).

**Managing brands.** *(Advertising: P&G **\$11.5bn**, Unilever \$7.9bn, GM \$3.4bn, Coca-Cola \$3bn+.)* **But: "brands are not maintained by advertising but by customers' engagement with brands and customers' brand experiences."** *"A brand is a living entity… the product of a thousand small gestures."* **Audit periodically.**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Levels and classes.)** (a) Give the three product levels with an example. (b) Give the four consumer classes. (c) Why does the distribution row of Table 8.1 follow from the buying-behavior row?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Core customer value → actual product → augmented product.**
>
> For an iPad: the **core** is *"entertainment, self-expression, productivity, and connectivity — a mobile and personal window to the world"*; the **actual product** is its name, features, design, quality level and packaging; the **augmented product** adds the warranty, repair service, app ecosystem and iCloud.
>
> **⚠️ The three levels are a hierarchy of imitability** — a competitor can copy the features next quarter and cannot easily displace what the buyer is really buying. That is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s *preemptive* criterion in product form.
>
> **(b)** **Convenience, shopping, specialty, unsought** — classified by **how consumers buy**, not by what the thing is.
>
> **(c) Because the distribution decision is forced by whether the buyer will search.**
>
> **Convenience buyers won't search** — little planning, minimal comparison — **so if you are not in front of them you do not get the sale.** ⇒ *widespread distribution, convenient locations, low price.*
>
> **Specialty buyers will travel** — "buyers are usually willing to travel great distances" for a Lamborghini — **so you can restrict outlets without losing sales**, and exclusivity itself supports the positioning. ⇒ *exclusive distribution, high price.*
>
> **⚠️ The promotion row follows the same way**: mass promotion where there is no comparison, personal selling where there is much comparison, aggressive selling where there is negative interest.
>
> **⇒ so Table 8.1 is a derivation, not four independent lists** — which also makes it memorable, because you only need the first row.
>
> *(And note the specialty column's "**low price sensitivity**": that is a low elasticity, hence pricing power. It is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s result appearing here under a different name, in a chapter that never mentions demand curves.)*

**2. (Hard — packaging.)** Kotler says a supermarket stocks ~42,000 items, a supercenter ~120,000, shoppers pass ~300 items a minute and make three of four decisions in store. What follows?

> [!example]- Solution
> **Divide the numbers he prints one page apart.**
>
> **(i) Nobody can walk the store.** 42,000 ÷ 300 = **140 minutes** for a supermarket; 120,000 ÷ 300 = **400 minutes = 6h40m** for a supercenter.
>
> **(ii) A real trip sees a small fraction:**
>
> | trip | items passed | supermarket | supercenter |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 20 min | 6,000 | 14.3% | 5.0% |
> | **40 min** | 12,000 | **28.6%** | **10.0%** |
>
> **(iii) ⚠️ Each package gets 0.20 seconds** *(60 ÷ 300)*.
>
> **⇒ so the three-of-four-decisions figure and the packaging investment both follow from arithmetic, not from assertion.** **If most of the decision happens in a store the shopper can only partly traverse, in a fifth of a second per item, then the package is not decoration — it is the last and often only piece of marketing the buyer sees.** Kotler's phrase "prime marketing space" understates it.
>
> **⚠️ And it settles his logo section.** A mark that must register in 0.2 seconds on a shelf faces the same constraint as one on a **2.5-inch smartwatch** — which is precisely why Google moved to a sans-serif that "shrinks down more legibly" and built a one-letter **G**. **The digital pressure and the shelf pressure are the same pressure.**
>
> *(A caution: the 300 items/minute is an average over a walking shopper, so it overstates attention to items on the route and understates attention to items being deliberately compared — a shopper choosing between two detergents spends far more than 0.2 seconds on those two. The figure bounds *unsought* exposure, which is exactly the case packaging must win.)*

**3. (Hard — the 98% standard.)** (a) Check Kotler's USPS and pharmacy figures. (b) What is 98% worth across Emirates' nine-stage encounter? (c) What per-stage standard delivers 98% end-to-end?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Back out the volume each implies, then compare with reality.**
>
> | | his figure | ÷ 2% ⇒ implied | **actual** | check |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | USPS | 356,000/hour | **155.9 bn/year** | 154.2 bn | **1.1% off ✓** |
> | pharmacies | 1.5m/week | **3.9 bn/year** | ~4.0 bn | **2.5% off ✓** |
>
> **Both figures are real.** ⇒ **⚠️ a percentage standard is not a quality level, it is an error count that scales with throughput.** 2% is nothing for a corner shop and 356,000 letters an hour for a postal service, and **the quantity that decides which is the volume, not the percentage.**
>
> **(b) 83.37% — one journey in six fails somewhere.**
>
> Nine stages *(booking, check-in, baggage drop, boarding, plane conditions, in-flight service, entertainment/shopping, disembarkation, baggage claim — his own list)*, so $0.98^9 = 0.833748$.
>
> | stages | end-to-end | ≥1 failure |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | 98.00% | 1 in 50 |
> | 5 | 90.39% | 1 in 10.4 |
> | **9** | **83.37%** | **1 in 6.0** |
>
> **(c) 99.7758% per stage** — since $0.98^{1/9}=0.997758$ — **an error rate of 0.2242%, ⚠️ 8.9× tighter than the 2% that "sounds good."**
>
> **⇒ THE PER-STAGE STANDARD MUST BE DERIVED FROM THE NUMBER OF STAGES.** Setting each stage at "98%, which sounds good" and then being surprised that customers complain is the commonest version of this mistake.
>
> **⚠️ And this is why service recovery is a designed process with a capacity, not an apology.** If a sixth of all journeys contain a failure, recovery is routine work — and Kotler's own Southwest figure sizes it: **29 people, 80,000 posts a month = 2,759 each per month = 138 per workday = 3.48 minutes per post** across an eight-hour day. That is the budget for "a quick and thoughtful response."
>
> *(Two honest caveats: the nine stages are not independent — a bad airline fails at several at once, which makes things worse, not better, since failures cluster onto the same unlucky passengers; and "98% per stage" is Kotler's illustrative standard, not Emirates' actual performance. The structure of the result is what matters.)*

**4. (Hard — Four Seasons and brand equity.)** (a) Cost the Four Seasons room-night perk. (b) What does the Joshua Bell experiment measure? (c) What does "Apple's brand is worth \$246 billion" actually claim?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) It breaks even at a \$2,500 replacement cost — if the room is costed correctly.**
>
> Turnover is 18% against an industry 36%, so **18 replacements per 100 employees per year are avoided**, and expected tenure **doubles from 2.78 to 5.56 years**. Six free nights per employee per year:
>
> | costed at | annual perk | break-even replacement cost |
> |---|---|---|
> | **marginal cost** *(my \$75)* | \$450 | **\$2,500** |
> | **rack rate** *(his "\$1,000+")* | \$6,000 | **\$33,333** |
>
> **⚠️ The same policy is 13.3× apart depending only on the costing convention** — obviously profitable one way, obviously ruinous the other.
>
> **And only marginal cost is right, because an unsold room-night is worth exactly zero at midnight.** ⇒ **that is service PERISHABILITY, §5's fourth characteristic**, and it is what makes the perk cheap. **Kotler defines perishability five pages earlier and never links it to the example.**
>
> *(General rule: cost a giveaway at what the alternative use would have earned. For a perishable service that is often nothing — which is why hotels, airlines and gyms give away capacity that looks expensive at list price.)*
>
> **(b) It measures brand equity, exactly as Kotler defines it — and he does not do the division.**
>
> Same performer, same repertoire, same **\$4 million Stradivarius**, three days apart:
>
> | | willingness to pay per listener |
> |---|---|
> | branded | **\$100.00** |
> | unbranded | **\$0.029091** *(\$32 ÷ 1,100)* |
> | **ratio** | **⚠️ 3,437.5×** |
>
> **Brand equity is defined as "the differential effect that knowing the brand name has on customer response."** **The physical product here was identical and unimprovable, so the differential is the whole of it.**
>
> **(c) It claims Apple earns \$19.7 billion a year purely because the logo says Apple.**
>
> A perpetual stream is worth $V=X/(k-g)$, so $X=V(k-g)=246\times0.08=\$19.68\text{bn}$.
>
> **⚠️ Stated as a flow the claim is checkable; stated as a stock it is not — and the flow moves 2.40× (from \$12.3bn at $k-g=5\%$ to \$29.5bn at 12%) on the same brand value.**
>
> ⇒ **that is [[Monetary and Financial Theories/contents/05 - The Stock Market, Rational Expectations and Efficient Markets|Monetary Theories ch. 05]]'s Gordon sensitivity again**, and it is why brand-value league tables from different valuers disagree by multiples on the same brand in the same year. **Kotler writes "measuring such value is difficult" and then quotes six figures to the nearest billion.**

**5. (Hard — extensions and licensing.)** (a) Answer Kotler's own Doritos question. (b) Who gains least from a line extension? (c) Check his claim that licensing "has grown rapidly in recent years."

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) ⚠️ The cannibalisation rate is the brand's own market share.**
>
> If a new flavour's buyers are drawn from category buyers in proportion to existing shares, a fraction $s$ of them are the brand's own customers switching. With **≈\$65m of sales per flavour** (his \$1.3bn ÷ 20 flavours), incremental sales are $65(1-s)$:
>
> | $s$ | cannibalised | **incremental** |
> |---|---|---|
> | 10% | \$6.5m | \$58.5m |
> | 50% | \$32.5m | \$32.5m |
> | **80%** | \$52.0m | **\$13.0m** |
>
> **(b) The market leader** — the very firm with the strongest name to extend.
>
> **⚠️ And the strategy is self-limiting: every successful extension raises $s$, which shrinks the next one's incremental sales.** So a brand's own success progressively destroys the case for extending it.
>
> **⇒ this single mechanism explains all three of Kotler's cautionary examples**: McDonald's cutting a menu that had slowed the drive-thru (**70% of sales**); Doritos, where "the original… now seem like just another flavor"; and P&G's divestitures, whose profitability arithmetic §4 computed (**core brands 2.11× as profitable per sales dollar**).
>
> *(Two refinements worth knowing. The share-based rate is an upper bound on cannibalisation only if the extension is undifferentiated; a genuinely new benefit draws disproportionately from outside the brand. And cannibalisation is not automatically bad — cannibalising yourself before a competitor does it is often the right call, which is the whole logic of [[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]].)*
>
> **(c) His three numbers say the opposite.**
>
> | period | | **CAGR** |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1977 → 1987 | \$4bn → \$55bn | **29.97%/yr** |
> | 1987 → 2017 | \$55bn → \$259bn | **5.30%/yr** |
>
> **⚠️ The growth rate fell by a factor of 5.7, and it has been falling for thirty years.** Deflating by US CPI *(my addition)*: **22.05%/yr real → 2.71%/yr real.**
>
> **⇒ licensing is a mature category growing barely faster than the economy.** The \$259bn is a large *level*, and a level is not a growth rate.
>
> **⚠️ This is [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s discipline exactly** — where Under Armour's 12%-of-sales international arm growing at 70% contributed **+8.4 points** against a 30% base contributing ~4.5. **Always compute the rate when a source hands you levels, and never read a large number as evidence of fast growth.**

## 📝 Summary

- **Product = anything offered for attention, acquisition, use or consumption; service = intangible, no ownership.**
- **Three levels: core customer value → actual product → augmented product** — **⚠️ a hierarchy of imitability.**
- **Consumer classes: convenience, shopping, specialty, unsought** — classified by **how people buy**.
- **⚠️ Table 8.1 is a derivation: distribution and promotion follow from buying behavior.** **"Low price sensitivity" in the specialty column is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s pricing power unnamed.**
- **Quality has two dimensions: level (choose it to match positioning) and consistency (always maximise).**
- **⚠️ AT 300 ITEMS PER MINUTE EACH PACKAGE GETS 0.20 SECONDS** — and a 40-minute trip passes **28.6%** of a supermarket, **10.0%** of a supercenter. **A supercenter would take 6h40m to walk past.**
- **Mix dimensions: width (lines), length (items), depth (versions), consistency.**
- **⚠️ P&G's core brands are 2.11× as profitable per sales dollar** — the tail is 10% of sales and 5% of profit.
- **Four service characteristics: intangibility, inseparability, variability, perishability**, each with a matching response.
- **⚠️ A 98% STANDARD IS AN ERROR COUNT, NOT A QUALITY LEVEL** — his figures imply **155.9bn USPS pieces/year (actual 154.2bn)** and **3.9bn prescriptions/year (actual ~4.0bn)**. **Both are real.**
- **⚠️ 98% PER STAGE IS 83.37% ACROSS EMIRATES' OWN NINE STAGES — one journey in six fails.** **98% end-to-end needs 99.7758% per stage, 8.9× tighter.**
- **⇒ set the per-stage standard from the number of stages, and treat recovery as a capacity** *(Southwest: 3.48 minutes per post)*.
- **⚠️ PERISHABILITY IS WHY THE FOUR SEASONS PERK IS CHEAP** — break-even \$2,500 at marginal cost, \$33,333 at rack rate, **13.3× apart**; turnover 18% vs 36% **doubles tenure to 5.56 years**.
- **Brand equity = the differential effect of the name; brand value = its financial total; customer equity underlies both.**
- **⚠️ JOSHUA BELL MEASURES THE DIFFERENTIAL AT 3,437×** — same performer, same Stradivarius, three days apart.
- **⚠️ "Apple's brand is worth \$246bn" = "Apple earns \$19.7bn/yr from the logo"** at $k-g=8\%$ — **and 2.40× either side of that on the same value.**
- **Positioning ladder: attributes (copyable, weakest) → benefits → beliefs and values (lovemarks).**
- **⚠️ Success can destroy a trademark** — cellophane, aspirin, escalator, thermos were all brands.
- **⚠️ The 25% store-brand saving measures branding, not quality**: the retailer earns **more** per unit while the consumer pays **25% less**, because **34.4% of the national brand's wholesale price** stops being paid.
- **⚠️ LICENSING'S GROWTH FELL FROM 29.97%/YR TO 5.30%/YR** (22.05% → 2.71% real) while Kotler calls it "grown rapidly."
- **⚠️ THE CANNIBALISATION RATE OF A LINE EXTENSION IS THE BRAND'S OWN MARKET SHARE** ⇒ **the leader gains least, and the strategy is self-limiting.**
- **Brand development 2×2: line extension / brand extension / multibrands / new brands.**

## ⚠️ Important Notes

1. **⚠️ The three product levels differ in how easily they are copied.** Attributes are cheapest to imitate.
2. **Classify a consumer product by how it is bought, never by what it is.** The same lawn mower is consumer or industrial by *purpose*.
3. **⚠️ Quality level is a choice; quality consistency is not.** "A Chevrolet can have just as much quality as a Rolls-Royce."
4. **⚠️ A package gets 0.20 seconds and no shopper sees the whole store.**
5. **Logo redesign is driven by the smallest screen it must survive**, not by fashion — and the shelf imposes the same constraint.
6. **⚠️ Line length is a profit test**, not an aesthetic one: too short if adding raises profit, too long if dropping does.
7. **Width/length/depth are counts; consistency is not.**
8. **⚠️ Never read a share of sales without its profitability** — P&G's tail was 10% of sales and 5% of profit.
9. **⚠️ A PERCENTAGE QUALITY STANDARD IS AN ERROR COUNT.** Multiply it by throughput before calling it good.
10. **⚠️ Reliability compounds down a service chain.** $0.98^9=83.37\%$.
11. **⚠️ Derive the per-stage standard from the number of stages** — $p=T^{1/n}$.
12. **Failures cluster**: stages are correlated, so the same customers absorb several. That makes recovery capacity more urgent, not less.
13. **⚠️ Cost a giveaway at the alternative use's earnings, not at list price.** For a perishable service that is often zero.
14. **Perishability also cuts the other way**: an unsold seat is a permanent loss, which is why off-peak pricing exists.
15. **⚠️ "Productivity" can destroy a service.** Kotler's own case for *deliberately* lowering it is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s premium trade.
16. **Internal marketing must precede external marketing.** You cannot promise what your staff will not deliver.
17. **⚠️ Brand equity can be negative.** The name can make people react *less* favourably.
18. **⚠️ Convert a brand value into an annual figure before believing it** — and check how much the discount rate moves it.
19. **The BrandAsset Valuator's four dimensions are a funnel, not a scorecard.** Averaging them hides the one that fails.
20. **⚠️ Attribute positioning is the weakest** because attributes are copyable — [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s *preemptive* test again.
21. **⚠️ Becoming the category name is both the goal and the trademark risk.**
22. **⚠️ The store-brand discount is the branding cost, not a quality gap.**
23. **⚠️ A line extension's cannibalisation rate ≈ the brand's own share**, so success shrinks the case for the next one.
24. **Cannibalising yourself first is often correct** — better you than a competitor.
25. **A brand extension must fit the parent AND the parent must confer advantage in the new category.** Both, or it fails.
26. **⚠️ Multibranding buys shelf space and segments; it spreads resources thin.** Ford went 97 nameplates → under 20.
27. **⚠️ Compute a growth rate whenever you are handed levels.** \$259bn hides a thirty-year deceleration.
28. **Advertising does not maintain a brand** — experience and engagement do. "A thousand small gestures."
