# Marketing Channels, Retailing and Wholesaling

**Three results, and the first is the one worth carrying.**

**§1 — ⚠️ KOTLER'S OWN CONTACTS FIGURE GENERALISES INTO THE THEORY OF DISINTERMEDIATION, AND HE PICKS THE WEAKEST POSSIBLE CASE.** *(Direct contacts $=MC$; through one intermediary $=M+C$; **the saving is exactly $(M-1)(C-1)-1$**.)* **His 3×3 illustration saves 33.3% and is the smallest case in which an intermediary saves anything at all — at 2×2 it saves NOTHING. At 100 manufacturers and 1,000 customers it saves 98.90%, a factor of 90.9.** **⇒ AN INTERMEDIARY IS WORTH MORE THE MORE FRAGMENTED BOTH SIDES ARE, AND WORTH NOTHING WHEN EITHER SIDE IS CONCENTRATED** — which predicts exactly who disintermediates and who does not.

**§6 — ⚠️ LOGISTICS IS A PRICING LEVER, AND [[10 - Pricing|CH. 10]] PRICES IT.** His *"as much as 20 percent of an average product's price is accounted for by shipping and transport alone"* ⇒ **a 10% logistics saving is 2% of price, which at a 3% net margin is +66.7% of profit.** **That is why "even small savings can be substantial", and he never does the division.**

**§8 — ⚠️ COSTCO EARNS 1.80× SAM'S CLUB'S OPERATING PROFIT ON A MARGIN 0.4 POINTS LOWER.** *(\$3.60bn against \$1.99bn, at 3.1% against 3.5%.)* **⇒ profit = margin × volume, for the fourth time in this subject.** **And by ch. 10's law a 1% price rise would raise Costco's operating profit by 32.3% — which is exactly what a \$1.50 hot dog held for three decades costs, and why the thin margin is the strategy rather than an accident.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. ⚠️ Why intermediaries exist — and how much they are worth

> **Value delivery network** — the company, suppliers, distributors and customers who *partner* to improve the performance of the entire system.
> **Marketing channel (distribution channel)** — the set of interdependent organizations that help make a product or service available for use or consumption.

**Kotler's Figure 12.1: three manufacturers reaching three customers directly need **nine contacts**; through one distributor, **six**.**

**⚠️ Generalise it. With $M$ producers and $C$ customers:**

$$\text{direct} = M\times C \qquad \text{one intermediary} = M + C \qquad \boxed{\text{saving} = (M-1)(C-1)-1}$$

| $M$ | $C$ | direct | with one | **saving** | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2** | **2** | 4 | 4 | **0** | **1.0×** |
| **3** | **3** | 9 | 6 | **3 (33.3%)** | 1.5× |
| 10 | 10 | 100 | 20 | 80 (80.0%) | 5.0× |
| **100** | **1,000** | 100,000 | 1,100 | **98,900 (98.90%)** | **90.9×** |
| 1,000 | 100,000 | 100,000,000 | 101,000 | 99.90% | 990.1× |

> [!warning] ⚠️ At 2×2 an intermediary saves nothing, and his 3×3 case is the smallest one that saves anything
> **He illustrates a result that scales to 99% with the example that yields 33%.**
>
> **⇒ AND THIS IS THE THEORY OF DISINTERMEDIATION, stated in one line: the intermediary's value grows with the product of the two fragmentations and collapses when either side concentrates.**
>
> **That single fact predicts his own examples.** **Mary Kay, Amway, GEICO and Omaha Steaks sell direct** because $M=1$ — the manufacturer *is* the whole supply side, so $MC = C = $ the direct-contact count and there is nothing to save. **Groceries have deep channels** because thousands of producers meet millions of households. **⇒ and disintermediation happens not when technology arrives but when technology makes the $M+C$ contacts cheap enough that the intermediary's cut exceeds the saving it provides.**
>
> *(Kotler defines **disintermediation** — "channel members are cut out by product or service producers, or displaced by radically new types of channel members" — and treats it as a trend. It is a computation.)*

**Kotler's other economic argument is *assortment transformation*:** **producers make narrow assortments in large quantities; consumers want broad assortments in small quantities.** *(Unilever makes millions of bars of soap a week; you want two.)*

**Channel functions — five that *complete* transactions and three that *fulfil* them:**

| complete | fulfil |
|---|---|
| **information**, **promotion**, **contact**, **matching**, **negotiation** | **physical distribution**, **financing**, **risk taking** |

> [!note] ⚠️ The sentence that makes this a decision rather than a description
> **"The question is not whether these functions need to be performed — they must be — but rather WHO will perform them."** **⇒ shifting a function to an intermediary lowers the producer's cost and raises the intermediary's charge; the functions should go to whoever "can add the most value for the cost."** **That is a make-or-buy problem, not a marketing preference.**

### 2. Channel levels

**A channel level is each layer of intermediaries that brings the product closer to the final buyer. Producer and consumer are part of every channel, so the *number of intermediary levels* is the channel's length.**

| **consumer channels** | | **business channels** |
|---|---|---|
| **1** — producer → consumer *(direct)* | | producer → business customer |
| **2** — producer → retailer → consumer | | producer → distributor → customer |
| **3** — producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer | | producer → rep/sales branch → distributor → customer |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Longer channels are harder to control and harder to observe
> **Kotler's point is that longer channels mean less producer control and worse information from the market.** **⇒ and that is [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]'s problem: a three-level channel means the producer never meets the customer, so demand data arrives filtered through two sets of ordering decisions.** *(That filtering is what makes [[06 - Business Markets and Business Buyer Behavior|ch. 06]]'s accelerator effect so violent.)*

### 3. Channel behaviour and organisation

**Channel conflict:** **horizontal** (among firms at the *same* level — two franchisees fighting over territory) and **vertical** (between *different* levels — a producer and its retailers).

> [!note] ⚠️ Some conflict is healthy; the failure mode is a specific one
> **"Without it, the channel could become passive and non-innovative."** **The danger is conflict that becomes *severe and chronic*.**

**Conventional distribution channels** consist of independent producers, wholesalers and retailers, each maximising its own profit **"even at the expense of the system as a whole."** **⇒ nobody has control over the others and there is no formal means of assigning roles.** **A vertical marketing system (VMS)** fixes this:

| **VMS type** | how leadership is established |
|---|---|
| **corporate** | **single ownership** of successive stages *(Kroger operates 37 manufacturing plants, giving it factory-to-store control over 40% of its 13,000 private-label items)* |
| **contractual** | **contracts** — chiefly the **franchise organization**, in three forms: manufacturer-sponsored retailer, manufacturer-sponsored wholesaler, and service-firm-sponsored retailer |
| **administered** | the **size and power** of one member, without ownership or contracts |

**A horizontal marketing system** joins two firms **at the same level** to pursue an opportunity together. **A multichannel (hybrid) distribution system** uses two or more channels to reach one or more segments.

> [!warning] ⚠️ Multichannel systems buy reach and buy conflict at the same time
> **Adding a channel adds sales and adds a rival for the existing channel's customers.** **⇒ that is why channel conflict is not a failure of a multichannel design — it is a cost of it, and the design question is whether the incremental sales exceed the incremental conflict.** *[[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|Ch. 09]]'s cannibalisation arithmetic, in a channel.*

### 4. Channel design decisions

**Four steps: analyse consumer needs → set channel objectives → identify major alternatives → evaluate.**

**Alternatives have three dimensions: *types* of intermediary, *number* of intermediaries, and the *responsibilities* of each.**

**⚠️ The number decision is the one that gets examined:**

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **intensive distribution** | stock in **as many outlets as possible** | convenience products |
| **selective distribution** | **more than one but fewer than all** willing intermediaries | shopping products *(STIHL sells chain saws only through servicing dealers, not mass merchants)* |
| **exclusive distribution** | **a limited number of dealers, exclusive rights** in their territories | specialty products *(Bentley: a handful of dealers per market)* |

> [!note] ⚠️ This is [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s Table 8.1 seen from the channel's side
> **Convenience → intensive; shopping → selective; specialty → exclusive.** **Both tables are consequences of the same fact — how far the buyer will travel — and Kotler presents them four chapters apart without connecting them.**
>
> **And the trade is explicit: exclusive distribution "enhances the brand's image and allows for higher markups", at the cost of coverage.** **⇒ it is [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]]'s premium bought with volume, decided in the channel.**

**Evaluating alternatives uses economic, control and adaptive criteria. Evaluating *members* uses sales quotas, inventory levels, delivery time, treatment of damaged goods, cooperation and customer service.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Channel decisions are the least reversible in the marketing mix, and Kotler says so
> **Prices, promotions and even products can be changed quickly.** **A channel commits the firm to other firms — through contracts, dealer investments and customer habit — for years.** **⇒ so the [[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]] discipline applies with extra force: compute before committing, because you cannot cheaply undo it.**

### 5. Public policy and international channels

**Exclusive dealing, exclusive territorial agreements and tying agreements are legal only where they do not substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly, and where both parties enter voluntarily.**

**International channels must be *designed to the country*, not exported.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ The China figure is a direct measure of channel inefficiency
> **China's logistics costs are 16% of GDP against the US's 8.3% — 1.93× the burden — because "China's distribution system is so fragmented."** **Even Walmart's executives "admit they have been unable to assemble an efficient supply chain in China."**
>
> **⇒ and §1 explains why fragmentation is expensive: the contacts cost scales as $M\times C$ until somebody consolidates it.** **A country's logistics share of GDP is a national-scale reading of the same quantity.**

### 6. ⚠️ Marketing logistics — and what a saving is worth

> **Marketing logistics (physical distribution)** — planning, implementing and controlling the physical flow of goods, services and related information from points of origin to points of consumption to meet customer requirements **at a profit**.
> **Supply chain management** — managing upstream and downstream value-added flows among suppliers, the company, resellers and final consumers.

**Modern logistics runs in both directions: *outbound*, *inbound*, and *reverse* logistics (reusing, recycling, refurbishing or disposing of returns).**

> [!note] ⚠️ Kotler's own framing correction is worth keeping
> **"It's also called the customer value delivery network."** **The logistics goal is NOT minimum cost** — *"no logistics system can both maximize customer service and minimize distribution costs"* — **it is the targeted level of service at the least cost.**

**⚠️ Now the arithmetic he leaves on the table.** *(His figures: **"as much as 20 percent of an average product's price is accounted for by shipping and transport alone"**; US companies spend **\$1.45 trillion a year — about 8.3% of GDP**; the supply chain "accounts for up to 75 percent of a product's cost"; **GM's annual logistics bill is about \$8 billion**.)*

**Combining that 20% with [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]]'s law that profit change $= d/m$:**

| logistics saving | **% of price** | at 3% net margin | at 5% | at 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | 1.00% | **+33.3%** | +20.0% | +10.0% |
| **10%** | **2.00%** | **+66.7%** | +40.0% | +20.0% |
| 25% | 5.00% | **+166.7%** | +100.0% | +50.0% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 10% logistics saving is worth 66.7% of profit at a 3% net margin
> **That is the whole content of "even small savings can be substantial" — and it is why logistics is a strategic function rather than a back office.**
>
> **⇒ his 3PL figures close the loop: 86% of Fortune 500 companies outsource logistics, and it "results in a 10 to 25 percent cost savings." On GM's \$8bn bill that is \$800 million to \$2 billion.**
>
> *(Internal check: \$1.45tn at 8.3% of GDP implies a GDP of **\$17.47 trillion** — the US level in 2014. ✓ Consistent.)*

**Four functions: warehousing** *(storage warehouses vs **distribution centres**, which move goods rather than store them)*, **inventory management** *(**just-in-time**; RFID)*, **transportation**, and **logistics information management**.

**Modal shares of US cargo ton-miles — an internal check:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **trucks** | **40%** |
| railroads | 26% |
| pipelines | 17% |
| water carriers | 7% |
| **multimodal** | **8%** |
| air carriers | **less than 1%** |

> [!note] The stated shares sum to just under 100% ✓
> **And the ordering is instructive: air moves under 1% of ton-miles while being indispensable for the goods it does carry** — *perishables and high-value, low-bulk items.* **⇒ ton-miles measures bulk, not value, so a modal share table understates air and overstates pipelines for any question about revenue.**

**Integrated logistics management** requires **cross-functional teamwork inside** and **channel partnerships outside** *(shared projects, cross-company teams, shared information systems, **vendor-managed inventory**)*, plus **third-party logistics (3PL)**.

**Sustainable supply chains** are driven by regulation, large customers, and consumers — *(50% of millennials will pay more for sustainable products; 39% research a company's practices)* — **but Kotler's honest argument is the commercial one: "the very logistics activities that create the biggest environmental footprint" are also the biggest costs.** *(One firm cut carbon 6% while production rose 20% — **carbon per unit of output fell 21.7%**; another saved \$8 million a year in shipping.)*

### 7. Retailing

> **Retailing** — all activities in selling goods or services directly to final consumers for their personal, non-business use. **Retailers accounted for more than \$5.3 trillion of sales to final consumers last year.**

**Retailers classify by:**

| **amount of service** | self-service · limited-service · full-service |
|---|---|
| **product line** | specialty store · department store · **supermarket** · convenience store · **superstore** *(supercenters, category killers)* · service retailer |
| **relative prices** | discount store · **off-price retailer** *(independent, **factory outlet**, **warehouse club**)* |
| **organizational approach** | corporate chain · voluntary chain · retailer cooperative · **franchise** |

**The format numbers, and what they say:**

| | |
|---|---|
| supermarkets' share of US packaged-food sales | **53% (1998) → 37% (2012)** |
| ⇒ | **−16 points, a 30.2% relative decline, −2.53%/yr compound over 14 years** |
| traditional grocery store | **\$517,000 a week** |
| supercenter | **\$1.4 million a week — 2.71×** |
| franchises' share of all US retail sales | **~45%** |
| McDonald's | **36,000 stores, >\$98bn system-wide = \$2.72m per store**, >80% franchised |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 2.5%-a-year share loss looks survivable and is not
> **Fourteen years of it took supermarkets from a majority of the category to just over a third.** **⇒ slow structural decline is the hardest kind to react to, because no single year justifies acting** — which is [[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]]'s decline stage as a management problem rather than a curve.

**Retailer marketing decisions mirror the whole book:** **segmentation, targeting, differentiation and positioning**, then the **retail marketing mix — product assortment and services, price, promotion, and place.**

> [!note] ⚠️ Two retail-specific ideas worth naming
> **The *wheel of retailing*: new formats enter at low price and low margin, gradually add services and costs, become high-price operators, and are displaced by the next low-price entrant.** **⇒ every format's success carries the seed of its replacement, which is [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s "more for less is not an equilibrium" as a life cycle.**
>
> **And *retail convergence* — different types of retailers selling the same goods at the same prices to the same consumers — means "the competition is no longer between formats."**

**Major trends:** tighter consumer spending; new retail forms and shortening retail life cycles; **the rise of megaretailers**; growth of **direct, online, mobile and social media retailing**; **omni-channel retailing**; growing importance of retail technology; and **global expansion of major retailers**.

### 8. ⚠️ Costco — the lower-margin firm earns more

*(His figures: Costco **\$116 billion**, the world's second-largest retailer; Sam's Club **652 stores, \$57 billion**; Costco has "only about 60 more stores"; Costco's operating margin **3.1%**, Sam's Club **3.5%**; both stock **about 4,000 items** against a supermarket's 40,000 and a Walmart supercenter's 150,000; over the past four years Costco's revenues **+30%** and profits **+50%**.)*

| | revenue | stores | revenue/store | operating margin | **operating profit** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Costco** | \$116.0bn | ~712 | **\$162.9m** | **3.10%** | **\$3.60bn** |
| Sam's Club | \$57.0bn | 652 | \$87.4m | 3.50% | \$2.00bn |

> [!warning] ⚠️ COSTCO EARNS 1.80× SAM'S CLUB'S OPERATING PROFIT ON A MARGIN 0.4 POINTS LOWER
> **2.04× the revenue on 1.09× the stores — 1.86× per store.**
>
> **⇒ PROFIT = MARGIN × VOLUME, for the fourth time in this subject** *(after [[02 - Company and Marketing Strategy|ch. 02]]'s contribution to growth $=$ share × rate, [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s premium arithmetic, and [[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]]'s Pop-Tarts)*. **Reading either factor alone gets the ranking wrong, and here it gets it exactly backwards.**

**And the margin itself moved.** Revenue $\times1.30$ with profit $\times1.50$ means the margin rose by $1.50/1.30 = 1.1538$:

> [!note] ⚠️ Costco's operating margin rose from 2.69% to 3.10% over four years — while it held the \$1.50 hot dog
> **⇒ so the famous price discipline is not a margin sacrifice made in ignorance. The margin improved anyway, because volume grew faster than cost.**

**⚠️ Finally, apply [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]]'s lever to a 3.1% margin:**

| price rise, no volume loss | operating profit |
|---|---|
| **+0.5%** | **+16.1%** |
| **+1.0%** | **+32.3%** |
| +2.0% | +64.5% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ A 1% price rise would raise Costco's operating profit by nearly a third
> **THAT is what a \$1.50 hot dog held for three decades costs, priced properly.** **And it is why the thin margin is a strategy and not an accident: at 3.1%, price restraint is the single most expensive thing the company does, and it does it deliberately to buy the membership renewal that is the actual product.**
>
> *(The rest of the case is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]: with **4,000 items against a supercenter's 150,000 — a factor of 37.5** — and **25% of them designated "treasure items"**, Costco is not competing on price at all. **It is differentiated by scarcity and surprise, which is why it beats an identically-priced rival.**)*

### 9. Wholesaling

> **Wholesaling** — all activities in selling goods and services to those buying for resale or business use.

**Wholesalers add value through:** selling and promoting, buying and assortment building, **bulk breaking**, warehousing, transportation, financing, **risk bearing**, market information, and management services.

| **type** | |
|---|---|
| **merchant wholesalers** | **independently owned, TAKE TITLE**; full-service *(wholesale merchants, industrial distributors)* or limited-service *(cash-and-carry, truck, drop shippers, rack jobbers, producers' cooperatives, mail-order)* |
| **brokers and agents** | **do NOT take title**; specialise by line or customer type; paid a commission |
| **manufacturers' sales branches and offices** | **owned by the seller**, not independent |

> [!warning] ⚠️ "Takes title" is the whole classification, and it is an exam favourite
> **A merchant wholesaler buys the goods and bears the inventory risk; a broker or agent never owns them and bears none.** **⇒ that single fact determines who carries working capital, who bears obsolescence, and how each is paid — margin versus commission.**

**Wholesaler marketing decisions are the same four:** segmentation and targeting, then **the wholesale mix — product assortment and services, price, promotion, place.** **Kotler's note is that wholesalers have historically been weakest at promotion and at using their location decisions strategically.**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Hard — the contacts result.)** (a) State Kotler's Figure 12.1. (b) Generalise it. (c) What does the generalisation predict?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** Three manufacturers reaching three customers directly need **9 contacts**; through one distributor, **6**.
>
> **(b)** $\text{direct} = MC$, $\text{intermediated} = M+C$, so
> $$\text{saving} = MC - (M+C) = (M-1)(C-1) - 1$$
>
> | $M\times C$ | direct | intermediated | **saving** | ratio |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **2 × 2** | 4 | 4 | **0** | **1.0×** |
> | 3 × 3 | 9 | 6 | 3 (33.3%) | 1.5× |
> | **100 × 1,000** | 100,000 | 1,100 | **98.90%** | **90.9×** |
>
> **⚠️ At 2×2 the intermediary saves nothing, and Kotler's 3×3 is the smallest case that saves anything at all.** **He illustrates a result that scales to 99% with the example that yields 33%.**
>
> **(c) It predicts who disintermediates.**
>
> **The intermediary's value grows with the *product* of the two fragmentations and collapses when either side concentrates.** **⇒ therefore:**
>
> - **Direct selling appears where $M=1$** — Mary Kay, Amway, GEICO, Omaha Steaks. With one producer, $MC = C = $ the direct contact count and there is literally nothing to save.
> - **Deep channels persist where both sides are fragmented** — groceries, with thousands of producers and millions of households.
> - **⚠️ Disintermediation happens not when technology arrives but when technology makes the $M+C$ contacts cheap enough that the intermediary's cut exceeds the saving it provides.** *An intermediary taking 30% of a channel that saves 33% is already marginal; one taking 30% of a channel that saves 99% is not.*
>
> *(Honest caveat: the count treats all contacts as equally costly, which they are not — a manufacturer-to-distributor relationship is far more expensive than a single retail transaction. The result is about the **scaling**, and the scaling is what matters, because $MC$ grows quadratically and $M+C$ linearly however you weight them.)*

**2. (Channel structure.)** (a) What are channel levels? (b) Distinguish conventional channels from a VMS and name the three types. (c) Why is channel conflict not always bad?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** Each layer of intermediaries doing work to bring the product closer to the buyer. **Producer and consumer are in every channel, so channel *length* is the number of *intermediary* levels.** Channel 1 (direct) has none; Channel 3 has wholesaler and retailer.
>
> **⚠️ Longer channels mean less control and worse information** — a three-level producer never meets the customer, so demand data arrives filtered through two sets of ordering decisions. *(That filtering is what makes [[06 - Business Markets and Business Buyer Behavior|ch. 06]]'s accelerator so violent.)*
>
> **(b)** A **conventional channel** is independent firms each maximising its own profit **"even at the expense of the system as a whole"**, with no member controlling the others. **A VMS** unifies them:
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | **corporate** | single **ownership** *(Kroger's 37 plants ⇒ control over 40% of its 13,000 private-label items)* |
> | **contractual** | **contracts**, chiefly franchising |
> | **administered** | the **size and power** of one member |
>
> **(c) Because the alternative is a passive channel.**
>
> **Kotler: "without it, the channel could become passive and non-innovative."** **⇒ conflict is information — a retailer fighting over margin is telling you something about the value split — and the failure mode is specifically conflict that is *severe and chronic*, not conflict as such.**
>
> **⇒ and in a multichannel system conflict is a *cost of the design*, not a defect in it.** Adding a channel adds sales and adds a competitor for the existing channel's customers, so the question is whether incremental sales exceed the conflict — **[[09 - New Products and the Product Life Cycle|ch. 09]]'s cannibalisation arithmetic applied to a channel.**

**3. (Hard — distribution intensity.)** (a) Give the three levels. (b) Match them to [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s consumer product classes. (c) What is being traded?

> [!example]- Solution

>
> **(b)**
>
> | product class | intensity | why |
> |---|---|---|
> | **convenience** | **intensive** | buyers won't search, so absence = lost sale |
> | **shopping** | **selective** | buyers compare, so you need presence plus support |

>

>
> **(c) Coverage against margin and image.**
>
> **Exclusive distribution "enhances the brand's image and allows for higher markups"** *(STIHL refusing mass merchants; Bentley's handful of dealers)*, and it does so by giving up reach. **⇒ that is [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]]'s premium bought with volume, decided in the channel rather than on the price list**, and [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]]'s break-even test applies: **the narrower distribution is worth it if the margin gain exceeds the volume lost.**
>
> **⚠️ And the decision is nearly irreversible.** Prices and promotions change in a week; a channel commits the firm to other firms through contracts, dealer investments and customer habit for years. **Compute before committing.**

> | Sam's Club | \$57.0bn | 652 | \$87.4m | 3.50% | \$2.00bn |
>
> **2.04× the revenue on 1.09× the stores = 1.86× per store.**
>
> **⚠️ PROFIT = MARGIN × VOLUME, for the fourth time in this subject** — and here reading margin alone gets the ranking exactly backwards.
>
> **(b) It rose, from 2.69% to 3.10%.**
>
> Revenue ×1.30 with profit ×1.50 ⇒ margin ×$1.50/1.30 = 1.1538$, so the earlier margin was $3.10\%/1.1538 = \mathbf{2.69\%}$.
>
> **⇒ so the price discipline was not a margin sacrifice made blindly. The margin improved anyway, because volume grew faster than cost** — which is the experience curve of [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]] §2 showing up in retail.
>
> **(c) About a third of operating profit per point of price.**
>
> | price rise, no volume loss | operating profit |
> |---|---|
> | +0.5% | **+16.1%** |
> | **+1.0%** | **+32.3%** |
>
> **⚠️ A 1% price rise would raise Costco's operating profit by nearly a third.** **That is what holding a \$1.50 hot dog for three decades costs, priced properly** — and Costco sells **110 million of them a year.**
>
> **⇒ so the thin margin is the strategy, not an accident.** **At 3.1%, price restraint is the most expensive thing the company does, and it does it deliberately** — because the product being sold is the **membership renewal**, and the hot dog is its advertising.
>
> **⚠️ And the rest of the case is [[07 - Segmentation, Targeting and Positioning|ch. 07]], not price at all.** **Costco and Sam's Club stock the same ~4,000 items at the same kind of price** — the difference is that **25% of Costco's are "treasure items"** that come and go. **Against a supercenter's 150,000 SKUs that is a factor of 37.5 fewer choices**, sold as scarcity and surprise. **⇒ two retailers with near-identical prices and cost structures, and the differentiated one wins — which is precisely ch. 07's finding that a sustainable advantage must be something a rival cannot simply match on price.**

## 📝 Summary

- **A marketing channel is a set of interdependent organizations making the product available; a value delivery network includes suppliers and customers.**
- **⚠️ INTERMEDIARIES REDUCE CONTACTS FROM $MC$ TO $M+C$; THE SAVING IS $(M-1)(C-1)-1$.**
- **⚠️ At 2×2 the saving is ZERO; his 3×3 saves 33.3%; at 100×1,000 it saves 98.90%, a factor of 90.9.**
- **⇒ an intermediary is worth more the more fragmented both sides are, and nothing when either concentrates — WHICH IS THE THEORY OF DISINTERMEDIATION.**
- **Eight channel functions; ⚠️ the question is never whether but WHO performs them.**
- **Channel level = each intermediary layer.** Longer channels mean less control and filtered demand information.
- **Conflict is horizontal or vertical; ⚠️ some is healthy, chronic severity is not.**
- **Conventional channels optimise locally "even at the expense of the system"; a VMS is corporate, contractual (franchising) or administered.**
- **⚠️ Multichannel systems buy reach and conflict together** — the incremental-sales test again.
- **Intensive / selective / exclusive ⇒ convenience / shopping / specialty.** ⚠️ **[[08 - Products, Services and Brands|Ch. 08]]'s table from the channel side; both follow from how far the buyer travels.**
- **⚠️ Channel decisions are the least reversible element of the mix.**
- **⚠️ The logistics goal is TARGETED SERVICE at least cost, never minimum cost.**
- **⚠️ TRANSPORT IS ~20% OF PRICE ⇒ A 10% LOGISTICS SAVING IS +66.7% OF PROFIT at a 3% net margin.**
- **US logistics \$1.45tn = 8.3% of GDP (implies \$17.47tn ✓); China 16% = 1.93× the burden.**
- **3PL: 86% of Fortune 500, 10–25% savings — \$800m–\$2bn on GM's \$8bn bill.**
- **Modal shares: trucks 40, rail 26, pipeline 17, multimodal 8, water 7, air <1** — sum just under 100 ✓. **Ton-miles measure bulk, not value.**
- **Retailing: >\$5.3 trillion. Classify by service, product line, price, organization.**
- **⚠️ Supermarkets went 53% → 37% of packaged food in 14 years = −2.53%/yr** — slow structural decline is the hardest to react to.
- **Supercenter \$1.4m/week vs grocery \$517k = 2.71×. Franchises ≈ 45% of retail. McDonald's \$2.72m/store.**
- **The wheel of retailing: every low-price entrant becomes a high-price incumbent and is displaced.**
- **⚠️ COSTCO EARNS 1.80× SAM'S CLUB'S OPERATING PROFIT ON A 0.4-POINT LOWER MARGIN** — profit = margin × volume, a fourth time.
- **⚠️ Costco's margin ROSE from 2.69% to 3.10% (revenue +30%, profit +50%).**
- **⚠️ A 1% price rise would lift Costco's operating profit 32.3% — the price of the \$1.50 hot dog, and why thin margin is a strategy.**
- **⚠️ 4,000 SKUs vs a supercenter's 150,000 — a factor of 37.5 — with 25% "treasure items".** Differentiation, not price.
- **Wholesalers: ⚠️ merchant wholesalers TAKE TITLE; brokers and agents do NOT; sales branches are owned by the seller.**

## ⚠️ Important Notes

1. **⚠️ Generalise a source's toy example before believing its magnitude.** 33% vs 98.90%.
2. **⚠️ An intermediary saves nothing at 2×2** — the value is in the product of the fragmentations.
3. **⇒ disintermediation is arithmetic, not a trend.**
4. **Channel functions can be shifted, never eliminated.** Cost follows them.
5. **Longer channels filter demand information** — the accelerator's cause.
6. **⚠️ A conventional channel optimises locally and loses globally.** That is what a VMS fixes.
7. **Franchising is a *contractual* VMS**, not a separate category.
8. **⚠️ Channel conflict is a cost of a multichannel design, not a fault in it.**
9. **⚠️ Distribution intensity follows the product class, which follows buyer search behaviour.**
10. **Exclusive distribution buys margin and image with coverage** — compute the trade.
11. **⚠️ Channel decisions bind for years.** Least reversible element of the mix.
12. **⚠️ Never state the logistics goal as "minimum cost".**
13. **⚠️ A cost that is 20% of price is a profit lever of 6.7× at a 3% margin.**
14. **A country's logistics share of GDP measures channel fragmentation.**
15. **⚠️ Ton-mile shares measure bulk, not value** — they understate air freight badly.
16. **Reverse logistics is part of the system**, not an afterthought.
17. **Sustainability and cost point the same way in logistics** — that is why it happens.
18. **⚠️ −2.5%/yr looks survivable and compounds to −30% in 14 years.**
19. **The wheel of retailing means today's format is tomorrow's incumbent.**
20. **Retail convergence: "the competition is no longer between formats."**
21. **⚠️ The lower-margin firm can earn more.** Costco: 1.80× on 0.4 points less.
22. **⚠️ Compute what price restraint costs before calling it a sacrifice or a virtue.**
23. **⚠️ Fewer SKUs is a positioning decision** — 4,000 vs 150,000.
24. **"Takes title" is the whole wholesaler classification** — and it determines who bears inventory risk.
