# Company and Marketing Strategy

**[[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]] defined marketing. This chapter puts it inside the firm** — where the marketing plan is one function among several, and the strategic decisions above it are about *which businesses to be in at all*.

**Four results.**

**§2 — ⚠️ ch. 01's finding holds a second time: the BCG matrix is a *cash-flow routing diagram* dressed as a taxonomy.** *(Computed from Kotler's own illustrative firm: **50% of units consume cash, 20% generate it, 30% are dogs** — which is exactly why he calls it "fair, not good", though he states the counts and the verdict without connecting them.)*

**§2 also — his own problem 2 is the vault's measurement theme.** **Both axes are *definitional choices*: "market share" depends on how you draw the market, and drawing it narrowly turns a dog into a star with no change in the business.**

**§3 — ⚠️ what Kotler's Under Armour percentages actually imply.** *(Computed: **contribution to growth = share × growth rate**, so international at **12% of sales growing 70% contributed 8.4 points**, footwear **5.7**, and direct-to-consumer — *the largest base* — only ~4.5.)* **⇒ a low share is an argument for a strategy only if the growth rate overcomes the small base.**

**§5 — the 4Ps are an inside-out list**, and Kotler says so himself. **[[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]]'s selling-versus-marketing distinction in a second costume.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. The four steps of strategic planning

| level | step |
|---|---|
| **company** | **1. define the mission** — *"what business are we in?"* |
| | **2. set company objectives and goals** |
| | **3. design the business portfolio** |
| **business unit** | **4. plan marketing and other functional strategies** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The mission statement is ch. 01's myopia test applied to the firm itself
> **Kotler's rule: a mission should be *market-oriented*, not product-oriented, and should not be stated in terms of products or technology — because those change.**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | product-oriented | *"we make cosmetics"* |
> | **market-oriented** | ***"we sell hope and self-expression"*** |
>
> **⚠️ And the failure mode is symmetric.** **Too narrow is myopia; too broad is meaningless** *("we make people happy")*. **A mission must be narrow enough to exclude something** — a statement that rules nothing out cannot guide a portfolio decision.

### 2. ⚠️ The BCG growth-share matrix — and what it allocates

**Two axes: *market growth rate* (how attractive the market is) × *relative market share* (how strong the firm is in it).**

| cell | growth | share | cash | destiny |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **stars** | **high** | **high** | **heavy investment** to finance rapid growth | become cash cows |
| **cash cows** | low | **high** | little investment — **they produce cash** | **fund everything else** |
| **question marks** | **high** | low | lots of cash **just to hold** share | build into stars *or* phase out |
| **dogs** | low | low | self-sustaining at best | divest |

**Four strategies, one per SBU: *build* share · *hold* share · *harvest* (milk short-term cash regardless of long-term effect) · *divest*.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ Apply ch. 01's test — what does the framework ALLOCATE?
> **The matrix is a cash-flow routing diagram.** **Cash cows generate, stars and question marks consume, dogs are cut loose.** **Kotler says it outright: "income from these cash cows will help finance the company's question marks, stars, and dogs."**
>
> **⚠️ So it is the same shape as [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s four relationship groups: a 2×2 that looks like a taxonomy and is really a budget rule.** **There the units were customers and the resource was relationship investment; here the units are businesses and the resource is cash.** **Second instance — look for it in every 2×2 in this book.**

> [!note] And the lifecycle claim is the matrix's real content
> **Question mark → star → cash cow → dog.** **⚠️ Position is not a *property*, it is a *stage*. A cash cow is a former star.** **Which is why the firm "needs to add new products and units continuously"** — the matrix is a snapshot of a process, and a portfolio with no question marks has no future stars.

*(Computed from Kotler's illustrative firm — 2 stars, 2 cash cows, 3 question marks, 3 dogs:)*

| | |
|---|---|
| **cash-consuming** (stars + question marks) | **50%** |
| **cash-generating** (cash cows) | **20%** |
| dogs — divestment candidates | 30% |

> [!warning] ⚠️ That ratio is the diagnosis
> **Two cows funding five consumers is thin — which is exactly why Kotler calls the firm "in fair shape, although not in good shape".** **He states the counts and he states the verdict, and never connects them.**

**Kotler's four problems with matrix approaches:**

1. **difficult, time-consuming and costly to implement;**
2. **⚠️ hard to *define* SBUs and *measure* share and growth;**
3. **⚠️ focuses on *current* businesses — "provide little advice for future planning";**
4. centralised, when planning is now decentralised to cross-functional teams.

> [!warning] ⚠️ Problem 2 is the vault's measurement theme again
> **Both axes are definitional choices.** **"Market share" depends on how you draw the market — and drawing it narrowly turns a dog into a star with no change whatever in the business.** *([[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]]'s "which metric?" becomes **"which market?"** here.)*

> [!note] Problem 3 is the deeper one, and it explains §3
> **A matrix built from *current* position cannot recommend what to do *next*.** **⇒ the two tools are complements, not alternatives: BCG looks at what you *have*, the expansion grid at where you could *go*.**

### 3. ⚠️ The product/market expansion grid

| strategy | products | markets | risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **market penetration** | existing | existing | **lowest** |
| **product development** | **new** | existing | medium |
| **market development** | existing | **new** | medium |
| **diversification** | **new** | **new** | **highest** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The risk ordering is the point, and Kotler does not state it
> **Each move away from "existing" adds one unknown.** **Penetration risks nothing new; either development risks *one* thing; diversification risks *both at once*.**
>
> **Which is why his closing warning attaches to diversification alone: "when diversifying, companies must be careful not to overextend their brands' positioning."**

**Under Armour, using Kotler's own figures:**

| segment | share of total | recent growth | strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| direct-to-consumer sales | **30%** | tripled over eight years | **penetration** |
| **international sales** | **12%** | **grew 70% last year** | **market development** |
| footwear | 13% | rose 44% last year | **product development** |

*(Plus: advertising and endorsement spending **+35%** — penetration; and the MapMyFitness / MyFitnessPal / Endomondo acquisitions — **diversification**.)*

> [!warning] ⚠️ What the shares imply — the computation Kotler leaves out
> $$\textbf{contribution to total growth}=(\text{share of total})\times(\text{growth rate of the part})$$
>
> | segment | share | growth | **contribution** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **international** | 12% | **70%** | **+8.4 pts** |
> | footwear | 13% | 44% | +5.7 pts |
> | direct-to-consumer | **30%** | ~15% p.a. | **+4.5 pts** |
>
> **⇒ international, at 12% of sales, contributed nearly twice what direct-to-consumer did at 30%.**
>
> **⚠️ The general rule is worth more than the case: a small fast-growing segment and a large slow-growing one can contribute equally.** **Kotler's phrase "leaving plenty of room for growth" is doing real work — but a low share is an argument for a strategy *only if* the growth rate is high enough to overcome the small base.**
>
> **⚠️ And note this is the *same arithmetic* as the BCG matrix's two axes — share and growth — applied to segments instead of SBUs.** **Kotler presents the two tools four pages apart and never says they use the same two variables at different levels of aggregation.**

### 4. The value chain and the value delivery network

**Value chain** *(Porter)*: **the series of internal departments that carry out value-creating activities** — design, produce, market, deliver, support.

> [!note] Marketing cannot do it alone
> **"Each department constitutes a link."** **Success depends on how well each department performs *and* on how well the activities are coordinated.** **⇒ the marketing department's job includes internal persuasion** — which is why "partnering to build customer engagement" is in the chapter's title.

**Value delivery network**: **the chain extended *outside* the firm — suppliers, distributors, and ultimately customers.**

> [!warning] ⚠️ And the consequence is the one worth keeping
> **Competition is no longer between firms but between *networks*.** **Whoever assembles the better network wins** — which is why [[11 - Marketing Channels, Retailing and Wholesaling|ch. 11]]'s channel decisions are strategic rather than logistical.

### 5. ⚠️ The 4Ps — and why they are the seller's view

| P | what it is | decisions |
|---|---|---|
| **product** | the goods-and-services combination offered | variety, quality, design, features, brand, packaging, services |
| **price** | the amount customers must pay | list price, discounts, allowances, payment period, credit terms |
| **place** | making the product available to target consumers | channels, coverage, locations, inventory, transport, logistics |
| **promotion** | communicating merits and persuading to buy | advertising, personal selling, sales promotion, public relations |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The criticism Kotler raises himself: the 4Ps take the seller's view
> | seller's 4P | **buyer's 4C** |
> |---|---|
> | product | **customer solution** — not "what we make" |
> | price | **customer cost** — the total cost of owning and using, not the tag |
> | place | **convenience** — buying convenience, not our distribution |
> | promotion | **communication** — two-way, not broadcast |
>
> **⚠️ This is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s selling-versus-marketing distinction in a second costume.** **There: inside-out versus outside-in. Here: 4Ps versus 4Cs.** **Kotler's own remark is that marketers should "first think through the four Cs and then build the four Ps on that platform."**
>
> **And note the asymmetry: *price* is the only P that produces revenue. The other three produce costs.** **That is why [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]] is a whole chapter.**

### 6. Managing the effort — analysis, planning, implementation, control

**SWOT is the analysis tool:**

| | helpful | harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **internal** | **strengths** | **weaknesses** |
| **external** | **opportunities** | **threats** |

> [!note] The commonest exam error is putting an external factor in the wrong half
> **The test: *can the firm change it by itself?*** **If yes, it is internal.**

**Marketing control asks two different questions, and Kotler splits them:**

| | question |
|---|---|
| **operating control** | **"are we achieving the plan?"** — annual, corrective |
| **strategic control** | **"are our strategy and opportunities still well matched?"** — the **marketing audit** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ The second is the one firms skip — and it is ch. 01's myopia again
> **Operating control asks whether you are doing the plan *well*; strategic control asks whether it is still the *right* plan.** **A firm can pass the first every year while the second quietly fails.**

**Marketing ROI** = **the net return from a marketing investment divided by its costs.** **Kotler concedes it is hard to measure and points instead at customer-centred measures — acquisition, retention, and ⚠️ customer lifetime value and customer equity ([[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]])** — **which is where the chapter hands back to the previous one.**

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (Mission and planning.)** (a) Give the four steps. (b) What makes a good mission statement? (c) Classify three missions.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Company level: define the mission → set objectives and goals → design the business portfolio.** **Business-unit level: plan marketing and other functional strategies.**
>
> **⚠️ Note the direction: marketing planning happens *last* and *inside* a portfolio decision already made.** **A marketing plan for a business the company should not be in cannot succeed** — which is why this chapter precedes the rest of the book.
>
> **(b) Market-oriented, and narrow enough to exclude something.**
>
> **Kotler's rule: state the mission in terms of *customer needs satisfied*, not products or technology, because products and technology change.** *("We make cosmetics" → "we sell hope and self-expression.")*
>
> **⚠️ But the failure is symmetric, and this is the part students miss.** **Too narrow is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s marketing myopia — defining yourself by the drill bit rather than the hole.** **Too broad is meaningless: "we make people happy" rules nothing out, and a mission that rules nothing out cannot guide the portfolio decision that follows it in step 3.**
>
> **The working test: does the mission *exclude* a business the firm could plausibly enter?** If not, it is decoration.
>
> **(c)**
> - **"We are in the railroad business"** — **product-oriented and the classic myopia case.** **Restated as "we are in the transportation business"** it survives the arrival of trucking and air freight.
> - **"We help people be their best selves"** *(a gym chain)* — **market-oriented but too broad.** It licenses entry into cosmetics, education and therapy equally, so it constrains nothing.
> - **"We provide fast, reliable, affordable transportation for short urban journeys"** — **market-oriented and bounded.** **It excludes long-haul freight and luxury travel**, which is what makes it usable.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Growth × share, with four cells and four moves.**
>
> | | high share | low share |
> |---|---|---|
> | **high growth** | **stars** — invest heavily | **question marks** — build or phase out |
> | **low growth** | **cash cows** — milk them | **dogs** — divest |
>
> **Strategies: build, hold, harvest, divest.**
>
> **⚠️ And the lifecycle claim is the matrix's real content: question mark → star → cash cow → dog.** **Position is a *stage*, not a property — a cash cow is a former star.** **Which is why a portfolio with no question marks has no future stars**, and why Kotler says the firm must "add new products and units continuously".
>
> **(b) Cash.**
>
> **⚠️ It is a cash-flow routing diagram: cash cows generate, stars and question marks consume, dogs are cut loose.** **Kotler states it plainly — "income from these cash cows will help finance the company's question marks, stars, and dogs."**
>
> **This is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s finding a second time.** **There, the four relationship groups looked like a customer taxonomy and were really a rule for allocating relationship investment.** **Here a business taxonomy is really a rule for allocating capital.** **⇒ read every 2×2 in this book by asking what resource it distributes.**
>
> **(c) Two cows funding five consumers — thin, and that is the diagnosis.**
>
> *(Computed from his 2 stars / 2 cash cows / 3 question marks / 3 dogs: **50% of units consume cash, 20% generate it, 30% are dogs.**)*
>
> **Kotler calls the firm "in fair shape, although not in good shape" and does not say why.** **The ratio is why.** **A single cash cow supports two and a half cash-consuming units, and the three dogs contribute nothing while occupying management attention.**
>
> **⚠️ And his own four objections deserve weight, especially the two substantive ones.**
> - **Problem 2 — defining SBUs and measuring share and growth — is the vault's measurement theme.** **Both axes are definitional choices: "market share" depends on how the market is drawn, so narrowing the definition turns a dog into a star with no change in the business.** **[[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]]'s *which metric?* becomes *which market?*.**
> - **Problem 3 — it classifies *current* businesses and gives "little advice for future planning".** **That is a statement about what the tool is for**, and it is exactly the gap exercise 3's expansion grid fills.

**3. (Hard — the expansion grid.)** (a) Give the four strategies and rank them by risk. (b) Classify Under Armour's moves. (c) What do the percentages imply?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Products × markets, and risk increases with distance from "existing".**
>
> | | existing products | new products |
> |---|---|---|
> | **existing markets** | **market penetration** *(lowest risk)* | **product development** |
> | **new markets** | **market development** | **diversification** *(highest)* |
>
> **⚠️ Kotler does not state the risk ordering, and it follows immediately: each move away from "existing" adds one unknown.** **Penetration risks nothing new; either development risks *one* thing; diversification risks *both at once*.** **Which is why his only warning — "be careful not to overextend their brands' positioning" — is attached to diversification alone.**
>
> **(b)**
> - **Ever-increasing styles and colours in existing lines, +35% advertising spend, own retail stores** — **market penetration**. *(More sales of current products in current markets, via marketing-mix improvements.)*
> - **Marketing to women; entering Japan, Europe, Canada, Latin America; a first store in China** — **market development**. *(Current products, new demographic and geographic segments.)*
> - **Athletic shoes from 2006; the SpeedForm line** — **product development**. *(New products, current market.)*
> - **Acquiring MapMyFitness, MyFitnessPal and Endomondo; the IBM partnership** — **diversification**. *(New products *and* new markets — fitness tracking is not apparel.)*
>
> *(Note the last one is riskiest and Kotler flags it in place: it binds "consumers to the brand via technology and services rather than just apparel", which is a change in what the brand *is*.)*
>
> **(c) That the small segments are carrying the growth.**
>
> $$\text{contribution to total growth}=(\text{share})\times(\text{growth rate})$$
>
> | segment | share | growth | **contribution** |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **international** | 12% | 70% | **+8.4 pts** |
> | footwear | 13% | 44% | +5.7 pts |
> | direct-to-consumer | **30%** | ~15% | **+4.5 pts** |
>
> **⚠️ International, at 12% of sales, contributed nearly twice what direct-to-consumer did at 30%.**
>
> **The general rule is worth more than the case: a small fast-growing segment and a large slow-growing one can contribute equally.** **So Kotler's repeated phrase "leaving plenty of room for growth" is doing real work — but incompletely.** **A low share is an argument for a strategy *only if* the growth rate is high enough to overcome the small base**, and a segment at 2% growing at 10% contributes 0.2 points however much "room" it has.
>
> **⚠️ And notice what this reveals about the two tools.** **The expansion grid's arithmetic is *share × growth* — the same two variables as the BCG matrix's axes, applied to segments rather than SBUs.** **Kotler presents them four pages apart and never says so.**

**4. (The marketing mix.)** (a) Give the 4Ps. (b) What is wrong with them, on Kotler's own account? (c) What follows?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Product, price, place, promotion** — *the goods-and-services combination; the amount paid; availability to target consumers; communicating merits and persuading.*
>
> **(b) They take the seller's view.**
>
> | 4P | **4C** |
> |---|---|
> | product | **customer solution** |
> | price | **customer cost** |
> | place | **convenience** |
> | promotion | **communication** |
>
> **Each C reframes the P from the buyer's side.** **"Customer cost" in particular is broader than price: it is the total cost of *obtaining, using and disposing of* the product**, so a cheap printer with expensive cartridges scores badly on the C while scoring well on the P.
>
> **⚠️ This is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s selling-versus-marketing distinction in a second costume — inside-out versus outside-in.** **The 4Ps are an inside-out list**, and Kotler's own prescription is to "first think through the four Cs and then build the four Ps on that platform."
>
> **(c) Two things.**
>
> **First, the order of work.** **A firm that starts from its 4Ps is designing around what it already makes and sells** — the selling concept with better vocabulary. **Starting from the 4Cs makes the 4Ps outputs rather than inputs.**
>
> **⚠️ Second, and less obvious: *price is the only P that produces revenue*.** **Product, place and promotion all produce costs.** **That asymmetry means pricing errors are not symmetric with the others** — a mistake in promotion wastes a budget, a mistake in pricing forfeits margin on every unit sold. **It is why [[10 - Pricing|ch. 10]] is a whole chapter and why Appendix 2's arithmetic sits there.**

**5. (Control.)** (a) Give the SWOT quadrants and the test. (b) Distinguish operating from strategic control. (c) Why does the chapter end on customer equity?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Internal/external × helpful/harmful.**
>
> | | helpful | harmful |
> |---|---|---|
> | **internal** | strengths | weaknesses |
> | **external** | opportunities | threats |
>
> **⚠️ The commonest error is putting an external factor in the wrong half, and the test settles it: *can the firm change it by itself?*** **If yes, internal.**
>
> *(So "a strong brand" is a strength; "rising disposable income" is an opportunity, not a strength, however much the firm benefits. The distinction matters because the two halves prompt different actions — strengths are to be *exploited*, opportunities to be *pursued*.)*
>
> **(b) Doing the plan well versus having the right plan.**
>
> | | asks |
> |---|---|
> | **operating control** | **"are we achieving the plan?"** — annual, corrective |
> | **strategic control** | **"are our strategy and opportunities still well matched?"** — the marketing audit |
>
> **⚠️ The second is the one firms skip, and it is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s myopia again at the level of process.** **A firm can pass operating control every year — hitting every target in a plan that has stopped making sense — while strategic control quietly fails.**
>
> **The structural reason it gets skipped: operating control has a natural trigger (the annual cycle) and a clear owner, while the strategic audit has neither.** **Nothing goes red when a strategy becomes obsolete.**
>
> **(c) Because that is where the value the chapter allocates actually shows up.**
>
> **Kotler concedes marketing ROI is hard to measure** — marketing's returns are often delayed and jointly produced with other functions — **and points instead at customer-centred measures: acquisition, retention, and [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s customer lifetime value and customer equity.**
>
> **⚠️ Which closes the loop the two chapters make together.** **Ch. 01 said the point of marketing is to capture value and measured it as customer equity; ch. 02 allocates resources across businesses and asks whether the allocation paid.** **The answer has to be in ch. 01's units** — and [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]] §4 showed those units must be *discounted margins*, not revenue, if the answer is to mean anything.
