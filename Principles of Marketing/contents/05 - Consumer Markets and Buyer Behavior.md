# Consumer Markets and Buyer Behavior

**[[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|Ch. 04]] said how to find things out. This chapter says what there is to find out** — and it contains the single most checkable claim in the book so far.

**Three results.**

**§6 — ⚠️ Kotler's adopter categories ARE the normal distribution.** *(Verified: he gives both the percentages and the standard-deviation definitions and never checks that they agree — **all five match**, and the cumulative figures **2.5%, 16%, 50%, 84%** are exactly $\Phi(-2),\Phi(-1),\Phi(0),\Phi(+1)$.)* **⇒ the diffusion S-curve is literally a cumulative normal, which makes it a *forecasting tool* with two parameters.**

**§5 — ⚠️ his "promise only what your brands can deliver" is not the optimum either.** *(Modelled — and the result contradicted what I expected: **under-promising beat promising accurately**. His own "delighted" category requires a negative gap by construction.)*

**§3 — all three perceptual filters favour the incumbent.** **Attention screens out the new message, distortion bends it toward existing belief, retention keeps what already agreed.** **⇒ the psychology of perception is a barrier to entry.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. The model of buyer behavior — a black box

$$\text{marketing and other stimuli}\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\text{the buyer's BLACK BOX}}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{buyer responses}$$

> [!note] Kotler's framing is the honest one
> **Marketers can measure what goes *in* and what comes *out*; the middle "is not easy to figure out".** **The whole chapter is an attempt to open the box.**
>
> **And it has a [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]] consequence: observational research sees inputs and outputs only.** **Only survey and experimental work reaches the middle** — which is why buyer behaviour is studied by *asking*, with all the bias that implies.

### 2. The four factor groups

| group | elements | |
|---|---|---|
| **cultural** | culture, **subculture**, social class | **the most basic cause of wants and behaviour** — learned, not innate |
| **social** | groups and social networks, **family**, roles and status | reference groups, opinion leaders, word of mouth |
| **personal** | age and life stage, occupation, economic situation, **lifestyle**, personality | lifestyle = the **AIO** profile (activities, interests, opinions) |
| **psychological** | **motivation, perception, learning**, beliefs and attitudes | the mechanisms inside the box |

> [!note] ⚠️ The ordering is a hierarchy of scope, not a list
> **Culture shapes social groups, which shape personal circumstances, which condition psychological response** — **and it runs from *least* to *most* malleable.** **[[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|Ch. 03]]'s core-versus-secondary beliefs distinction, generalised.**

**Four definitions worth getting exactly right:**

- **Subculture** — a group with **shared value systems based on common life experiences**. **⚠️ Not a demographic slice: it is defined by shared *values*.**
- **Social class** — **"not determined by a single factor, such as income, but measured as a combination of occupation, income, education, wealth and other variables."** **⚠️ Income alone is not social class.**
- **Reference group** — a point of comparison in forming attitudes, **including *aspirational* groups the person does not belong to**.
- **Family** — **"the most important consumer buying organization in society."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ The family is where the commercial payoff is
> **It is [[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|ch. 03]]'s finding again: the *buyer*, the *user* and the *influencer* can be three different people.** **There, Gen Z influenced \$13.60 for every \$1 spent; here Kotler supplies the mechanism.**

### 3. The psychological mechanisms — and each has a trap

**Motivation.** **A need becomes a *motive* when raised to a sufficient level of intensity.** **Maslow's hierarchy: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualization, each dominant until satisfied.**

> [!note] The marketing use is the *ordering*, not the list
> **A product sold on esteem fails to a buyer whose safety needs are unmet.**

**Perception — the one with real operational bite. Three filters:**

| filter | what it does |
|---|---|
| **selective attention** | screening out most of what one is exposed to |
| **selective distortion** | interpreting information **to fit existing beliefs** |
| **selective retention** | remembering good points about brands one **already likes**, forgetting those about rivals |

> [!warning] ⚠️ All three favour the incumbent
> **Attention filters out the new message; distortion bends it toward what is already believed; retention keeps what already agreed.**
>
> **⇒ the psychology of perception is a *barrier to entry*** — which is what [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s brand equity is worth paying for, and why a challenger needs disproportionate spend to achieve parity of awareness.

**Learning** — drives, stimuli, cues, responses, reinforcement.

**Beliefs and attitudes** — **attitudes are hard to change**, and **Kotler's advice is to *fit* the product to existing attitudes rather than try to change them.** **⚠️ [[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|Ch. 03]]'s core-versus-secondary beliefs at the individual level.**

### 4. The five-stage buyer decision process

| | stage | |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **need recognition** | internal or external stimuli |
| 2 | **information search** | personal, commercial, public, experiential sources |
| 3 | **evaluation of alternatives** | using information to rank the choice set |
| 4 | **purchase decision** | which brand — *but see below* |
| 5 | **postpurchase behavior** | satisfaction, dissatisfaction, dissonance |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Two warnings Kotler gives and students skip
> **(i) Consumers may skip or reverse stages.** **The model describes *considered* purchases, not habitual ones.**
>
> **(ii) Purchase *intention* is not purchase.** **"Unexpected events may change the purchase intention" — the economy turns, a competitor cuts price, a friend reports disappointment.** **"Preferences and even purchase intentions do not always result in an actual purchase choice."**
>
> **⚠️ (ii) is a measurement warning for [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]]: survey research measures *intentions*, and intentions are a biased predictor of behaviour.** **It is also why causal work matters — an experiment measures the purchase, a survey measures the intention.**

> [!note] ⚠️ The information-source asymmetry
> **Commercial sources** *(advertising, salespeople)* **INFORM the buyer. Personal sources** *(family, friends)* **LEGITIMIZE or EVALUATE.**
>
> **⇒ the most effective source is the one the marketer controls least** — which is [[12 - Integrated Marketing Communications and Digital Marketing|ch. 12]]'s word-of-mouth problem stated at the individual level.

### 5. ⚠️ Satisfaction is a gap — and Kotler's advice is not the optimum

**His model: satisfaction depends on the relationship between the consumer's *expectations* and the product's *perceived performance*.**

| | |
|---|---|
| performance **<** expectations | **disappointed** |
| performance **=** expectations | satisfied |
| performance **>** expectations | **delighted** |

**"The larger the negative gap… the greater the dissatisfaction. This suggests that sellers should *promise only what their brands can deliver*."**

> [!warning] ⚠️ But expectations drive the purchase decision too — promise less and fewer people buy
> *(Modelled with performance fixed at 100: a higher promise raises trial and lowers retention, valued through [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s CLV formula. **Parameters are illustrative and mine; the shape is the result.**)*
>
> | promise | trial | gap | retention | CLV | **value per 1,000 prospects** |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | **80** | 0.14 | **+20** | 0.94 | \$335.71 | **\$47,000** |
> | 90 | 0.18 | +10 | 0.82 | \$157.69 | \$28,385 |
> | **100** | 0.22 | **0** | 0.70 | \$92.11 | \$20,263 |
> | 120 | 0.30 | −20 | 0.46 | \$37.10 | \$11,129 |
>
> **⚠️ The result is not what I expected, and it is better.** **The optimum is *not* at gap = 0 — under-promising beats promising accurately.**
>
> **⇒ Kotler's "promise only what your brands can deliver" is a compromise between over-promising (which he warns against) and under-promising (which he does not consider).** **The business maxim *under-promise and over-deliver* is what this arithmetic produces — and his own "delighted" category is the mechanism, since performance *above* expectations requires a negative gap by construction.**
>
> **⚠️ Where the true optimum sits is empirical, not theoretical.** **It depends on how steeply trial responds to the promise versus how steeply retention responds to the gap.** **These parameters make retention dominate; a category with weak retention effects — one-off purchases, no repeat — flips the answer toward over-promising, which is exactly where over-promising is observed in practice.**

**Cognitive dissonance**: **"almost all major purchases result in cognitive dissonance"** — discomfort from post-purchase conflict. **The marketer's job continues *after* the sale**, which is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s relationship view.

### 6. ⚠️ The adopter categories are the normal distribution

**Kotler gives *both* the percentages *and* the sigma definitions:**

> **innovators 2.5% — "those beyond **two standard deviations** from mean adoption time"; early adopters 13.5% — "**between one and two standard deviations**"; early mainstream 34%, late mainstream 34%, lagging adopters 16%.**

**⚠️ He never says this is a normal distribution, and never checks that the two sets of numbers agree. They do.**

| category | region | **normal** | Kotler | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| innovators | $<-2\sigma$ | **2.28%** | 2.5% | ✓ |
| early adopters | $-2\sigma$ to $-1\sigma$ | **13.59%** | 13.5% | ✓ |
| early mainstream | $-1\sigma$ to $0$ | **34.13%** | 34.0% | ✓ |
| late mainstream | $0$ to $+1\sigma$ | **34.13%** | 34.0% | ✓ |
| lagging adopters | $>+1\sigma$ | **15.87%** | 16.0% | ✓ |
| **TOTAL** | | **100.00%** | **100.0%** | ✓ |

**All five match and they sum to exactly 100%.** *(The only gap is innovators: the normal gives 2.28% and Kotler rounds to 2.5%.)*

> [!warning] ⚠️ So the adopter categories are not a taxonomy — they are QUANTILES
> **Cut at $-2\sigma$, $-1\sigma$, the mean and $+1\sigma$ of a normal distribution of adoption *times*.**
>
> **And the cumulative figures are the normal CDF itself:**
>
> | after | cumulative | |
> |---|---|---|
> | innovators | **2.5%** | $=\Phi(-2)$ |
> | early adopters | **16%** | $=\Phi(-1)$ |
> | early mainstream | **50%** | $=\Phi(0)$ |
> | late mainstream | **84%** | $=\Phi(+1)$ |
>
> **⚠️ The diffusion S-curve is literally a cumulative normal.** **Kotler draws the curve, states the sigmas, prints the percentages, and leaves the three facts unconnected.**

> [!note] ⚠️ The practical consequence, which no amount of prose gives you
> **The model has *two parameters* — a mean adoption time and a standard deviation — so observing two points on the curve pins the whole thing, including the final penetration date.** **That is a forecasting tool**, and it is why launch tracking measures *cumulative* trial.

> [!warning] ⚠️ And the honest caveat — normality is an assumption, not a finding
> **Real diffusion curves are often right-skewed** *(the Bass model is the standard alternative)*, **and Kotler's own counter-example is in the same section**: all-electric cars, launched in 2010, **"still account for far less than 1 percent" of US sales years later.** **A normal curve cannot produce that shape.**

### 7. The five characteristics that set the adoption rate

| characteristic | | effect |
|---|---|---|
| **relative advantage** | degree to which the innovation appears **superior** to existing products | **+** |
| **compatibility** | degree to which it fits the **values and experiences** of potential consumers | **+** |
| **complexity** | degree to which it is **difficult** to understand or use | **−** |
| **divisibility** | degree to which it may be **tried on a limited basis** | **+** |
| **communicability** | degree to which results can be **observed or described** to others | **+** |

> [!warning] ⚠️ Only one of the five is about the product being better
> **Four push adoption up and one (complexity) pushes it down** — so the marketer's job is to raise four and reduce one. **Relative advantage is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.**
>
> **Kotler's electric-car example runs all five, and the diagnosis is that relative advantage is *high* while divisibility (you cannot cheaply "try" an EV) and compatibility (charging does not fit existing habits) are *low*.** **⇒ a superior product with low trialability diffuses slowly — which is why free trials and test drives exist.**
>
> **⚠️ And divisibility is the cheapest lever on the list**, because samples, trials, freemium tiers and rentals change the *offer* rather than the *product*.

## ✏️ Exercises

**1. (The factors.)** (a) Name the four groups. (b) Define subculture and social class precisely. (c) Why does the ordering matter?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Cultural** (culture, subculture, social class), **social** (groups and networks, family, roles and status), **personal** (age and life stage, occupation, economic situation, lifestyle, personality), **psychological** (motivation, perception, learning, beliefs and attitudes).
>
> **(b) Both are defined by *combinations*, not single variables.**
>
> **A subculture is a group with *shared value systems based on common life experiences*.** **⚠️ It is not a demographic slice.** **Two people of the same age, income and ethnicity may be in different subcultures, and people of different demographics may share one** — which is why subcultural targeting is not the same as demographic targeting.
>
> **Social class is "not determined by a single factor, such as income, but measured as a combination of occupation, income, education, wealth and other variables."** **⚠️ Income alone is not social class**, and the distinction has commercial force: a high-income tradesperson and a low-income academic occupy different classes and buy differently, so a firm segmenting on income alone will mix them.
>
> *(This is [[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|ch. 03]]'s "which boundary?" problem again — the variable you segment on determines who you find.)*
>
> **(c) Because it is a hierarchy of scope running from least to most malleable.**
>
> **Culture shapes social groups, which shape personal circumstances, which condition psychological response.**
>
> **⚠️ And the malleability gradient is [[03 - Analyzing the Marketing Environment|ch. 03]]'s core-versus-secondary beliefs generalised**: a marketer can work on the psychological layer (framing, positioning, cues), can sometimes reach the social layer (reference groups, opinion leaders), and cannot move culture at all. **⇒ effort spent trying to shift the outer layers is wasted, which is the practical content of the whole taxonomy.**

**2. (Hard — perception.)** (a) Name the three filters. (b) Who do they favour? (c) What follows commercially?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Selective attention** (screening out most of what one is exposed to), **selective distortion** (interpreting information to fit existing beliefs), **selective retention** (remembering the good points about brands one already likes and forgetting those about rivals).
>
> **(b) ⚠️ All three favour the incumbent — and this is the chapter's most useful structural observation.**
>
> - **Attention** filters out the *new* message, because attention is drawn to the familiar and the relevant.
> - **Distortion** bends what does get through *toward what is already believed*.
> - **Retention** keeps what already agreed and discards what did not.
>
> **⇒ each filter, independently, protects the brand the consumer already uses.**
>
> **(c) Perception is a barrier to entry, and it prices brand equity.**
>
> **A challenger does not face a level playing field on communication: it must overcome three filters that the incumbent benefits from.** **⇒ achieving parity of awareness costs a challenger *more* than maintaining it costs the incumbent** — which is exactly what makes an established brand an asset rather than a name.
>
> **⚠️ This is what [[08 - Products, Services and Brands|ch. 08]]'s brand equity is worth paying for**, and it explains a fact that otherwise looks irrational: firms with dominant awareness continue to advertise heavily. **They are maintaining the filter, not building awareness.**
>
> *(And it connects to §3's advice on attitudes: since attitudes are hard to change, Kotler says **fit the product to existing attitudes** rather than trying to change them. **The same logic — work with the filter, not against it.**)*

**3. (The decision process.)** (a) Give the five stages. (b) What are the two warnings? (c) What is the source asymmetry?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a)** **Need recognition → information search → evaluation of alternatives → purchase decision → postpurchase behavior.**
>
> **(b) Stages can be skipped, and intention is not purchase.**
>
> **(i) The model describes *considered* purchases.** **Habitual and low-involvement purchases skip or reverse stages** — nobody runs five stages to buy salt. **⚠️ Applying the model where it does not fit produces elaborate research on a decision nobody makes.**
>
> **(ii) ⚠️ "Preferences and even purchase intentions do not always result in an actual purchase choice."** **Unexpected events intervene: the economy turns, a competitor cuts price, a friend reports disappointment.**
>
> **The second is a measurement warning for [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]].** **Survey research measures *intentions*; intentions are a biased predictor of behaviour** — and the bias is systematic, so [[04 - Managing Marketing Information and Research|ch. 04]] §4's point applies: **a bigger survey measures the same wrong thing more precisely.** **⇒ this is a direct argument for causal (experimental) research, which measures the purchase itself.**
>
> **(c) Commercial sources inform; personal sources legitimize.**
>
> **⚠️ The most effective source is the one the marketer controls least.** **Advertising and salespeople can deliver information the buyer did not have; only family and friends can make it *credible*.**
>
> **⇒ two consequences.** **First, communication budgets buy *reach* but not *persuasion*, so a campaign that fails to generate conversation has bought only half the job.** **Second, this is [[12 - Integrated Marketing Communications and Digital Marketing|ch. 12]]'s word-of-mouth problem in miniature** — and it explains why firms invest in influencer and referral programmes, which are attempts to buy access to the uncontrolled channel.

**4. (Hard — satisfaction.)** (a) Give Kotler's model. (b) Evaluate his advice. (c) What decides the answer?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Satisfaction is a *gap*.**
>
> **Performance below expectations → disappointed; equal → satisfied; above → delighted.** **"The larger the negative gap… the greater the dissatisfaction."** **His conclusion: "sellers should promise only what their brands can deliver."**
>
> **(b) ⚠️ The advice is incomplete, because expectations drive purchase too.**
>
> **Promise less and fewer people buy.** **So the promise level trades *acquisition* against *retention*, and Kotler's chapter only looks at one side.**
>
> *(Modelled with performance fixed at 100 and valued through [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s CLV formula:)*
>
> | promise | trial | gap | retention | **value / 1,000 prospects** |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **80** | 0.14 | +20 | 0.94 | **\$47,000** |
> | 100 | 0.22 | 0 | 0.70 | \$20,263 |
> | 120 | 0.30 | −20 | 0.46 | \$11,129 |
>
> **⚠️ The result contradicted what I expected.** **I anticipated an interior optimum; the arithmetic gives a corner — *under*-promising beats promising accurately.**
>
> **⇒ so "promise only what you can deliver" is not the optimum either. It is a compromise between over-promising, which Kotler warns against, and under-promising, which he does not consider.** **The maxim *under-promise and over-deliver* is what this produces — and his own "delighted" category is the mechanism, since performance above expectations requires a negative gap by construction.**
>
> **(c) It is empirical, and the deciding quantity is [[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|ch. 01]]'s CLV.**
>
> **The optimum depends on how steeply trial responds to the promise versus how steeply retention responds to the gap.** **These parameters make retention dominate.** **A category with weak retention effects — one-off purchases, no repeat business — flips the answer toward over-promising, which is exactly where over-promising is observed in practice** *(timeshares, tourist-trap restaurants, some infomercial products)*.
>
> **⚠️ And there is a systematic bias worth flagging.** **[[01 - Marketing, Customer Value and Engagement|Ch. 01]] §4 showed most firms overstate CLV by a factor of fourteen by using undiscounted revenue.** **A firm doing that over-values the trial and under-values the churn** — **so it will systematically over-promise.** **The CLV error propagates directly into the promise decision.**
>
> *(Parameters here are illustrative and mine; the *shape* of the trade-off is the result, and I have reported it as it came out rather than tuning it to match my expectation.)*

**5. (Hard — adoption.)** (a) Verify the adopter percentages. (b) What does that make the S-curve? (c) Give the five characteristics and diagnose electric cars.

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) They are the normal distribution, and all five match.**
>
> **Kotler gives both the percentages and the sigma definitions** — "beyond two standard deviations", "between one and two standard deviations" — **and never checks that they agree.**
>
> | category | region | normal | Kotler |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | innovators | $<-2\sigma$ | 2.28% | 2.5% |
> | early adopters | $-2\sigma$ to $-1\sigma$ | 13.59% | 13.5% |
> | early mainstream | $-1\sigma$ to $0$ | 34.13% | 34.0% |
> | late mainstream | $0$ to $+1\sigma$ | 34.13% | 34.0% |

>
> **They sum to exactly 100%, and the only discrepancy is innovators, where he rounds 2.28% to 2.5%.**
>
> **(b) ⚠️ Literally a cumulative normal.**
>
> **The cumulative percentages are 2.5%, 16%, 50%, 84%, 100% — which are $\Phi(-2)$, $\Phi(-1)$, $\Phi(0)$, $\Phi(+1)$.**
>
> **⇒ the adopter categories are *quantiles*, not a taxonomy, and the famous S-curve is not a stylised fact — it is the normal CDF.**
>
> **⚠️ And that makes it a forecasting tool.** **The model has two parameters — a mean adoption time and a standard deviation — so *observing two points on the curve pins the whole thing*, including the date of final penetration.** **This is why launch tracking measures cumulative trial rather than period sales.**
>
> **The honest caveat: normality is an assumption, not a finding.** **Real diffusion is often right-skewed (the Bass model is the standard alternative), and Kotler's own counter-example sits in the same section — electric cars, launched 2010, "still account for far less than 1 percent" years later.** **No normal curve produces that.**
>
> **(c) Relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, divisibility, communicability.**
>
> **⚠️ Four raise the adoption rate and one (complexity) lowers it — and only *one* of the five is about the product being better.**
>
> **Electric cars: relative advantage is high (no petrol, cleaner, cheaper energy), but *divisibility* is low (you cannot cheaply try one) and *compatibility* is low (charging does not fit existing habits and infrastructure).**
>
> **⇒ a superior product with low trialability diffuses slowly**, which is the whole explanation of Kotler's own puzzle about why EVs took so long despite their advantages.
>
> **⚠️ And divisibility is the cheapest lever available**, because samples, trials, freemium tiers, rentals and test drives change the *offer* rather than the *product*. **A firm that cannot improve relative advantage can nearly always improve trialability** — which is why free trials are ubiquitous and why extended test drives were the industry's actual response.
