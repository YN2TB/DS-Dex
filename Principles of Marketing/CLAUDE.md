## ⚠️ The five findings worth keeping

1. **THE KNIFE-EDGE (ch. 10, the sharpest single result of the subject).** Appendix 2 ends demanding **20,000,000 units / \$3.36bn** for a 25% return on sales and asks *"could it possibly achieve this?"* without answering. **The denominator $0.75(168)-125$ is EXACTLY \$1.00 — 0.80% above a vertical asymptote at \$166.67 — and at his own markup price of \$193.33 the required volume is EXACTLY 1,000,000 units, the figure that price was defined from.** ⇒ **a 13.10% price cut multiplies required volume by TWENTY.** ***RULE: when a break-even returns an absurd number, INSPECT THE DENOMINATOR before blaming the market.***
2. **THE CROSS-SUBJECT RESULT (ch. 07, cashed in ch. 10).** Kotler's Hearts On Fire *"15 to 20 percent price premium over comparable competing diamonds"* ⇒ Lerner ⇒ **an implied firm elasticity of 6.71 against INFINITE under perfect competition.** ***A sustainable premium over a comparable product IS a downward-sloping firm demand curve*** — his book is a manual for entering monopolistic competition, and neither source connects them. **Cashed in ch. 10: Table 10.1 solves MR=MC by enumeration (Lerner e=2.25, bracketed by his own arcs) and never names the condition; and his 10% price cut needs an elasticity of 9.09 against a justification that only means >1.**
3. **GENERALISE THE TOY EXAMPLE (ch. 11's contacts figure — the most reusable move here).** 9 direct vs 6 intermediated ⇒ **saving = (M−1)(C−1) − 1**, ZERO at 2×2, 33.3% in his own case, **98.90% at 100×1,000.** ***An intermediary is worth the PRODUCT of the two fragmentations*** — the theory of disintermediation in one line.
4. **TAKE SCATTERED FIGURES AND DIVIDE THEM.** The habit that produced almost everything else: **ch. 08's package gets 0.20 seconds** (300 items/min) and **a 98% standard is an ERROR COUNT that compounds to 83.37% over nine stages**; **ch. 09's marketing strategy statement SOLVES** (\$1,125 contribution, both years exact, break-even 63,333 vs a plan of 50,000, a 20% miss making the loss 75% worse); **ch. 11's Costco earns 1.80× Sam's Club on a 0.4-point LOWER margin**; **ch. 12's Super Bowl CPM (\$45.05) sits BELOW a space banner (\$50)**, and **4/7 = 57.14% means the buyer self-serves the first four selling steps.**
5. **⚠️ THE SUBJECT'S UNIFYING LESSON, true in every one of the twelve chapters: A HEADLINE NUMBER WAS ACCURATE AND MEANT SOMETHING OTHER THAN WHAT IT APPEARED TO MEAN.** ch. 01's \$50,000 CLV was undiscounted *revenue* (\$3,571 after discounting, margin and retention — **a factor of 14**); ch. 02's share quoted without a growth rate; ch. 08's 98%; ch. 09's launch that hit every volume target and lost \$50,000; ch. 10's cost-plus price 33% below optimal; ch. 11's higher margin that earned less; ch. 12's media value at 25–38× a bought impression. ***The habit that catches all of them is the same: divide the source's own figures by each other.***

**⚠️ CH. 11's HEADLINE — the generalise-the-toy-example template, and the best instance of it in the subject.** Kotler's Figure 12.1 draws **9 contacts direct vs 6 through a distributor** and stops. **Generalised: direct = M×C, intermediated = M+C, so the SAVING = (M−1)(C−1) − 1.** ⇒ **ZERO at 2×2; his own 3×3 saves 33.3% and is the SMALLEST case with any saving at all; at 100 producers × 1,000 customers it saves 98.90%, a factor of 90.9.** ***AN INTERMEDIARY IS WORTH THE PRODUCT OF THE TWO FRAGMENTATIONS AND NOTHING WHEN EITHER SIDE CONCENTRATES*** — which is the theory of disintermediation, and it explains his own direct-selling examples (Mary Kay, Amway, GEICO: M=1, so there is nothing to save) that he lists without a common cause.

**What ch. 06 established:**
- **⚠️ KOTLER LISTS THREE PROPERTIES OF BUSINESS DEMAND — DERIVED, INELASTIC, MORE FLUCTUATING — AS THREE PARALLEL FACTS. THE LAST TWO FOLLOW FROM THE FIRST.** *(He gives no arithmetic for any of them.)*
  - **DERIVED ⇒ INELASTIC:** *derived elasticity ≈ cost share × final elasticity.* **Computed from his own leather example: a 30% price collapse raises leather demand 9% — elasticity 0.30 — even though final shoe demand is ELASTIC at 1.5.** ⇒ **cutting your price buys almost no volume**, so a component supplier's growth must come from the FINAL market — **which is why his Corning and Gore-Tex illustrations are CONSUMER campaigns run by INGREDIENT suppliers. That is the only lever the elasticity leaves.**
  - **DERIVED ⇒ FLUCTUATING (the accelerator):** **a 10% rise in consumer demand DOUBLES machine orders; a 10% fall ELIMINATES them.** **Amplification = ASSET LIFE** (×4 for 4-year assets, ×20 for 20-year) ⇒ **capital goods are the most cyclical markets in any economy, and it has nothing to do with buyers being fickle.** **Asymmetric: orders can't go below zero ⇒ boom-bust, and recovery waits until demand passes its old peak.**
- **⚠️ THE BUY CLASS IS CONTESTED, and Kotler never says so.** Incumbents want every purchase a **straight rebuy**; challengers must convert one into a **modified rebuy**. **Systems selling AND e-procurement both push toward straight rebuy ⇒ both advantage incumbents** (the workflow mirror of ch. 05's perceptual filters).
- **The B2B selling problem is IDENTIFICATION, not persuasion** — the buying centre is not fixed (1 to 30 people), not visible ("even the people in it are not aware of all the participants"), and not unanimous. **Gatekeepers have no consumer analogue: they decide who gets to SPEAK.**
- **Stage 3 (product specification) is the highest-leverage moment and precedes formal contact; stage 8 (performance review) feeds the next cycle's buy class** ⇒ ch. 01's CLV with a measurement process attached.

## ⚠️ This subject needs a different note shape — adapt, don't force

The vault template (root `../CLAUDE.md`) assumes formulas and computable exercises. **Kotler has almost none.** Adapt rather than padding:

- **`📘 Main Knowledge`** — frameworks, not derivations. Name each framework precisely (4Ps, STP, the five-stage buyer decision process, BCG matrix, Porter's generic strategies) and state what each *distinguishes*, since exams test definitional boundaries.
- **`✏️ Exercises`** — still five, easy → hard, but as **applied cases and classification problems** ("here is a firm and a situation — which positioning strategy, and why not the other three?") with full model answers. Where the chapter *does* support arithmetic (**break-even and markup pricing, customer lifetime value, market-share arithmetic, advertising elasticity, marketing ROI**), use it and **verify the arithmetic before writing**, exactly as elsewhere.
- **`⚠️ Important Notes`** — the highest-value section here. Marketing exams punish confusing adjacent terms (segmentation vs targeting vs positioning; needs vs wants vs demands; marketing concept vs selling concept; sales promotion vs advertising). Make it a disambiguation section.
