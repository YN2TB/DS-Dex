# Unemployment

**[[09 - Production and Growth|Chapter 09]] explained long-run living standards and [[10 - Saving, Investment and the Financial System|ch. 10]] the market that funds them. This chapter is about the resource that is *not* being used.**

**Two results.**

**§2 — the headline unemployment rate is a classification choice.** *(Computed: reclassifying 1.8 million discouraged workers raises the rate from **4.93% to 6.00%** — a swing of over a percentage point **with not one person's situation changed**.)*

**§5 — the minimum wage's employment effect is one unknown elasticity.** *(Computed: the same 20% wage floor destroys anywhere from **1% to 30%** of employment, and **the only thing that changes between those rows is the demand elasticity**.)* **That is why the empirical literature is contested and the theory is not.**

**And it is the third time this shape has appeared** — after [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice|ch. 07]]'s labour supply and [[10 - Saving, Investment and the Financial System|ch. 10]]'s crowding out. **The theory is complete; the answer is empirical.**

## 📘 Main Knowledge

### 1. Measurement

**The BLS sorts the adult population into three categories:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **employed** | worked for pay in the past week |
| **unemployed** | not working, **available for work**, and **actively looking in the last four weeks** *(or awaiting recall)* |
| **not in the labour force** | everyone else — students, homemakers, retirees |

$$\text{labour force}=E+U\qquad \text{unemployment rate}=\frac{U}{E+U}\times100\qquad \text{participation rate}=\frac{E+U}{\text{adult pop.}}\times100$$

*(Verified — Mankiw's January 2016 figures: employed **150.5 mn**, unemployed **7.8 mn**, not in the labour force **94.1 mn**.)*

| | | book |
|---|---|---|
| labour force | $150.5+7.8=\mathbf{158.3}$ mn | 158.3 ✓ |
| adult population | $158.3+94.1=\mathbf{252.4}$ mn | 252.4 ✓ |
| **unemployment rate** | $7.8/158.3=\mathbf{4.93\%}$ | |
| **participation rate** | $158.3/252.4=\mathbf{62.72\%}$ | |

> [!note] The middle definition does all the work
> **"Unemployed" requires *actively looking*.** Someone who wants a job and has stopped searching is **not** unemployed — they are out of the labour force. **§2 is what follows from that.**

### 2. ⚠️ The headline rate is a classification choice

*(Computed — reclassifying discouraged workers as unemployed:)*

| discouraged workers (mn) | reclassified rate | headline | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 4.9274% | 4.9274% | — |
| 1.0 | 5.5242% | 4.9274% | +0.60 |
| **1.8** | **5.9963%** | **4.9274%** | **+1.07** |
| 3.0 | 6.6956% | 4.9274% | +1.77 |

> [!warning] Over a percentage point, with nobody's situation changed
> **A **discouraged worker** wants a job but has stopped looking, so is counted out of the labour force.** That is a definitional decision, and it moves the headline number by more than the difference between a good year and a bad one.
>
> **Mankiw is careful that it cuts both ways:**
> - **the rate *overstates*** if some "unemployed" are not genuinely searching — a job search maintained to keep benefits, or unreported work;
> - **the rate *understates*** if discouraged workers are excluded, and if part-time workers who want full-time work are counted as employed.
>
> **⚠️ So the participation rate must be read alongside the unemployment rate.** **A falling unemployment rate together with a falling participation rate may be people giving up rather than people finding work** — and the two series routinely move that way in a recovery's early stages.
>
> *(This is [[08 - Measuring the Macroeconomy - GDP and the Cost of Living|ch. 08]]'s caution again: a number that looks objective rests on choices about what to count. The BLS publishes several alternative measures precisely because no single one is right.)*

> [!note] Most spells are short; most observed unemployment comes from long spells
> **Both are true and they answer different questions.** *(Illustrated: four workers unemployed one week each and one unemployed 52 weeks — **80% of spells are short**, but the long spell is **93% of the worker-weeks**.)*
>
> **So "most unemployment is brief" and "most of the unemployment problem is long-term" are both correct**, which is why policy aimed at the long-term unemployed can be right even when average duration is short.

### 3. The natural rate

| | |
|---|---|
| **natural rate** | the unemployment that **persists in the long run** — what the economy gravitates to |
| **cyclical** | the year-to-year **deviation** from it *(that is [[14 - Short-Run Fluctuations - AD-AS, Policy and the Phillips Curve|ch. 14]]'s subject)* |

**The natural rate has two sources:**

- **frictional** — it takes **time** to match workers to jobs *(§4)*;
- **structural** — the wage is held **above** the market-clearing level, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded *(§§5–6)*.

> [!note] Only frictional unemployment is arguably efficient
> **Search takes time because workers and jobs genuinely differ**, and a match found quickly may be a bad match. **Structural unemployment is a different animal: it is people who would work at the going wage and cannot.**

### 4. Frictional unemployment and job search

**Job search exists because workers and jobs are heterogeneous.** Even with as many vacancies as job-seekers, matching takes time.

**Sectoral shifts** — changes in the composition of demand across industries or regions — create frictional unemployment continually.

> [!note] An economy with no sectoral shifts would be an economy with no innovation
> **[[02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|Ch. 02]] showed technological progress in an inelastic sector shrinking that sector** — US farm employment falling from ~40% to under 2% *because* farming improved. **Every one of those transitions was frictional unemployment**, and it is the price of the productivity growth [[09 - Production and Growth|ch. 09]] identified as the source of living standards.

**Policy:** job boards and training programmes speed matching; **unemployment insurance** protects income **and reduces the incentive to search.** *(Mankiw cites experiments in which offering a **bonus for finding work quickly** shortened unemployment spells.)*

> [!warning] UI is a genuine trade-off, not a mistake
> **It makes people better off — it insures a real risk that individuals cannot insure privately — *and* it raises measured unemployment.** Both are true. **The design question is the replacement rate and duration, not whether to have it**, and treating either effect as the whole story gets the policy wrong.

### 5. ⚠️ The minimum wage: the answer is one elasticity

**[[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|Ch. 03]] treated this as a price floor. Now the labour market [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice|ch. 07]] built can be put inside it.**

*(Computed — equilibrium wage \$10 and employment 100; a minimum wage of \$12, i.e. 20% above:)*

| demand elasticity $\eta_d$ | **jobs lost** | **% of employment** | unemployment created |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 1.00 | **1.00%** | 7.00 |
| 0.10 | 2.00 | **2.00%** | 8.00 |
| 0.30 | 6.00 | **6.00%** | 12.00 |
| 0.60 | 12.00 | **12.00%** | 18.00 |
| 1.00 | 20.00 | **20.00%** | 26.00 |
| 1.50 | 30.00 | **30.00%** | 36.00 |

$$\text{jobs lost}=\eta_d\times L^*\times\frac{W_{\min}-W^*}{W^*}$$

> [!warning] The answer spans 1% to 30%, and only the elasticity changes
> **Job losses are proportional to the demand elasticity and to the size of the increase. Nothing else enters.**
>
> **So the theory is not in dispute at all.** The *sign* is agreed — a binding wage floor reduces employment. **The magnitude is an unknown parameter, and the policy question is entirely about the magnitude.** That is precisely why the empirical literature is contested while the theory is not.
>
> **⚠️ And note who is affected.** A minimum wage binds **only where it is above the equilibrium wage** — so it affects the least-skilled workers, teenagers and specific regions, **not the labour market as a whole.** *(Which is why aggregate studies can miss the effect entirely, and why the empirical debate is largely about which comparison group is right.)*
>
> **The unemployment created exceeds the jobs lost**, because a higher wage also draws *new* entrants into the labour force — the surplus is $(\eta_d+\eta_s)$-driven while job losses are $\eta_d$-driven alone.

> [!warning] ⚠️ Third appearance of one shape
> | chapter | question | what the answer depends on |
> |---|---|---|
> | [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice\|07]] | do higher wages raise hours worked? | substitution vs income — **ambiguous in sign** |
> | [[10 - Saving, Investment and the Financial System\|10]] | how much does a deficit crowd out? | **100% or 33%** — the saving slope |
> | **11** | how many jobs does a minimum wage cost? | **1% or 30%** — the demand elasticity |
>
> **In each case the theory is complete and the answer is empirical.** **Quoting a point estimate as though theory delivered it is the error** — and it is a very common one, in both political directions.

### 6. Unions and efficiency wages

**Unions** raise the wage for members above the competitive level — **the same structural mechanism as §5.** Fewer jobs in the unionised sector, and displaced workers move to the non-union sector, **pushing wages down there.**

> [!note] So unions raise insiders' wages partly at outsiders' expense
> **Mankiw gives the other side fairly**: unions may offset an employer's market power (a **monopsony**), and may lower turnover and raise morale. **The net effect is contested** — but the insider/outsider structure is not.

**Efficiency wages** — firms may *choose* to pay above market, because a higher wage:

1. improves **worker health** *(matters most in poor countries)*;
2. lowers **turnover**, and so hiring and training costs;
3. raises the **quality of applicants**;
4. raises **effort**, because being fired now costs the worker something.

> [!warning] ⚠️ Reasons 3 and 4 are asymmetric-information results
> **Reason 3 is adverse selection** — the wage changes *who applies*. **Reason 4 is moral hazard** — the wage changes *how they behave once hired.*
>
> **That is exactly the material [[00-Index]] records as excluded from Micro ch. 22 because [[Commercial Banking/contents/11 - Lending - Policy, Credit Risk and Business Loans|Commercial Banking ch. 11]] already owns it** — and the structure is identical. **There, a lender charging a higher rate changes *which* borrowers accept and *how* they behave, producing a humped return curve where charging more earns less.** Here an employer paying more changes which workers apply and how hard they work, **so paying above market can be profitable.**
>
> **Same mechanism, opposite direction, two subjects.** *(A lender's price is a cost to the counterparty and screens badly upward; an employer's price is a benefit and screens well upward.)*
>
> **And the macro consequence is the important one: efficiency wages mean the wage does *not* fall to clear the market.** **Unemployment persists as an equilibrium rather than as a disequilibrium waiting to correct** — which is what makes it a candidate explanation for a *natural* rate rather than a temporary one.

### 7. What the four causes share

> [!note] Four causes, really two
> **Minimum wages, unions and efficiency wages are one mechanism: the wage is above the market-clearing level, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, and the gap is unemployment.**
>
> **Job search is different — it is unemployment *with* the wage at equilibrium**, caused by the time matching takes.
>
> **They call for different policy:**
> - **frictional** → improve matching: information, training, mobility;
> - **structural** → a wage floor is doing it, and removing the floor has the distributional consequences [[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|ch. 03]] already analysed.
>
> **[[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|Ch. 03]]'s price-floor diagram was the whole of the structural analysis, drawn before the labour market was available to put in it.**

**1. (Measurement.)** (a) Compute the rates. (b) Why is the headline rate a choice? (c) Reconcile "most spells are short" with "most unemployment is long-term".

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Verified against Mankiw's January 2016 figures.**
>
> *(Computed: labour force $150.5+7.8=\mathbf{158.3}$ mn ✓; adult population $158.3+94.1=\mathbf{252.4}$ mn ✓; unemployment rate **4.93%**; participation rate **62.72%**.)*
>
> **The three categories turn on one definition: "unemployed" requires being available *and* actively looking in the past four weeks.**
>
> **(b) Because discouraged workers are excluded by that definition.**
>
> *(Computed: reclassifying 1.8 mn discouraged workers raises the rate from **4.93% to 6.00%** — over a percentage point, **with nobody's actual situation different.**)*
>
> **And the bias runs both ways.** The rate **overstates** if some "searchers" are not genuinely searching; it **understates** if discouraged workers and involuntary part-timers are excluded. **Which dominates is not knowable from the headline figure.**
>
> **⚠️ The practical rule: read the participation rate alongside it.** **A falling unemployment rate with falling participation may be people leaving the labour force, not finding jobs** — a distinction that matters enormously for whether a recovery is real.
>
> *(This is [[08 - Measuring the Macroeconomy - GDP and the Cost of Living|ch. 08]]'s caution recurring: an objective-looking number rests on choices about what to count. The BLS publishes several alternative measures for exactly this reason.)*
>
> **(c) They measure different things — spells versus worker-weeks.**
>
> *(Illustrated: four one-week spells and one 52-week spell. **80% of spells are short**, but the single long spell accounts for **93% of total unemployment experienced**.)*
>
> **Most people who become unemployed find work quickly. Most unemployment observed at any moment belongs to people who have been unemployed a long time.**
>
> **Both statements are true**, and they support different policies: **the first says the labour market works; the second says a small group bears almost all the cost.** *(Which is why long-term-unemployment programmes can be justified even when the average spell is short.)*

**2. (Hard — the minimum wage.)** (a) Compute the employment effect. (b) Why is the empirical literature contested when the theory is not? (c) What else does this pattern apply to?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) It depends entirely on the demand elasticity.**
>
> *(Computed — a 20% wage floor on a market with equilibrium wage \$10 and employment 100:)*
>
> | $\eta_d$ | jobs lost |
> |---|---|
> | 0.05 | **1.00%** |
> | 0.30 | 6.00% |
> | 1.00 | 20.00% |
> | 1.50 | **30.00%** |
>
> $$\text{jobs lost}=\eta_d\times L^*\times\frac{W_{\min}-W^*}{W^*}$$
>
> **Proportional to the elasticity and to the size of the rise, and nothing else enters.**
>
> **⚠️ Note also that the *unemployment* created exceeds the *jobs lost*** — a higher wage draws new entrants into the labour force, so the surplus depends on $\eta_d+\eta_s$ while job losses depend on $\eta_d$ alone. **Those are different numbers and are often conflated.**
>
> **(b) Because the theory settles the sign and the policy question is the magnitude.**
>
> **Nobody disputes that a binding wage floor reduces the quantity of labour demanded.** *(That is [[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|ch. 03]]'s price floor, and [[02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|ch. 02]]'s downward-sloping demand curve.)* **The dispute is over a parameter, and the range spans "negligible" to "large".**
>
> **⚠️ Two further reasons the empirics are hard, both of which follow from the model:**
>
> 1. **A minimum wage binds only where it is above the equilibrium wage** — so it affects the least-skilled, teenagers, and particular regions, **not the labour market as a whole.** **Aggregate studies can miss the effect entirely**, and most of the empirical dispute is about which comparison group is the right one.
> 2. **The relevant elasticity is a *long-run* one** ([[02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|ch. 02]] §6: elasticity rises with the time horizon), **so a study measuring the effect over one year understates it** — firms substitute capital for labour, relocate, or close over longer periods.
>
> *(And the monopsony case in §6 is a genuine exception: if the employer has wage-setting power, a minimum wage can raise employment. That is not a fudge — it is a different market structure, and which one applies is again empirical.)*
>
> **(c) It is the third instance of one shape.**
>
> | chapter | question | unknown parameter |
> |---|---|---|
> | [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice\|07]] | higher wages → more hours worked? | **the sign itself** is ambiguous |
> | [[10 - Saving, Investment and the Financial System\|10]] | how much crowding out? | **100% or 33%** — the saving slope |
> | **11** | minimum-wage job losses? | **1% or 30%** — the demand elasticity |
>
> **In each case the theory is complete and the answer is empirical**, and in each case the model's job was to identify *which parameter to measure* — which is a real contribution, not a failure.
>
> **⚠️ The error to avoid is quoting a point estimate as though theory delivered it.** **It happens in both political directions**, and the honest form is always a range plus a statement of what the range depends on. *(This is [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice|ch. 07]]'s closing rule: a model that identifies two opposing forces, or an undetermined magnitude, has done its job.)*

**3. (The natural rate.)** (a) Distinguish frictional from structural. (b) Why do efficiency wages exist? (c) What do the four causes share?

> [!example]- Solution
> **(a) Frictional is matching time; structural is a wage above market-clearing.**
>
> | | cause | wage |
> |---|---|---|
> | **frictional** | matching takes **time** | **at** equilibrium |
> | **structural** | the wage is **held above** equilibrium | **above** equilibrium |
>
> **Frictional unemployment is arguably efficient** — workers and jobs genuinely differ, and a match found instantly may be a bad match. **Structural unemployment is people who would work at the going wage and cannot find work.**
>
> **And frictional unemployment is the price of growth.** **Sectoral shifts create it continually, and an economy with no sectoral shifts would be an economy with no innovation** — [[02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|ch. 02]] traced US farm employment from ~40% to under 2% *because* farming improved, and every one of those transitions was frictional unemployment.
>
> **Unemployment insurance is the honest trade-off here**: it insures a genuine risk **and** lengthens search. *(Mankiw cites experiments where a **bonus for finding work quickly** shortened spells — direct evidence the incentive effect is real.)* **The design question is the replacement rate and duration, not whether to have it.**
>
> **(b) Because the wage changes *who* applies and *how hard* they work.**
>
> **Four reasons: health, turnover, worker quality, and effort.**
>
> **⚠️ The last two are asymmetric-information results.** **Worker quality is adverse selection** — a higher wage changes the applicant pool. **Effort is moral hazard** — a higher wage makes dismissal costly, so it changes behaviour after hiring.
>
> **This is the structure [[Commercial Banking/contents/11 - Lending - Policy, Credit Risk and Business Loans|Commercial Banking ch. 11]] computed**, where a lender charging a higher rate changes which borrowers accept and how they behave, **producing a humped return curve on which charging more earns less.** **Same mechanism, opposite direction**: a lender's price is a *cost* to the counterparty and screens badly upward; an employer's price is a *benefit* and screens well upward.
>
> **⚠️ The macro consequence is what matters here: the wage does not fall to clear the market, because the firm does not *want* it to.** **So unemployment persists as an equilibrium rather than a disequilibrium waiting to correct** — which is what makes efficiency wages a candidate explanation for a *natural* rate rather than a temporary one.
>
> **(c) Three of the four are one mechanism.**
>
> **Minimum wages, unions and efficiency wages all put the wage above market-clearing**, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded and the gap is unemployment. **They differ only in *who* holds the wage up** — the government, a union, or the firm itself.
>
> **Job search is the odd one out**: unemployment *with* the wage at equilibrium.
>
> **So the policy split is clean:** **frictional → improve matching** (information, training, mobility); **structural → the wage floor is doing it**, and removing it has the distributional consequences [[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|ch. 03]] analysed — **the workers who keep their jobs gain and the ones who lose them bear the whole cost.**
>
> **[[03 - Welfare Analysis - Surplus, Price Controls, Taxes and Trade|Ch. 03]]'s price-floor diagram was the entire structural analysis, drawn before the labour market existed to put in it.**

## 📝 Summary

- **Three categories: employed, unemployed (available *and* actively looking), not in the labour force.** *(Verified against Mankiw's Jan-2016 figures: labour force **158.3 mn**, population **252.4 mn**, unemployment rate **4.93%**, participation **62.72%**.)*
- **⚠️ The headline rate is a classification choice** *(computed: reclassifying 1.8 mn discouraged workers gives **5.996% against 4.927%** — over a point, with nobody's situation changed)*.
- **It cuts both ways** — overstating if searchers are not genuinely searching, understating if discouraged workers and involuntary part-timers are excluded. **Read the participation rate alongside it.**
- **Most spells are short; most observed unemployment comes from long spells** *(80% of spells vs 93% of worker-weeks in the illustration)*. **Both true, different questions.**
- **Natural rate = frictional + structural; cyclical is the deviation** ([[14 - Short-Run Fluctuations - AD-AS, Policy and the Phillips Curve|ch. 14]]).
- **Frictional unemployment is the price of growth** — sectoral shifts create it, and an economy without them would have no innovation ([[02 - Supply, Demand and Elasticity|ch. 02]]'s farm-employment collapse).
- **Unemployment insurance insures a real risk *and* lengthens search.** Both. The question is design, not existence.
- **⚠️ The minimum wage's employment effect spans 1% to 30% of employment**, and **the only thing that changes is the demand elasticity** *(computed)*. $\text{jobs lost}=\eta_d L^*(W_{\min}-W^*)/W^*$.
- **The theory is not in dispute — the sign is agreed and the magnitude is unknown.** That is why the empirics are contested.
- **⚠️ A minimum wage binds only where it is above equilibrium** — the least-skilled, teenagers, particular regions — **so aggregate studies can miss it**; and the relevant elasticity is a **long-run** one, so short studies understate.
- **Unemployment created exceeds jobs lost** — a higher wage draws new entrants, so the surplus depends on $\eta_d+\eta_s$ and job losses on $\eta_d$ alone.
- **⚠️ Third appearance of one shape**: [[07 - Factor Markets and the Theory of Consumer Choice|ch. 07]] (labour supply — sign ambiguous), [[10 - Saving, Investment and the Financial System|ch. 10]] (crowding out — 100% or 33%), ch. 11 (job losses — 1% or 30%). **Theory complete, answer empirical. Quoting a point estimate as though theory delivered it is the error.**
- **Unions raise insiders' wages partly at outsiders' expense** — displaced workers push non-union wages down. *(Though monopsony and morale effects cut the other way.)*
- **⚠️ Efficiency wages: health, turnover, worker quality (adverse selection), effort (moral hazard).** **The last two are [[Commercial Banking/contents/11 - Lending - Policy, Credit Risk and Business Loans|Commercial Banking ch. 11]]'s structure with the sign reversed** — there, charging more earns less; here, paying more can earn more.
- **And efficiency wages make unemployment an *equilibrium***, because the firm does not want the wage to fall.
- **Three of the four causes are one mechanism** — the wage held above market-clearing, differing only in who holds it there. **Job search is the exception.**
